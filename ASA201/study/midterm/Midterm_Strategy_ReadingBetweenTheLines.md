## The Prompt

**ASA201 / Introduction to Asian American Studies / Midterm**

Choose a key term or concept from the list below and use it to discuss a "cultural text" of your choice. Examples of Asian American cultural texts include: music, poetry, new media, architecture, video games. The key is to argue clearly what the text shows its readers about that key term -- its complexities, its contradictions, its implications, its imaginings -- and how does it do so? What are the formal, aesthetic, or rhetorical strategies that the cultural text uses to forward that understanding of the key term? You must run your chosen cultural text by me prior to the midterm.

**Key Terms/Concepts:**

Grading will focus, in part, on originality of your analysis. You are encouraged to draw connections and "mix" a cultural text with a concept or key term.

Your paper should be thesis-driven (and your thesis is your interpretation of how the text engages that key term). Your total page count should be about 4-5 pages, handwritten, double-spaced.

Successful essays will be argumentative and analyze specific images or instances from the theoretical and cultural texts to support claims but does not require the use of direct quotations since you will be writing the essay from memory. ## The 6 Hidden Signals in the Prompt

### 1. "Originality of your analysis" -- This Is the #1 Grading Criterion

The professor literally tells you grading focuses "in part" on originality. This is the differentiator between an A and a B+. What this means:

- **Don't just apply theory to text like a formula.** ("Lowe says X, and the lunchbox scene shows X.") That's competent but not original.
- **Find something the theory DOESN'T fully explain**, or where the text **pushes back** on the theory, or where the text reveals a **contradiction or tension** the theorist didn't anticipate.
- Your professor has read dozens of papers that say "this show performs differential inclusion." What they haven't read is a paper that identifies a specific moment where the text *complicates* or *exceeds* a concept.

**What this means for your FOTB essay:** Your strongest move isn't just showing how the lunchbox scene performs differential inclusion (that's your foundation). Your A-level move is identifying a moment where the scene does something the theory doesn't fully account for -- like how the laugh track creates a double audience (white viewers laugh AT the difference, Asian American viewers laugh WITH recognition), meaning the same formal mechanism does two things at once. Or how Eddie Huang's collaborative antagonism reveals that differential inclusion isn't total -- it has seams and cracks.

---

### 2. "Formal, aesthetic, or rhetorical strategies" -- The Professor Wants You to Analyze HOW, Not Just WHAT

This phrase appears explicitly in the prompt and it's the most overlooked part. Most students will write: "The lunchbox scene SHOWS differential inclusion because Eddie is included but on limited terms."

The professor wants: "The lunchbox scene PRODUCES differential inclusion through specific formal strategies: the laugh track, the 22-minute resolution structure, the bright lighting that codes violence as wholesome, and the camera work that isolates Eddie's individual experience rather than connecting it to systemic patterns."

**Translation:** You need to name concrete formal/aesthetic/rhetorical devices and show how they do ideological work. Not just "the scene means X" but "the scene achieves X through these specific techniques."

Your cheat sheet does this well (laugh track as racial pedagogy, sitcom genre as hegemonic apparatus, 22-minute structure preventing sustained critique). Make sure at least 1-2 body paragraphs are dedicated to formal analysis, not just thematic summary.

---

### 3. "The bulk of your essay should be spent on your critical interpretation of the cultural text"

This is the biggest trap students fall into: **spending 60% of the essay explaining theory and 40% on the text.** The professor explicitly says to invert that.

**What they want:**
- ~20% framing with theory (set up the concept, cite 1-2 key formulations)
- ~80% analyzing the cultural text through that lens

**What they don't want:**
- A theory summary paper that happens to mention a TV show
- Long block quotes or paraphrases from Lowe/Chuh/Espiritu with a sentence of analysis after each

**Practical implication:** In your handwritten essay, you have roughly 4-5 pages. Your introduction should establish your thesis and briefly frame the concept (half a page). Then you should spend 3+ pages closely reading specific scenes, formal strategies, and the adaptation process. Weave theory in as analytical vocabulary, not as standalone summaries.

---

### 4. "You may want to draw on one of these theoretical texts to frame your essay"

Note the phrasing: "one of these theoretical texts." The professor is signaling that you should primarily anchor in ONE theorist's framework, not try to cite everyone. Drawing on Lowe + Chuh + Espiritu/Lowe/Yoneyama across 4 pages will feel scattered. Better to:

- **Anchor in ONE framework** (e.g., Lowe's "aestheticizing commodification" + hegemony)
- **Use a second theorist for ONE specific move** (e.g., Chuh's "collaborative antagonism" for the Huang disavowal section)
- Let the cultural text do the heavy lifting, not the theory citations

Your cheat sheet uses 3-4 theorists extensively. For the actual handwritten essay, narrow this down. Pick your strongest theoretical thread and commit to it.

---

### 5. "Writing the essay from memory" -- They're Testing Internalization, Not Recall

The professor knows you can't quote perfectly from memory. They don't care. What they're testing:

- **Do you understand the concepts deeply enough to use them as analytical tools without having the text in front of you?**
- **Can you reconstruct the logic of an argument, not just its exact words?**
- **Can you think with theory, not just about it?**

This means: paraphrasing is fine and expected. Getting a page number slightly wrong doesn't matter. What matters is that you deploy concepts precisely and in service of YOUR argument, not as decorative citations.

---

### 6. "The final essay can be a revision/expansion of this midterm"

This tells you two things:
- **Pick something you genuinely care about** (not the "safe" or "easy" choice)
- **The professor sees the midterm as a draft of your thinking** -- they want to see an ambitious, developing argument, not a polished but shallow one. They'd rather see a bold thesis that's slightly rough around the edges than a cautious one that's perfectly structured but says nothing new.

---

## What Separates an A from a B+

| **B+ Essay** | **A Essay** |
|---|---|
| Applies theory correctly to text | Uses theory to reveal something non-obvious about the text |
| Summarizes what happens, then explains what it "means" | Analyzes HOW the text produces meaning through formal strategies |
| Treats theory and text as separate (theory section, then analysis section) | Weaves theory into close reading seamlessly |
| Says "this shows differential inclusion" | Says "this produces differential inclusion through [specific mechanism], which reveals [specific tension/contradiction]" |
| Uses one reading of the scene | Shows how the scene operates on MULTIPLE levels simultaneously (content, form, production, ideology) |
| Describes | Argues |

## Specific Advice for Your FOTB / Differential Inclusion Essay

### Your Strongest Moves (lean into these):

1. **The memoir-to-TV transformation as your analytical engine.** This IS your originality. Most students analyzing FOTB will just analyze the show. You're analyzing the adaptation process itself as revealing the mechanism of differential inclusion. This is sophisticated and the professor will recognize it immediately.

2. **Formal analysis of the sitcom genre.** Analyzing the laugh track, 22-minute structure, and bright lighting as ideological apparatuses shows you understand that FORM carries meaning. This is exactly what the prompt asks for.

3. **Huang's disavowal as collaborative antagonism.** This is a genuinely interesting argumentative move -- treating the paratext (Huang's public critique) as part of the cultural text itself. It shows you're thinking beyond surface-level analysis.

### Watch Out For:

1. **Don't try to cover everything in your cheat sheet.** Your prep material covers aestheticizing commodification, hegemony, multiculturalism, model minority, strategic essentialism, AND collaborative antagonism across 9 parts. For 4-5 handwritten pages, pick 2-3 and go deep, not wide. Depth > breadth.

2. **Don't spend a full page defining differential inclusion.** The professor assigned the readings. They know what it means. Give a tight 2-3 sentence framing, then get into analysis.

3. **Your thesis is strong but long.** For handwritten in-class writing, trim it. Core claim: *The lunchbox scene's transformation from memoir to sitcom reveals differential inclusion's mechanism: Asian Americans are granted visibility only when material violence is aestheticized, structural racism is privatized into family narrative, and the sitcom form itself forecloses sustained critique.*

### Recommended Structure for 4-5 Handwritten Pages:

**Page 1: Introduction + Thesis**
- Open with the lunchbox scene (concrete, immediate)
- State thesis (how this scene demonstrates differential inclusion through its formal strategies AND its transformation from memoir)
- Brief framing with Espiritu/Lowe/Yoneyama's "violence of inclusion"

**Pages 2-3: Close Reading of the Scene (this is "the bulk")**
- Paragraph on formal strategies: laugh track, 22-min resolution, bright lighting, individual framing. Show HOW these produce differential inclusion at the level of form.
- Paragraph on memoir vs. TV: what got erased (material violence, ongoing trauma, structural analysis) and what replaced it (cute misunderstanding, quick fix, family solution). Use Lowe's "aestheticizing commodification" as analytical vocabulary here.

**Page 4: Huang's Disavowal**
- Brief on collaborative antagonism (Chuh)
- Huang used the system AND refused its terms
- This reveals that differential inclusion isn't total -- there are ways to work within and against it simultaneously

**Page 5: Stakes + Conclusion**
- Why this matters: representation =/= liberation
- The scene matters because it makes visible the mechanism (what must be erased for inclusion to be granted)
- The gap between memoir and show is where the politics live

## One Last Thing: What Does "Ace" Look Like?

Your professor (based on discussion prompts) values:
- **Precision with concepts** -- using terms exactly as theorists define them, not loosely
- **Genuine critical thinking** -- not just agreeing with theorists but pushing their ideas further, finding tensions
- **Specificity** -- concrete scenes, formal devices, not generalizations
- **Intellectual ambition** -- willingness to make a bold claim and defend it

The discussion responses that seem to get the most traction in your class are ones that either (a) identify a specific tension or limit in a theorist's argument, or (b) apply a concept to an unexpected example in a way that reveals something new.

Your essay has the ingredients. The key is execution: stay close to the text, lead with analysis not summary, and let your argument -- not the theory -- drive every paragraph.
