## Summary


### Orientalism & Techno-Orientalism Overview

- Professor introducing Edward Said’s influential concept of orientalism- Originally focused on British colonialism and Middle East- Now applied to US relationships with East/Southeast Asia- Traces how Western imaginaries shape perceptions of Asian peoples
- Upcoming guest speaker’s work examines different genealogy of Asian-technology relationships during Cold War
- Tuesday class will include orientalism primer for students new to Asian American studies

### Korean War as “Unending War” Framework

- War never officially ended after armistice agreement - became chronic state of “peacelessness”
- Current US military presence: 28,500 troops, 100+ installations south of DMZ
- War naturalized as “forgotten” in US but remains structure of fear/destruction for both Koreas
- Institutionalized peacelessness serves US imperial state building and military-industrial complex
- Americans experience war economically (reduced social services, increased defense spending) rather than directly

### Temporal Disjuncture & Racialized Violence

- US portrays foreign effects as causes - North Korean self-defense framed as unprompted aggression
- 1951 “We Charge Genocide” petition linked US racial violence at home to Korean casualties abroad
- Korean War was first with racially integrated US units- Allowed US to portray military as civil rights leader- Obscured organized violence against people of color- North Koreans called soldiers of color “cannon fodder”
- Imperial powers and war sufferers exist in different timelines - US progress vs Korean destruction

### Biopolitical Excess & War Orphans

- War orphans and comfort women cannot escape war timeline
- Their situations born directly from US imperialism/militarism conditions
- US narrativizes them as “liberated and rescued” despite ongoing trauma
- Necropolitics framework: US determines who is disposable/worthy of attack
- Korean intervention framed as success story justifying continued presence

### Knowledge Production & Historical Complicity

- US conducts war through knowledge production and academic area studies
- Discussion questions on historiographical justice:- Can truly unbiased historical process exist or do historians have responsibility to narrativize gaps?- Does institutionalization within complicit systems always reproduce complicity?
- Archives themselves contain human bias at every level- Archivists select materials- Transcribers clean up/interpret unclear content- Primary documents written by biased actors
- Academic reckoning with “post-truth” society challenges previous embrace of multiple truths

### Korean Soft Power & Cultural Forgetting

- South Korea transformed from post-war poverty to sub-imperial economic power
- Korean Wave (K-pop, K-dramas) serves as soft power export but avoids addressing war foundations
- Student examples of romanticized war content:- “Crash Landing on You” uses division as romantic barrier rather than serious historical analysis- Most Korean media consumed in US focuses on globally palatable themes
- Pattern mirrors other cultural appropriation: consuming “good” aspects while ignoring structural violence
- Successful Korean films (Parasite, Train to Busan) critique capitalism but don’t address US role

### Adoptee Experience & Ongoing Trauma

- War orphans as forced cultural exports - children migrated against their will to US families
- Adoptee experiences vary widely:- Some pursue lifelong rediscovery journey of Korean heritage- Others reject past entirely, focusing on American present
- White adoptive parents often push cultural responsibility back onto adopted children
- Adoption framed as US generosity rather than addressing structural causes of displacement
- Individual positive outcomes don’t negate broader imperial dynamics creating need for adoption
