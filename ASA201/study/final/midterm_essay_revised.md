## Differential Inclusion in *Fresh Off the Boat*

The lunchbox is a staple in American childhood. Yet, in the ABC sitcom *Fresh Off the Boat*, that very lunchbox becomes a battleground of Asian American representation, racial inclusion, and contradictions. While the show watches eleven-year-old Edison Huang encounter awkward and tense moments in his life, the audience views his life through a comedic framework. The story is, however, very real. When Eddie brings in his homemade, Chinese lunch, he is immediately laughed at for bringing in "worms" that had an awful smell. Eddie's reaction and his mom's response to buy "Lunchables" to fit in become an apparent case study for differential inclusion: the act of being included into mainstream at the expense of accepting the terms of the ones in power.

The very nature of differential inclusion is complicated and contradicted even within the pilot episode, though, which provides a strong example on how to accept its terms or reject it.

The very visual aperture of the ABC-family sitcom adoption of Eddie Huang's memoir complicates differential inclusion. While differential inclusion often is seen by losing an aspect of culture or even one's self, the show uses humor to subvert that narrative.

After the incident at the lunch table, the narration jokes about living in a "Lunchable world" and how Jessica (Eddie's mom) had to venture into a supermarket. This resolution, however, reveals that racism can be solved through purchasing "whiteness." It almost seems like this is the cost of admission, or the aestheticizing commodification of what is considered white. Even more peculiar is the narration of this scene done by Eddie Huang himself.

By using his own voice and his own story, the show lulls the audience into a false sense of security that Eddie has successfully overcome racism and this is something of the past. It hides the fact that Edison reads scripted lines written by ABC producers.

The lunchbox scene becomes a powerful illustration of how differential inclusion masks the structural racism and microaggressions by offering opportunity, like a seat at a table or a seat on national television.

Yet, the very nature of differential inclusion contradicts itself even in a 22-minute sitcom. The lunchbox again plays a powerful role in establishing that the system was never in Eddie's favor. Even after obtaining the lunchbox, Eddie encounters a bully who cuts him in line. We see the scene unfolding, the hip hop music fading into the background, and the lunchroom becoming silent. The bully calls Eddie a "chink," and we feel the full weight of the word. Eddie aggressively fights back. The scene cuts straight to the principal's office where Jessica surprisingly stands up for Eddie and threatens to sue the "American Way." This anecdote displays the self-defeating nature of differential inclusion: even after buying the solution of Lunchables, Eddie is still bullied.

What brings this point home is Edison Huang's narration itself. By the end of season 1, Huang refused to narrate his own story. This very action of quitting demonstrates an active rebellion against the narrative ABC was pushing. His action highlights the role of collaborative antagonism to gain mainstream attraction by conformity to certain rules but also standing for his own values and story. By rejecting his own memoir's representation, Huang not only shows why differential inclusion can never truly fulfill its promise of inclusion, but also shows how to turn these realizations into concrete actions.
