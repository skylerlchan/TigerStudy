Prompt: These readings from Zahzah and Troeung give us different views of the difficulties many refugees face in the "afterlives" of war after relocation to the West. While Zahzah traces how state programs like the "CVE" continue to repress and criminalize Middle East and African refugees even in San Diego under the guise of "care economies," Troeung gives us a powerful look into how refugees face different problems with "care" when they are identified as subjects of charity and pity. How does Troeung's concept of "refugee lifeworlds" describe different possibilities for self-expression and political change beyond state "care" for refugees in San Diego and Canadian, respectively? (Zahzah does not use the term itself but what they describe resonates with Troeung's concept).

In both cases, Zahzah and Troeung reveal how care systems function as mechanisms of control rather than support. In Zahzah's case, it's CVE surveillance and in Troeung's case, it's charitable pity. This fundamental paradox reveals how care is a power play. Troeung's concept of refugee life worlds is best described by her account of the Cambodian genocide, stating that, "We need to look at it, think about it, mourn it, be angry about it, and at least live in it for a little while." By simply remembering the past, Troeung is saying that is how refugees fight back against being controlled. The state is threatened because their version is attacked by the first person account of the refugees. Hence, the "lifeworlds" become a form of resistance using memory and community, turning refugees into analysts and witnesses rather than victims or threats. Zahzah's analysis of San Diego's CVE program reveals the same dynamic in her term "warcare economies." Under CVE, the very public assistance that Muslim and African refugees use become a source of surveillance data and control. Specifically, Zahzah explains that CVE creates "the illusion of benevolent, good-faith humanitarianism to build trust with community members, encouraging support for patriotic agendas to freely access intel. In turn, communities believe there is a safe space and open up, as programmers identify political threats and criminalize the opinions and actions of those presumed threats." Yet, refugees can resist by maintaining what Troeung calls illegible practices: organizing autonomously, preserving community solidarities, and refusing total transparency.
