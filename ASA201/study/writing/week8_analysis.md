# The Impossibility of Unbiased Humanitarian Care: Warcare Economies and Refugee Lifeworlds

## The Care-Control Paradox

Both Zahzah and Troeung dismantle the fiction of "neutral" humanitarian care, revealing how care systems function as sophisticated mechanisms of state control, surveillance, and assimilation. Rather than ask whether unbiased care is merely difficult to achieve, these scholars demonstrate that the very architecture of Western humanitarian frameworks is constitutively bound to security apparatuses and imperial projects. The answer is not that care "becomes" corrupted by surveillance—it is that care and control are co-constitutive in the afterlives of war.

## Zahzah: Warcare Economies and the CVE Apparatus

Zahzah's concept of "warcare economies" exposes the material symbiosis between U.S. militarization and humanitarian programming. In San Diego, Countering Violent Extremism (CVE) programs operate through a deliberate paradox: they present themselves as benevolent social services while simultaneously extracting intelligence and criminalizing political dissent. The programs Zahzah documents—vocational workshops, youth programming, "peacemaking" initiatives—appear as care, but function as surveillance infrastructure.

The mechanism is insidious. CVE architects weaponize refugees' material needs against them. As Zahzah explains, these programs "use the illusion of benevolent, good-faith humanitarianism to build trust with community members, encouraging support for patriotic agendas to freely access intel." Refugees accessing public assistance, English classes, or community spaces become legible to the security state. Their survival needs are transformed into data extraction points. This is not care co-opted by surveillance—it is care designed from inception as a surveillance technology.

What makes warcare economies particularly devastating is how they exploit the structural vulnerability created by U.S. imperialism itself. San Diego's Somali, Iraqi, Syrian, and Palestinian refugees fled conflicts directly precipitated by U.S. military intervention. Yet the violence prevention framework depoliticizes this history entirely, locating "extremism" in individual psychology, religious ideology, or cultural deficiency rather than in state violence. As Zahzah notes, U.S. militarization in Somalia, Iraq, Syria, and Palestine is framed as legitimate self-defense, while refugees' political consciousness about these interventions is pathologized as "radicalization."

The gendered dimensions of CVE further reveal care's entanglement with imperial projects. Programs like "Women, Peace, & Security" and "Women Peacemakers" selectively invoke liberal feminist frameworks to recruit women as informants in their own communities. Under the guise of "empowerment," women are trained to identify family members and neighbors as security threats. This precise exploitation of feminist language demonstrates how humanitarian care cannot be disentangled from counterinsurgency—the vocabulary of women's rights becomes instrumental to surveillance regimes.

## Troeung: Refugee Lifeworlds and the Violence of Charitable Pity

Where Zahzah examines overt surveillance, Troeung illuminates the more subtle violence of charitable humanitarianism that constructs refugees as objects of pity, gratitude, and rescue. The Canadian state's incorporation of Cambodian refugees operates through what Troeung calls "settler subjectivity"—refugees must perform gratitude for their "rescue" and assimilate into narratives of Canadian benevolence. This assimilation pressure is a form of epistemic violence that silences refugee knowledge and flattens complex histories of survival into simplistic tropes of victimhood.

Troeung's concept of "refugee lifeworlds" offers a counter-framework: the lived, embodied, and relational experiences that refugees carry which exceed and resist state categorization. These lifeworlds encompass memory practices, community solidarities, embodied knowledge of trauma and survival, and political consciousness forged through displacement. Critically, lifeworlds are what the state seeks to render illegible—they represent autonomous spaces of meaning-making that threaten official narratives.

The state's imposition of the "refugee" category itself operates as a technology of control. As Troeung argues, the refugee label is not a neutral descriptor but "emerges from liberal imperial technologies of governance designed to police the boundaries of when, how, and who is granted asylum." The label strips refugees of political agency, reducing them to subjects requiring protection rather than recognizing them as witnesses, analysts, and survivors with critical knowledge about war, genocide, and imperial violence.

The Canadian government's celebration of Cambodian refugees as the "last" and "lucky" recipients of humanitarian generosity—epitomized in the images of baby Troeung waving a Canadian flag—produces what she calls "the violence of benevolence." Refugees are incorporated into national mythology as evidence of Canadian moral superiority, while the actual conditions that necessitated their flight (including U.S. bombing of Cambodia) are erased. Humanitarian care here functions as historical amnesia, demanding refugees' complicity in forgetting the violence that produced their displacement.

## Refugee Lifeworlds as Resistance

Both scholars point toward how refugees resist these regimes of care-as-control, though neither romanticizes this resistance. Troeung's emphasis on memory work—"We need to look at it, think about it, mourn it, be angry about it, and at least live in it for a little while"—suggests that simply preserving first-person accounts and refusing state narratives constitutes a threat to the humanitarian apparatus. When refugees insist on their own historical analysis rather than accepting rescue narratives, they become "illegible" to state frameworks—and this illegibility creates space for autonomous worldmaking.

In Zahzah's account, San Diego's Muslim and African refugee communities have challenged CVE incorporation through community organizing, refusing facilitators at community events, changing where they access services, and self-advocating at municipal meetings. These practices maintain what we might call, borrowing Troeung's language, the illegible lifeworlds that refuse total transparency to the security state. Refugees preserve community solidarities that cannot be fully penetrated by surveillance, maintaining political consciousness that the violence prevention framework seeks to eliminate.

Yet both scholars also emphasize the severe constraints on this resistance. Zahzah documents how refugees who express "material political resistance" face criminalization and counterinsurgency punishment. Young Somali Americans have been incarcerated for sending small sums to Al Shabaab, while Syrian refugees face surveillance for opposing U.S. intervention. The CVE apparatus criminalizes precisely the political consciousness that would allow refugees to name U.S. imperialism as the source of their displacement. Similarly, Troeung shows how the psychologization of Cambodian refugees as "traumatized"—with diagnostic rates as high as 86% for PTSD—pathologizes their knowledge of violence, recasting structural and historical analysis as individual mental illness.

## The Structural Impossibility of Unbiased Care

What emerges from reading these scholars together is that "unbiased humanitarian care" is a conceptual impossibility within the current global order. This is not a failure of implementation or a problem of insufficient resources, but a structural feature of how humanitarian systems operate within settler-colonial nation-states embedded in imperial warfare.

Several factors make this impossibility structural rather than contingent:

**First, the symbiosis of NGO economies and militarism**: As Zahzah documents, CVE programming flows through institutions like USAID, the UN, the Department of Homeland Security, and the military. The same governmental sources funding refugee resettlement also fund counterinsurgency operations. This is not coincidental infiltration but constitutive—humanitarian infrastructure is built as an extension of war-making capacity.

**Second, the depoliticization of displacement**: Both humanitarian pity (Troeung) and violence prevention frameworks (Zahzah) require erasing the political-economic causes of refugee displacement. To acknowledge that U.S. bombing created Cambodian refugees, that U.S. intervention in Somalia generated displacement, or that U.S. support for Israeli occupation produces Palestinian refugees would undermine the rescue narrative. Care can only be offered on the condition that refugees forget why care became necessary.

**Third, the instrumentalization of vulnerability**: Warcare economies depend on the material precarity of refugee populations. Refugees' need for housing, language education, employment, and community resources becomes the leverage point for surveillance and political control. "Care" is offered precisely because need can be weaponized. As Zahzah notes, this is "a precise exploitation of material need that leverages political control—it is warcare."

**Fourth, the assimilation imperative**: Both scholars show how refugees must perform integration into national narratives—Canadian benevolence or American patriotism—as a condition of receiving care. This assimilation pressure targets refugees' political consciousness, demanding they accept state violence as legitimate and reframe their own displacement through individualized therapeutic or cultural narratives rather than structural analysis.

## Toward Abolitionist Alternatives

If unbiased humanitarian care is impossible within current structures, what alternatives exist? Both scholars gesture toward abolitionist frameworks that refuse to reform care systems and instead imagine refugee self-determination beyond state control.

This would require, at minimum: demilitarization and reparations from countries responsible for producing displacement; recognition of refugees' political knowledge and historical witness rather than their pathologization; material support without surveillance or assimilation demands; and ultimately, the abolition of the border regimes and imperial warfare that produce "refugees" as a category of displaced persons in the first place.

Troeung's concept of refugee lifeworlds points toward forms of knowledge, community, and meaning-making that exist despite and against humanitarian frameworks. These lifeworlds are not solutions to be scaled up, but reminders that refugees have always created autonomous spaces of survival and resistance that exceed state recognition. The question is not how to make humanitarian care unbiased, but how to dismantle the systems that make humanitarian care necessary while extracting political compliance in exchange for survival.

As Zahzah concludes, we must broaden our analysis "to incorporate the many layers of war, migration, and repression" to make calculated gains in struggles against hegemonic violence. This means refusing the false choice between surveillance-laden CVE programs and abandonment, between charitable pity and exclusion. It means recognizing that truly supporting refugees requires confronting the imperial warfare and racial capitalism that produces displacement—a project that cannot be accomplished through humanitarian care systems that exist to manage rather than abolish these structures of violence.

## Redefining Care: From Humanitarian Management to Revolutionary Solidarity

If state-administered humanitarian care is structurally bound to surveillance, assimilation, and control, how do we redefine care itself? Both Zahzah and Troeung point toward alternative frameworks that emerge from refugee lifeworlds and community organizing rather than state benevolence. These alternatives don't reform humanitarian systems but fundamentally reimagine care's relationship to power, knowledge, and political struggle.

### 1. Care as Solidarity, Not Charity

The first redefinition requires severing care from the charity-pity-gratitude nexus that Troeung critiques. Care reimagined as solidarity operates between equals engaged in mutual struggle rather than from saviors to victims. This means:

**Recognizing shared stakes in dismantling empire**: When San Diego refugees organize against CVE, they're not asking for better services—they're fighting the same surveillance state that targets Black, Indigenous, and colonized communities domestically. Their struggle against warcare economies is continuous with anti-war, abolitionist, and decolonial movements. Care-as-solidarity means non-refugees understanding that refugee liberation is bound up with everyone's liberation from militarized policing and imperial violence.

**Refusing the rescue narrative**: Troeung's analysis reveals how rescue narratives position refugees as perpetually indebted to their "saviors." Care-as-solidarity rejects this entirely. It acknowledges that refugees are survivors of violence that Western states perpetrated or enabled, meaning solidarity is not generosity but accountability. The U.S. owes reparations to Cambodian, Vietnamese, Somali, Iraqi, Syrian, Palestinian, and Afghan refugees—not as charity but as restitution for imperial destruction.

**Horizontal rather than vertical relations**: Zahzah documents how Muslim and African communities in San Diego organize autonomously, making decisions collectively about which services to trust, how to protect members from surveillance, and how to maintain political consciousness. This mutual aid and collective decision-making models care as horizontal relationship rather than top-down service delivery.

### 2. Care That Doesn't Demand Legibility

The state's hunger for refugee legibility—through intake forms, trauma assessments, surveillance databases, integration metrics—is central to how humanitarian care becomes control. Redefining care requires embracing and protecting illegibility.

**Opacity as refuge**: Scholar Édouard Glissant's concept of "the right to opacity" is instructive here. Refugees don't owe the state (or the humanitarian apparatus) total transparency about their histories, politics, traumas, or communities. Care that respects opacity doesn't require refugees to perform their suffering, explain their politics, or make their communities legible to outsiders as a condition of support.

**Preserving autonomous spaces**: Troeung's refugee lifeworlds exist precisely because they can't be fully captured by state categorization. Care means protecting these spaces—temple gatherings, community organizing, informal networks, cultural practices—from institutional penetration. This is why Zahzah's documentation of refugees refusing CVE facilitators at community events is crucial: it maintains spaces where political consciousness can develop without surveillance.

**Rejecting extractive knowledge production**: Much humanitarian work demands refugees narrate their traumas, migration journeys, and cultural backgrounds for NGO reports, grant applications, and research studies. Care redefined means refugees control their own narratives and aren't required to produce knowledge for institutional consumption. When refugees choose to share testimony—as Troeung does about Cambodian genocide—it's on their terms, for their purposes, within their communities.

### 3. Care That Centers Political Consciousness

Both scholars show how humanitarian systems depoliticize refugees—through trauma frameworks that psychologize structural violence, through gratitude narratives that erase imperial causation, through CVE that criminalizes anti-imperial politics. Care redefined must do the opposite: it must support and amplify refugees' political analysis.

**Refugees as analysts and witnesses**: Troeung insists refugees carry critical knowledge about genocide, war, and empire that dominant institutions seek to suppress. Care means treating refugees as the primary experts on the conditions that displaced them. Cambodian refugees understand U.S. imperial violence in Southeast Asia. Somali refugees understand U.S. militarization of the Horn of Africa. Iraqi refugees understand occupation. Palestinian refugees understand settler colonialism. Care means centering rather than pathologizing this knowledge.

**Supporting political organizing**: Real care supports refugees' self-organization for political change—not "civic engagement" programs that channel participation into state-approved forms, but autonomous organizing that challenges the conditions producing displacement. This includes supporting refugees who advocate against ongoing U.S. military intervention, who connect their struggles to domestic abolitionist movements, who organize for Palestinian liberation or against sanctions.

**Historical consciousness against amnesia**: Where state care demands amnesia about what created displacement, care-as-solidarity preserves historical memory. This means teaching about the U.S. Secret War in Laos and Cambodia in schools with Southeast Asian refugee communities. It means acknowledging how U.S. support for Siad Barre's regime contributed to Somali state collapse. It means recognizing U.S. complicity in creating the conditions refugees flee from.

### 4. Care Without Conditional Assimilation

State humanitarian care offers survival in exchange for assimilation—learning English, adopting "American values," performing patriotism, integrating into labor markets on exploitative terms, accepting subordinate positions within racial hierarchies. Care redefined rejects these conditions entirely.

**Unconditional material support**: People deserve housing, healthcare, education, and safety without having to renounce their languages, religions, cultures, or politics. This seems obvious but contradicts the entire integration framework that makes services conditional on assimilation metrics. Care means material support with no strings attached—no English proficiency requirements, no patriotism tests, no demands to abandon political consciousness.

**Cultural preservation and self-determination**: Rather than treating refugee cultures as deficits to overcome through integration, care supports communities' efforts to preserve languages, cultural practices, religious traditions, and ways of life. This includes supporting mother-tongue education, community-controlled cultural institutions, and autonomous governance structures within refugee communities.

**The right to return and the right to stay**: Care acknowledges that many refugees want to return to their homelands once conditions allow, while others build permanent lives in diaspora. Neither choice should be coerced. This means supporting both efforts to transform political conditions in countries of origin (which might be "radical" or opposed to U.S. interests) and efforts to build thriving autonomous communities in places of resettlement.

### 5. Care as Reparative Accountability

Perhaps most fundamentally, redefining care means transforming it from benevolent gift to reparative accountability for the violence that created displacement.

**Reparations, not charity**: The U.S., Canada, and European states owe material reparations to refugees from countries they bombed, occupied, sanctioned, or destabilized. These reparations aren't "aid" but compensation for destruction. They should be substantial enough to allow real community self-determination—not subsistence survival that maintains dependence. And crucially, they should be provided without surveillance, data extraction, or political conditions.

**Demilitarization as care**: The most meaningful form of care would be to stop producing refugees in the first place. This means ending wars, occupations, sanctions, drone strikes, arms sales, and support for dictatorships and settler colonialism. Every dollar diverted from the Pentagon's $886 billion budget to no-strings-attached refugee support represents a shift from war-making to actual care.

**Dismantling border regimes**: Immigration restrictions, detention systems, and deportation machinery are forms of violence that make refugees perpetually precarious even after resettlement. Care means fighting for open borders, abolishing ICE and CBP, ending detention and deportation, and ensuring everyone has unconditional right to move and stay.

### 6. Care Grounded in Refugee Lifeworlds

Finally, Troeung's concept of refugee lifeworlds suggests that care already exists within refugee communities—it doesn't need to be imported by NGOs or state programs. The question is how to support what already exists rather than impose external frameworks.

**Funding community-controlled infrastructure**: Rather than funding NGOs to deliver services to refugees, care means providing resources directly to refugee-led organizations with no programmatic strings attached. This allows communities to define their own needs and solutions rather than conforming to institutional priorities.

**Recognizing existing care practices**: Refugee communities already have mutual aid networks, healing practices, conflict resolution traditions, and ways of caring for each other that predate humanitarian intervention. Care means respecting and supporting these practices rather than replacing them with professionalized services.

**Long-term commitment**: State humanitarian programs treat refugees as a crisis requiring short-term intervention followed by "self-sufficiency." But displacement's effects span generations. Care means sustained, long-term commitment to refugee communities—not to manage them into integration, but to support their self-determined futures.

### The Limits and Possibilities

None of these redefinitions can be fully realized within current state structures. As both scholars demonstrate, state institutions exist to manage and control displaced populations, not to enable their liberation. This means that truly redefining care likely requires building alternative structures outside and against the state—mutual aid networks, autonomous refugee-led organizations, transnational solidarity movements.

Yet both scholars also document how refugees are already doing this work: maintaining illegible lifeworlds, organizing against CVE, preserving political consciousness, building autonomous communities. The task isn't to invent new forms of care from scratch, but to learn from, support, and join struggles that refugees are already leading.

Redefining care ultimately means recognizing that refugees don't need to be cared for—they need the structural violence targeting them to end. They need the resources and power to care for themselves and each other on their own terms. And they need those of us implicated in empire to stop confusing our complicity with compassion, to stop offering surveillance as sanctuary, to stop demanding assimilation as the price of survival.

Care, truly redefined, looks less like humanitarian programs and more like revolution: the dismantling of imperial warfare, border regimes, and racial capitalism, and the building of worlds where displacement doesn't happen because everyone has what they need to flourish where they are—or move freely if they choose.
