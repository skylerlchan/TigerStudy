# Puar Discussion Responses: Sexuality, Orientalism, and Imperial Justification

---

## The Question (Broken Down)

The prompt has three interlocking parts:

1. **BUILDS ON:** How does Puar's focus on sexuality/gender as an East-West dividing line *extend* existing arguments about US imperial relationships (Orientalism, exceptionalism, civilizing missions)?
2. **COMPLICATES:** How does Puar's framework *destabilize or challenge* those same arguments -- what does she reveal that prior critiques missed?
3. **INTERVENTION:** How does all of this specifically function as *justification for intervention* into nations framed as "backwards"?

Below are multiple standalone quote-analysis responses for each part. Each can work on its own in discussion.

---

---

## PART 1: How Puar BUILDS ON Previous Discussions of US Empire

---

### Response 1A: The "Coming Out" of Empire

> "This sanctioning of the lingua franca of gay liberation hints that the liberation of American empire from its closets -- an empire already known but concealed -- will and should result in pride, a proud American empire." (Puar, 2)

**Analysis:** This is a devastating opening move. Puar catches the US appropriating the foundational metaphor of queer liberation -- "coming out" -- to narrate its own imperial self-realization. This builds directly on earlier discussions of American exceptionalism: the US has always framed itself as reluctantly powerful, a benevolent force dragged into global leadership. What Puar exposes is that the *language of sexual identity* has been absorbed into that national myth. "Coming out" as an empire maps the queer narrative of hidden truth revealed onto the geopolitical stage -- the US isn't conquering, it's *finally being authentic about what it always was*. The same rhetorical structure that makes "coming out" feel brave and liberating for an individual now makes imperial expansion feel honest and courageous for a nation. Empire becomes a form of self-actualization -- and anyone who opposes it is pushing America back into the closet.

---

### Response 1B: Gender Exceptionalism Was Already the Template

> "Gender exceptionalism works as a missionary discourse to rescue Muslim women from their oppressive male counterparts. It also works to suggest that, in contrast to women in the United States, Muslim women are, at the end of the day, unsavable." (Puar, 5)

**Analysis:** This builds on what scholars like Gayatri Spivak called "white men saving brown women from brown men" -- the feminist alibi for colonialism. We already knew that the US used *gender* as an imperial measuring stick: the oppressed Afghan woman in her burqa became the reason the US "had to" invade. But Puar surfaces the cruel double-bind buried inside this logic: the "rescue" mission is designed to fail. Muslim women are positioned as needing saving *and* as fundamentally unsavable -- which means the intervention never has to end. The template has now been *expanded* from gender to sexuality. It is no longer just "their women are oppressed, so we must act." Now it is also "their sexuality is pathological, so we must act." The framework hasn't changed -- the West still positions itself as the liberator of bodies it claims are trapped -- but the *range of bodies it claims to liberate has widened*, and so has the range of wars it can justify.

---

### Response 1C: Homonormativity Joins Heteronormativity in the Imperial Project

> "An exceptional form of national heteronormativity is now joined by an exceptional form of national homonormativity, in other words, homonationalism. Collectively, they continue or extend the project of U.S. nationalism and imperial expansion endemic to the war on terror." (Puar, 2)

**Analysis:** Classical Orientalism drew the East-West line using categories like rationality, democracy, and progress. Puar shows that sexual tolerance has now been *added to that list of civilizational benchmarks* -- and this is not a disruption of heteronormative nationalism but an *extension* of it. The state didn't abandon its investment in heteronormativity; it simply added a second, parallel track. Now there are *two* sexual norms working together for empire: the good heterosexual family *and* the good homonormative couple. Both are "exceptional," both are "American," and both are defined *against* the sexually deviant Muslim Other. What makes this especially powerful is the timing. The *same* post-9/11 moment that intensified surveillance and violence against Muslim, Arab, and South Asian communities *also* saw the expansion of certain gay rights domestically (Lawrence v. Texas, 2003). The queer patriot and the Muslim terrorist are co-constituted -- you cannot produce one without the other.

## PART 2: How Puar COMPLICATES Previous Discussions

---

### Response 2A: "Liberation" Itself Becomes the Problem

> "The paradigm of gay liberation and emancipation has produced all sorts of troubling narratives: about the greater homophobia of immigrant communities and communities of color, about the stricter family values and mores in these communities, about a certain prerequisite migration from home, about coming-out teleologies." (Puar, 22)

**Analysis:** This is where Puar breaks from the standard critique of empire. Previous discussions tended to argue: "The US *claims* to bring freedom, but really it brings domination." Puar goes further. She asks: what if the *concept of liberation itself* -- as the West defines it -- is already a form of domination? When Western queerness defines liberation as "coming out," as visibility, as leaving your community of origin, it creates a framework where anyone who doesn't perform liberation *in that specific way* is automatically classified as unfree. Communities of color and immigrant communities are preemptively labeled as "more homophobic" -- not because of any universal measure, but because they don't conform to a white Western teleology of sexual progress. The complication is profound: the West doesn't just *fail* to liberate the East. The very *definition of liberation* that the West exports is a cultural product of Western individualism being imposed as a universal truth.

---

### Response 2B: The Terrorist Is Already Queered

> "The proper modern gay or lesbian Muslim subject is foreclosed, while the terrorist is forever queer, improperly sexual, embedded in an 'always already homosexualized population.'" (Puar, 14)

**Analysis:** This is the most disorienting complication. In prior discussions, the "backwards Other" was framed as *heterosexually* oppressive -- patriarchal, repressive, puritanical. Puar reveals a contradictory undercurrent: the Muslim/terrorist figure is *simultaneously* coded as excessively, deviously sexual. The Abu Ghraib photographs, the obsession with "sodomy" in depictions of Muslim male sociality, the fantasy of the harem -- these are not incidental. The proper queer Muslim -- someone who is both Muslim and gay in a legible, dignified way -- is made impossible. Meanwhile, the entire Muslim population is *queered from the outside*: sexualized, made deviant, rendered perverse by the Western gaze. The Oriental Other is trapped in an impossible bind: too sexually repressed (they oppress women, they punish homosexuality) *and* too sexually deviant (they are pedophiles, sodomites, perverts) *at the same time*. The East isn't coherently represented as *anything* -- it is whatever the West needs it to be in a given moment. The "backwardness" ascribed to the Other is not a stable category but a *moving target* that serves empire.

---

### Response 2C: Inclusion as a Weapon

> "The cost of being folded into life might be quite steep, both for the subjects who are interpellated by or aspire to the tight inclusiveness of homonormativity offered in this moment, and for those who decline or are declined entry due to the undesirability of their race, ethnicity, religion, class, national origin, age, or bodily ability." (Puar, 10)

**Analysis:** Previous critiques of empire focused on *exclusion* -- how the US excludes, marginalizes, and dehumanizes the Other. Puar complicates this by showing that *inclusion can be just as violent*. Being "folded into life" -- accepted into the national body -- comes at a cost. For those who get in (white, normative, patriotic, consuming queer subjects), the price is complicity: your inclusion is used as proof of American superiority. For those who don't get in (queer people of color, queer Muslims, non-normative queers), the price is intensified exclusion: the nation can now say it's welcoming, so if you're still outside, the problem must be *you*. The complication for anyone invested in progressive politics is gut-wrenching: the very civil rights gains that feel like victories (marriage equality, anti-discrimination protections) can be *instrumentalized by the state* to fuel war abroad. Your liberation becomes their justification for someone else's destruction. Puar forces us to ask: who benefits when the state "accepts" you, and what is being done in your name?

## PART 3: How This Functions as JUSTIFICATION FOR INTERVENTION

---

### Response 3A: The Sexual Rescue Narrative

> "Gay marriage, 'less about gay rights and more about codifying an ideal of European values,' has become a steep but necessary insurance premium... whereby an otherwise ambivalent if not hostile populace can guarantee that extra bit of security that is bought by yet another marker in the distance between barbarism and civilization, one that justifies further targeting of a perversely sexualized and racialized Muslim population." (Puar, 20)

**Analysis:** This is the mechanism laid bare. Gay rights have become an "insurance premium" -- a cost that Western societies pay not out of genuine ethical commitment, but because it purchases *civilizational distance* from the Muslim Other. If gay marriage = civilization, then nations without it = barbarism. And barbarism, as previous discussions have established, has *always* been the justification for intervention. What Puar reveals is that sexuality has become the *newest and most effective* barometer for this judgment. It works precisely because it feels progressive -- it doesn't *look* like old-school racism or colonialism. When the US says "we must act because they execute gay people," it sounds like humanitarianism. But the structure underneath is identical to every prior intervention, from the Philippines to Vietnam to Iraq. The metric changes (from Christianity, to democracy, to women's rights, to now gay rights), but the underlying logic -- "we are advanced, they are backwards, therefore we must intervene" -- never does. The brilliance of using sexuality is that it recruits liberal and progressive constituencies into supporting the imperial project. The very people who might have opposed the Iraq War on anti-imperialist grounds can be brought on board if the war is reframed as a project of sexual liberation.

---

### Response 3B: Abu Ghraib as the Logic Made Visible

> "The 'sexual torture scandal' at Abu Ghraib is an instructive example of the interplay between exception and exceptionalism whereby the deferred death of one population recedes as the securitization and valorization of the life of another population triumphs in its shadow." (Puar, 3)

**Analysis:** Abu Ghraib is typically discussed as a scandal -- a failure, an aberration, something that went wrong. Puar reframes it as the *logical outcome* of the entire framework. The torture was sexualized -- forced nudity, simulated homosexual acts, the leash photograph -- because the entire war was built on the premise that Muslim sexuality is deviant. The torturers didn't invent this out of nowhere; they drew on the same Orientalist fantasies that structured the justification for intervention in the first place. And the framing as an "exception" -- a few bad apples -- is itself part of the machinery of exceptionalism. The US gets to simultaneously *commit* sexualized violence and *disavow* it, preserving its self-image as sexually civilized. Meanwhile, the "deferred death" of the Muslim population -- targeted, tortured, dehumanized -- is obscured by the "valorization of life" of the American population it supposedly protects. The justification for war doesn't just *precede* the violence -- it *shapes the specific form the violence takes*. The way the US told itself stories about Muslim sexuality before the invasion directly produced the specific kinds of cruelty enacted on those bodies during occupation.

---

### Response 3C: Homonationalism Conscripts the Oppressed into Oppression

> "It is precisely by denying culpability or assuming that one is not implicated in violent relations toward others, that one is outside of them, that violence can be perpetuated." (Puar, 24)

**Analysis:** The most insidious dimension of homonationalism as a justification for intervention is *who it recruits to carry it out*. Queer communities in the US -- communities that have themselves experienced state violence, police brutality, medical pathologization, legal exclusion -- are now invited to identify *with* the state against the Muslim Other. "Support the troops -- they're fighting for your right to marry." "These are countries where they'd throw you off a building." The intervention is justified not just by abstract ideals of freedom but by making queer Americans feel that the war is *personal*, that it's being fought *for them*. And Puar's quote cuts to why this works: these communities can perpetuate violence precisely because they *assume they are outside of it*. Their own history of marginalization becomes proof of innocence -- "how could we, as an oppressed group, be complicit in someone else's oppression?" This is what makes homonationalism more dangerous than older forms of imperial justification: it turns formerly marginalized communities into stakeholders in empire, and their very history of suffering becomes the shield that prevents them from seeing what's being done in their name.

---

### Response 3D: The Impossible Muslim Subject

> "'It's bad enough to be hated for being gay,' says Mahmoud, a Muslim living in Pittsburgh who asked that his real name not be used. 'But now I'm also hated for being Muslim. That mistrust seems to emanate from all Americans too. I'd hoped that my gay friends -- themselves the target of so much prejudice -- would be more likely to question the stereotypes. But my gay friends are no better than anyone else.'" (quoted in Puar, 15)

**Analysis:** This is the human cost of the justification framework made concrete. Mahmoud's testimony reveals what happens when sexuality is weaponized as a dividing line between East and West: actual queer Muslims are *erased from both sides*. The intervention narrative requires a clean binary -- the liberated gay Westerner vs. the repressive Muslim society. A person who is both gay *and* Muslim collapses that binary, so they must be made invisible. Mahmoud cannot be legible to the imperial framework. His Muslim identity makes him a potential target of the war on terror; his gay identity should make him a beneficiary of it. He is caught in the crossfire of a geopolitical narrative that has no place for him. And the most devastating line -- "my gay friends are no better than anyone else" -- reveals that homonationalism has done its work. The very community that should understand prejudice has internalized the Orientalist framework so completely that they reproduce it against someone who shares their marginalization. The justification for intervention doesn't just operate at the level of policy. It restructures intimacy, friendship, and solidarity at the ground level.
