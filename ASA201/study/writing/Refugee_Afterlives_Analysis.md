# The Product Is You: Humanitarian Care as Surveillance Infrastructure

## TL;DR - The "Free" Care Model

**If humanitarian care is free, you're the product.**

Refugees escaping U.S. wars get "free" services in America—but the price is surveillance, political compliance, and giving up their analysis of why they're refugees in the first place. San Diego's CVE programs offer job training and community centers while mining refugees for intelligence data. Canada celebrates "rescuing" Cambodian refugees while erasing that U.S. bombs created the crisis. Both systems offer survival in exchange for forgetting who caused the displacement and accepting that any anger about it makes you a terrorist threat.

The care isn't corrupted by surveillance—**it's designed as surveillance**. The product being extracted is political compliance, historical amnesia, and data on "radicalization threats."

## The Jaw-Dropping Shit

### 1. "Women's Empowerment" Programs Are Recruitment for Informants

Zahzah exposes how CVE programs in San Diego weaponize feminist language to turn refugee women into informants on their own families. Programs called "Women, Peace, & Security" and "Women Peacemakers" train women to identify sons, husbands, and neighbors as terrorist threats. Liberal feminism becomes counterinsurgency. Empowerment means reporting your community to DHS.

### 2. The U.S. Bombs Your Country, Then Surveils You for Being Mad About It

San Diego's Somali, Iraqi, Syrian, and Palestinian refugees fled U.S. military intervention. CVE treats their political consciousness about this as "radicalization risk." Young Somali Americans get incarcerated for sending small amounts to relatives in Somalia (terrorism charges), while U.S. drone strikes that created displacement are called "self-defense." The violence that created refugees is legitimate; refugees' anger about it is criminal.

### 3. Trauma Diagnosis as Political Suppression

86% of Cambodian refugees are diagnosed with PTSD—the highest rate of any refugee group. Troeung reveals this isn't just medical care but political containment. Psychological frameworks reframe structural violence (U.S. bombing campaigns, genocide, imperial war) as individual mental illness. When refugees analyze U.S. imperialism, they're told they need therapy. Their political knowledge is pathologized as trauma symptoms.

### 4. Refugees Must Perform Gratitude for Being "Rescued" from Violence Their "Saviors" Caused

Canada celebrates its "humanitarian generosity" to Cambodian refugees while completely erasing that U.S. secret bombing of Cambodia (which Canada supported diplomatically) destabilized the country and enabled the Khmer Rouge genocide. Refugees are incorporated into national mythology as evidence of Western benevolence—but only if they forget the West caused their displacement. Baby Troeung waving a Canadian flag becomes propaganda while the bombs that made her a refugee disappear from history.

### 5. Public Assistance Programs Double as Intelligence Databases

Every interaction refugees have with social services feeds CVE surveillance. Language classes, job training, youth programs, housing assistance—all become data collection points. Material need is weaponized: refugees must choose between accessing survival resources or avoiding the surveillance that comes with them. Zahzah calls this "warcare economies"—the fusion of humanitarian programming with counterinsurgency intelligence gathering.

### 6. The Same Institutions Fund Both Refugee Resettlement and Military Operations

USAID, the UN, Department of Homeland Security—the agencies funding CVE "care" programs are the same ones funding wars that create refugees. This isn't a bug, it's the design. Humanitarian infrastructure is built as an extension of war-making capacity. The NGO economy and military-industrial complex aren't separate—they're symbiotic.

### 7. "Integration" Means Accepting Your Place at the Bottom of Racial Capitalism

Humanitarian care offers refugees access to exploitative labor markets, subordinate positions in racial hierarchies, and conditional belonging that requires renouncing political consciousness. "Successful integration" means working low-wage jobs, policing your own community for "extremism," teaching your children to be grateful, and never publicly connecting U.S. foreign policy to your displacement.

## The Core Argument: Care and Control Are the Same Thing

Troeung and Zahzah don't argue humanitarian care is "corrupted" by surveillance or "sometimes" serves security agendas. They demonstrate that **humanitarian care and imperial control are co-constitutive**—you can't separate them because they're designed together.

**Troeung's "Refugee Lifeworlds"**: The lived knowledge, memory, community, and political consciousness refugees carry that exceeds state categories. The state targets these for erasure through charitable pity that demands gratitude and psychological frameworks that pathologize political analysis.

**Zahzah's "Warcare Economies"**: The material fusion of humanitarian programming and counterinsurgency. Care is the mechanism through which political compliance is extracted and dissent is criminalized.

Both scholars show: **The state doesn't want to help refugees—it wants to manage the populations its wars created while preventing them from politically analyzing their displacement.**

---

## How Refugees Resist (The Hope Part)

Despite everything, refugees maintain what Troeung calls "illegible lifeworlds"—spaces the state can't fully penetrate:

- **Autonomous organizing**: San Diego Muslim and African communities collectively decide which services to trust, refuse CVE facilitators at events, change where they access resources to avoid surveillance
- **Memory work**: Preserving first-person accounts of genocide, war, and displacement that contradict state rescue narratives
- **Political consciousness**: Maintaining analysis of U.S. imperialism, connecting refugee struggles to abolitionist and anti-war movements, organizing against ongoing military intervention
- **Community solidarity**: Mutual aid networks, healing practices, and care systems that exist independently of humanitarian institutions

Troeung: "We need to look at it, think about it, mourn it, be angry about it, and at least live in it for a little while." Simply refusing to forget—and refusing the state's version of events—is resistance.

## So What the Fuck Is the Alternative?

Both scholars point toward **abolitionist frameworks** that don't reform humanitarian systems but build alternatives:

### Care Redefined:

1. **Solidarity not charity**: Refugees are survivors of Western imperial violence, not charity cases. Support means accountability and reparations, not benevolent rescue.

2. **Opacity as a right**: Refugees don't owe total transparency about their lives, traumas, or politics to institutions. Illegibility is protection.

3. **Material support without conditions**: Housing, healthcare, education with no surveillance, no assimilation requirements, no English-only rules, no patriotism tests.

4. **Centering refugee political knowledge**: Treat refugees as the primary analysts of war, genocide, and empire—not as victims needing management.

5. **Reparations from countries that caused displacement**: The U.S. owes Cambodian, Vietnamese, Somali, Iraqi, Syrian, Palestinian, and Afghan refugees massive compensation for destruction—not aid, but restitution.

6. **Stop making refugees**: Demilitarize. End occupations. Stop drone strikes. Divest from Israel. Cut the $886 billion Pentagon budget. The most meaningful care is to stop producing displacement.

### The Revolutionary Endpoint:

"Care, truly redefined, looks less like humanitarian programs and more like revolution: the dismantling of imperial warfare, border regimes, and racial capitalism, and the building of worlds where displacement doesn't happen because everyone has what they need to flourish where they are—or move freely if they choose."

## Answer to Your Question: Is Unbiased Care Possible?

**No. Not within current structures.**

Humanitarian care is structurally bound to:
- Military and security state funding
- Depoliticization of displacement (erasing imperial causation)
- Exploitation of material vulnerability for political control
- Assimilation demands that target political consciousness

This isn't a failure of implementation. It's the design.

Refugees don't need better humanitarian programs. They need the systems producing displacement to be dismantled. They need resources and power to care for themselves on their own terms. They need those of us in the imperial core to stop confusing our complicity with compassion.

**Real care means ending the wars, not managing their survivors.**
