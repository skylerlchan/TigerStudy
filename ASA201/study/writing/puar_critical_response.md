# Critical Engagement with Puar's "Homonationalism and Biopolitics"

## Introduction: Appreciating Before Critiquing

Puar's intervention is undeniably important—she raises crucial questions about complicity, challenges the assumed radicality of queerness, and centers questions of race and empire that queer theory often sidelines. However, several aspects of her theoretical framework warrant closer scrutiny. This critique proceeds from a place of taking her work seriously enough to identify its limitations.

## 1. The Theoretical Density Problem: Who Can Access This?

### The Issue

Puar's prose is extraordinarily dense, laden with theoretical jargon (biopolitics, assemblages, regulatory apparatus, valorization of life, etc.) that presumes extensive familiarity with Foucault, Agamben, Deleuze, and poststructuralist theory more broadly.

### The Critique

**For whom is this book written?** If the goal is to challenge the violence of homonationalism and call queer movements to accountability, shouldn't the text be accessible to activists, organizers, and community members most affected by these dynamics? The irony is that Puar critiques elite cosmopolitan queerness while writing in a register that requires elite academic training to parse.

Moreover, the very communities she aims to defend—queer Muslims, queer immigrants, working-class queers of color—are least likely to have access to the theoretical literacy required to engage with this text. This raises questions about **epistemic gatekeeping**: does the theoretical apparatus itself reproduce the exclusions Puar critiques?

### The Counter-Argument

One might argue that theoretical sophistication is necessary to dismantle deeply entrenched power structures, or that accessibility shouldn't require "dumbing down" complex ideas. But surely there's a middle ground between theoretical rigor and making oneself intelligible to non-specialists.

## 2. The Paralysis Problem: If Everything Is Regulatory, What Do We Do?

### The Issue

Puar demonstrates that queerness operates as a regulatory formation even through its claims to transgression. Intersectionality can become regulatory. Liberal inclusion strengthens whiteness. Even queer of color critique risks reproducing its own norms.

### The Critique

**This leaves us where, exactly?** If every critical framework is potentially regulatory, if every act of resistance can be complicit, if even acknowledging complicity "signals not the failure" but just more complexity—what viable political action remains?

Puar's framework risks producing **political paralysis**. If we can't celebrate transgression (it's regulatory), can't pursue inclusion (it's violent), can't rely on intersectionality (it has blind spots), and can't position queer of color organizing as beyond reproach (it has conservative elements)—what's left?

She gestures toward "resisting resistance" and "sitting with contradiction," but these feel more like theoretical postures than actionable politics. The question remains: **How do we organize, mobilize, and build movements if every move is always-already complicit?**

### The Stakes

This isn't just academic handwringing. Activists need frameworks that help them navigate complicity *and* act. Puar's critique is so totalizing that it risks immobilizing the very movements she wants to see become more accountable.

## 3. The Generalization Problem: All Queers? All the Time?

### The Issue

Puar makes sweeping claims about "queerness," "queer liberalism," "queer secularity," and "homonationalism" that flatten significant differences across contexts, geographies, and communities.

### The Critique

**Which queers?** When Puar writes about "the queer liberal imaginary" or "queer secularity," who exactly is she describing? The claims often slide between:
- Mainstream LGBT organizations (HRC, GLAAD)
- Queer theory as an academic discipline
- Individual queer subjects
- "Queerness" as an abstract formation

This **lack of specificity** is problematic. The homonationalism of Log Cabin Republicans is not the same as the gay liberalism of HRC is not the same as the queer cosmopolitanism of graduate students is not the same as the everyday negotiations of working-class queer POC.

**Empirical questions**:
- How prevalent is homonationalism? Is it dominant or marginal within LGBTQ movements?
- Are most queer people actually secular? Or is this an elite phenomenon being universalized?
- What percentage of queer organizing actually participates in nationalist projects vs. challenging them?

Puar provides some examples (OutRage!, World Pride in Jerusalem) but then extrapolates to make claims about "queerness" writ large. The gap between her specific examples and her generalizations is significant.

### The Risk

By painting with such a broad brush, Puar may **obscure actually-existing queer resistance** that doesn't fit her framework. What about:
- Queer immigrant rights organizing?
- Black queer abolition movements?
- Palestinian queer activism she briefly mentions?
- Indigenous two-spirit organizing?
- Trans justice movements?

Do these all reproduce homonationalism? Are they all implicated in the ascendancy of whiteness? Or might Puar's framework itself be too rigid to account for their complexity?

## 4. The Agency Problem: Do Queer Muslims Get to Speak?

### The Issue

Puar argues that queer secularity denies agency to queer Muslims, positioning them as either repressed or heroically escaped from Islam. This is an important intervention.

### The Critique

But there's a subtle tension here: **Does Puar's framework itself circumscribe queer Muslim agency?**

She tells us that queer Muslims who lean on religious community post-9/11 are exercising agency against secular norms. That queer Muslims who claim "I am a homosexual also" are challenging Orientalist binaries. That the photographer staging "terrorist drag" disrupts queer liberal imaginaries.

But **who gets to decide what counts as agentic?** Puar's readings are persuasive, but they're still *her* readings. The danger is that queer Muslims become theoretical objects—useful for demonstrating the violence of queer secularity—rather than subjects with their own complex, potentially contradictory politics.

**What if some queer Muslims DO experience Islam as repressive?** What if some DO embrace secular queerness? Does Puar's framework allow for that without reading them as victims of false consciousness?

### The Broader Question

This touches on a perennial problem in postcolonial and critical race theory: **How do we critique oppressive discourses without denying the agency of those who accept or navigate those discourses?** Puar is right that queer secularity forecloses certain possibilities, but her counter-narrative risks foreclosing others.

## 5. The Intersectionality Critique: What's the Alternative?

### The Issue

Puar critiques intersectionality for failing to account for simultaneity, for potentially becoming regulatory, for holding queer of color subjects to impossible standards.

### The Critique

**Okay, but then what?** Puar invokes Deleuze and Guattari's "assemblage" as an alternative, but this remains underdeveloped in the introduction. Assemblage theory can feel:
- **Abstract**: What does it mean concretely to think through assemblages vs. intersections?
- **Disconnected from organizing**: Intersectionality, whatever its limits, has been useful for activists. What's the political utility of assemblage?
- **Difficult to operationalize**: How do we analyze power through assemblages in ways that guide action?

Moreover, Puar's critique of intersectionality sometimes feels like a straw person. She writes:

> "Queer of color and queer immigrant communities (not to mention queer of color critique) are always beyond reproach, an untenable position."

**But who actually holds this position?** Most intersectional scholars are quite attentive to intra-community tensions, power dynamics within marginalized groups, and the impossibility of pure resistance. Cathy Cohen, whom Puar cites approvingly, certainly doesn't position queer of color communities as beyond critique.

### The Question

Is Puar critiquing **intersectionality as practiced** or **a caricature of intersectionality**? And if assemblage is the answer, we need more development of how it functions analytically and politically.

## 6. The Historical Specificity Problem: Is This Really New?

### The Issue

Puar frames homonationalism as emerging in the post-9/11 moment, tied specifically to the War on Terror and U.S. sexual exceptionalism.

### The Critique

**But is this dynamic actually novel?** Couldn't we find earlier moments where:
- Certain homosexual subjects were incorporated into nationalist projects?
- Queerness was deployed to mark civilizational difference?
- Sexual politics were used to justify imperialism?

For example:
- **Colonial history**: European colonizers often positioned themselves as sexually enlightened vs. "primitive" or "perverse" colonized sexualities
- **Cold War**: The U.S. positioned itself as more sexually modern than the Soviet Union
- **19th century**: Sexology and the "invention" of the homosexual were already tied to race science and imperial projects

**If homonationalism has deeper roots**, then Puar's focus on the post-9/11 moment might be overstated. The mechanisms she describes—incorporation of some queers, Orientalization of others, sexual exceptionalism—may be **longstanding features of modernity** rather than products of the War on Terror specifically.

This matters because it affects **strategy**. If homonationalism is tied specifically to post-9/11 politics, then changing those conditions might dismantle it. If it's built into western modernity itself, we're facing a much more intractable problem.

## 7. The Binary Problem: Does Puar Reproduce What She Critiques?

### The Issue

Puar critiques binary thinking throughout—assimilation vs. transgression, secular vs. religious, resistance vs. complicity.

### The Critique

Yet her own framework sometimes relies on binaries:
- **"Good" vs. "bad" queers**: She distinguishes between regulatory queer liberalism and... what? A more accountable queer politics she never fully articulates
- **Liberal vs. radical**: Her critique targets liberalism, but what's the alternative? Is she presuming a radical position that's somehow exempt from her critique?
- **U.S./Western vs. non-Western**: While she's attentive to how the West constructs the East, there's still a risk of reproducing geographic binaries

Moreover, her call to "resist resistance" and "sit with contradiction" can feel like a privileged theoretical position. **Who has the luxury of not resisting?** For people facing violence, deportation, or premature death, "sitting with contradiction" may not be an option.

### The Performative Contradiction

If Puar is right that all frameworks become regulatory, **isn't her own framework regulatory?** Doesn't it create its own ideal subject (the self-reflexive scholar aware of complicity) against which others are measured? Doesn't it risk becoming a new orthodoxy in queer studies?

## 8. The Empirical Gap: Where's the Evidence?

### The Issue

Puar makes sweeping theoretical claims about queerness, biopolitics, and empire but provides limited empirical grounding.

### The Critique

**What's the evidence for these claims?** She offers:
- A photograph (Poulomi Desai's "I Am a Homosexual Also")
- References to OutRage! and World Pride
- Quotes from a few queer Muslims post-9/11
- Abstract theoretical argumentation

But to substantiate claims about how "queerness operates as a regulatory script" or how "the ascendancy of whiteness" functions through queer inclusion, we'd need:
- **Systematic analysis** of LGBTQ organizations' rhetoric and practices
- **Ethnographic work** with queer communities navigating these dynamics
- **Historical research** tracing the emergence of homonationalist discourses
- **Discourse analysis** of media, policy, popular culture

The introduction promises a book-length treatment, but even here, the theoretical apparatus outstrips the empirical foundation. This is a common problem in high theory—the concepts become so abstract they float free of the material conditions they're meant to explain.

### The Consequences

Without robust empirical work, Puar's claims risk becoming:
- **Unfalsifiable**: How would we know if homonationalism wasn't dominant? What would count as evidence against her claims?
- **Overgeneralized**: Maybe her analysis applies to specific elite formations but not to queer life more broadly
- **Ahistorical**: Without tracking how these dynamics shift over time, we can't assess causality or change

## 9. The Practical Application Problem: Now What?

### The Issue

Puar's analysis is devastating, but it's unclear what it enables politically.

### The Critique

**If you're a queer activist after reading Puar, what do you do differently?** Her framework helps us identify problems:
- Don't assume queerness is automatically radical
- Be aware of how LGBTQ rights can be weaponized for imperialism
- Recognize that "progress" narratives can strengthen white supremacy
- Attend to who's excluded from queer inclusion

But **what affirmative politics emerge?** Puar gestures toward:
- "Resisting resistance"
- "Sitting with contradiction"
- "Greater room for self-reflection"
- Avoiding "pure spaces" of critique

These are valuable intellectual orientations, but they don't translate easily into **organizing strategy**. How do you build a movement on "resist resistance"? How do you mobilize people around "acknowledge your complicity"?

### The Risk of Quietism

There's a danger that Puar's critique, taken to its logical conclusion, produces **political withdrawal**. If all action is potentially complicit, if all frameworks become regulatory, if resistance itself must be resisted—why organize at all?

This isn't Puar's intention, but it's a potential effect of her rhetorical strategy. The critique is so powerful that it can overshadow any possibility of generative politics.

## 10. The Question of Audience: Who Benefits From This Critique?

### The Issue

Finally, we might ask: **Who is served by Puar's analysis?**

### The Critique

The best-case scenario: queer movements become more self-critical, attentive to their implication in imperialism and white supremacy, and work to build more genuinely liberatory politics.

But there are risks:
- **Fuel for the right**: Could anti-LGBTQ forces weaponize Puar's critique? "See, even queer scholars admit LGBTQ rights are just imperialism!"
- **Perfectionism paralysis**: Does the call for constant self-interrogation create impossible standards that prevent action?
- **Academic gatekeeping**: Does this framework primarily serve to demonstrate theoretical sophistication within the academy rather than enable movement building?
- **Missing the bigger picture**: While queer movements have problems, are they really the primary drivers of U.S. imperialism? Or is Puar asking them to carry disproportionate responsibility for structural violence?

### The Question of Scale

Yes, homonationalism is real. Yes, some LGBTQ organizations are complicit with imperialism. But **how significant is this compared to other drivers of U.S. empire?** Military-industrial complex, oil interests, geopolitical strategy, economic extraction—these seem far more determinative than whether HRC supports gay rights abroad.

**Is Puar asking queer movements to bear a burden of critique that should be more widely distributed?**

## Conclusion: The Value of Critical Engagement

None of these critiques invalidate Puar's project. Her intervention remains crucial, and these questions are productive precisely because her work is substantial enough to merit serious engagement.

The strongest critique might be this: **Puar is brilliant at diagnosis but weaker on prescription.** She shows us what's wrong—and she's persuasive—but leaves us with few tools for building something better.

A truly generative engagement with Puar would:
1. Take seriously her critique of queer liberalism and homonationalism
2. Demand empirical grounding for sweeping theoretical claims
3. Ask what affirmative politics her framework enables
4. Insist on accessibility without sacrificing complexity
5. Refuse both uncritical celebration and paralyzed self-flagellation

The goal isn't to dismiss Puar but to **think with and beyond her**—to use her critique as a starting point while recognizing its limitations. That's what it means to take intellectual work seriously: not worship, not rejection, but critical engagement in service of better theory and better practice.

## Questions for Discussion

1. **Is Puar's theoretical density a bug or a feature?** Does complexity require complexity, or is there a politics to accessibility?

2. **How do we organize in the face of inevitable complicity?** What does accountability without paralysis look like?

3. **Are Puar's claims about queerness overgeneralized?** How do we balance critique of dominant formations with attention to resistant practices?

4. **What would "non-regulatory queerness" even look like?** Or is Puar saying that's an impossible fantasy?

5. **How do assemblages differ from intersections in practice?** Is this a meaningful theoretical distinction or academic jargon-swapping?

6. **Who gets to speak about queer Muslim agency?** How do we avoid both Orientalist erasure and theoretical ventriloquism?

7. **Is homonationalism really ascendant, or is Puar focusing on elite formations that don't represent most queer organizing?**

8. **Does Puar's critique provide ammunition for anti-LGBTQ politics?** Should we worry about that? Does it matter?

9. **What's the relationship between Puar's academic work and movement organizing?** Should theory serve movements, or is that the wrong question?

10. **If you were to rewrite this introduction with Puar's insights but more accessible prose and concrete examples, what would you prioritize?**
