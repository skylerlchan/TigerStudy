# ASA 201: Introduction to Asian American Culture & Difference

**Date:** 2026-04-21


## Summary

### Haunani-Kay Trask’s Theoretical Framework

- Indigenous sovereignty vs. settler colonialism analysis
- Critiques “local” identity as obscuring Native Hawaiian claims
- Local = born/raised in Hawaii but not indigenous- Asian Americans use “local” to feel better about settler status- Masks fact they’re on stolen indigenous land
- Historical context: 1898 illegal US annexation of Hawaiian Kingdom
- Queen Liliuokalani imprisoned for resisting white business interests- Strategic military location for US Pacific operations

### Trask’s Critique of Asian American Political Power

- Japanese American Citizens League vs. Local Japanese Women for Justice
- JACL criticized for not supporting indigenous sovereignty- LJWJ praised for solidarity with Native Hawaiians
- Asian Americans gained political power through statehood push
- Used by mainland US as “multiracial paradise” narrative- Convenient tool for racial liberalism messaging
- Current demographics complicate sovereignty claims
- Asians largest population group in Hawaii- Native Hawaiians now minority on their own land

### 1993 Speech Analysis - Queen Liliuokalani’s Resistance

- “We are not Americans” repeated 6+ times
- Calls Americans “enemies” who “banned our language, took our land”
- Advocates abandoning Christianity as “white man’s religion”
- Criticizes mixed-race Hawaiian politicians for assimilating
- Militant tone: “I will fight to the death”
- Emphasizes sovereignty as political power, not just feeling

### Class Discussion on Activism Effectiveness

- Debate: Militant vs. peaceful approaches
- Trask’s aggressive stance may alienate potential allies- But peaceful approaches often lead to pacification- “Sovereignty is not a feeling, it’s political power”
- Tourism industry complications
- Major income source but profits go to US corporations- 2023 Maui wildfires caused by water diverted to resorts/military- New green taxes on tourists to fund conservation

### Settler Allyship and Mixed-Race Identity

- Trask’s solution: Asian Americans should support, not lead sovereignty efforts
- Candace Fujikani example: criticized by Trask, later became settler ally
- Mixed-race populations complicate binary settler/indigenous framework
- International law precedent for indigenous rights claims
- Comparison to other colonial contexts (Okinawa, Hong Kong, Palestine)

### Ongoing Challenges and Contradictions

- Infrastructure dependence creates practical sovereignty barriers
- Inequality exists in all systems - question of who controls it
- US strategic interests make independence unlikely
- Local identity remains contested term
- Need for concrete solutions beyond theoretical frameworks
