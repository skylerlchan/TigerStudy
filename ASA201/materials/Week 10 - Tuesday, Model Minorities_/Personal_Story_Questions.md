# Questions Connecting Personal Chicago Economics Background to Critique

## Your Narrative Setup
- Personal story: dad + Princeton strikes 1997
- Background in Chicago school economics (first principles thinking)
- Understanding WHY Chicago economists extrapolate phenomena into evidence
- Using insider knowledge to both appreciate AND critique the methodology

---

## Option 1: The Insider Question
**"As someone trained in Chicago school economics, I understand the appeal of first principles thinking - it feels rigorous, scientific, neutral. But if that same methodology led Becker and Schultz to turn Asian families into economic models that justified cutting welfare, when does 'rigorous' thinking become weaponized thinking?"**

*Why this works:*
- Uses YOUR voice and background
- Shows you understand the methodology from inside
- The question is: when does rigor become racism?
- Acknowledges appeal but shows danger
- Personal stakes: you're implicated in this tradition
- Forces class to think about methodology itself as political

---

## Option 2: The First Principles Problem
**"Chicago economics teaches us to build from first principles and extrapolate outward. But Nadal shows Becker doing exactly that - taking Asian educational success as a 'principle' and building human capital theory around it. If the first principles themselves are cherry-picked to serve an ideology, is the rigor real or just performance?"**

*Why this works:*
- Directly names the Chicago school methodology
- Shows you understand HOW they built the theory (extrapolation from principles)
- The critique: garbage in, garbage out
- Cherry-picked examples = ideological foundations disguised as neutral principles
- Gets at the PROCESS of theory-building as political
- Sophisticated because you're questioning the methodology itself

---

## Option 3: The Legitimacy Question (Most Personal)
**"Growing up around Chicago school thinking, I was taught that economic theory is more legitimate when it's built on observable patterns and first principles. But what happens when those 'observable patterns' are racialized examples? Does using Asian success as your foundation make the theory more scientific - or does it just make the racism harder to see?"**

*Why this works:*
- Most personal - "I was taught"
- Shows your stakes in this
- Questions what you were taught
- Gets at legitimacy/authority of "science"
- The paradox: using real examples makes it look more rigorous, but embeds racism deeper
- Shows intellectual honesty: you're questioning your own training
- Will resonate with anyone else who's internalized these frameworks

---

## Option 4: The Synthesis Question (Connects Both Articles)
**"Hayakawa weaponized the model minority myth through politics - that's visible and we can fight it. But Becker and Schultz embedded it into economic theory using the methodology I was trained in - first principles, rigorous extrapolation. If the second one feels more 'legitimate' because it's academic, does that make it more dangerous?"**

*Why this works:*
- Ties both articles together
- Personal: "methodology I was trained in"
- Shows the difference: political (visible) vs academic (invisible)
- Gets at legitimacy/authority of science
- Implicit critique of your own training
- Opens debate: what's more dangerous?

---

## RECOMMENDATION: Option 3

**Go with Option 3** because:
1. Most personal and honest
2. Shows you're interrogating what you were taught
3. Gets at the core problem: using racialized examples makes racism look scientific
4. "Growing up around Chicago school thinking" - establishes your insider status
5. Vulnerable but analytical
6. Will make people lean in because you're putting your own background on the line

## Alternative: Option 2 if you want more technical

Use **Option 2** if you want to sound more technical/methodological and less personal. It's equally strong but more abstract.
