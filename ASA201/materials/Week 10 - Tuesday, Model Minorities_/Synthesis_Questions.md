# Synthesis Questions: Tying Both Articles Together

## TEACHER'S FRAMING (For Context)
The teacher is asking: Is distinguishing between "conservatives" and "model minority" just academic? What's the point if their political goals align? Why make this distinction in present-day Asian American politics?

---

## Option 1: Responding Directly to Teacher's Question
**"If Hayakawa shows us that being 'model minority' and being 'conservative' can serve the same political project, but Nadal shows us that the model minority myth gets embedded in supposedly neutral economic science - doesn't that suggest the 'model minority' category is actually MORE dangerous because it's less visible than explicit conservatism?"**

*Why this works:*
- Directly engages the teacher's prompt about the distinction
- Argues the distinction DOES matter - model minority is harder to see/fight
- Synthesizes both readings perfectly
- Hayakawa = visible conservative
- Human capital theory = invisible model minority logic embedded in "science"
- Shows sophistication: you're saying the distinction matters because of institutional embedding
- Clear position but opens debate

---

## Option 2: The Institutional Reach Question
**"Both readings show the model minority myth working through institutions - Hayakawa through politics, human capital theory through economics. If 'conservative' describes individual ideology but 'model minority' describes structural logic, does fighting one require different strategies than fighting the other?"**

*Why this works:*
- Distinguishes individual vs structural
- Directly responds to "why make the distinction"
- Answer: because you FIGHT them differently
- You can vote out Hayakawa, but how do you remove ideas from Nobel Prize-winning economics?
- Moves to praxis/action
- Shows you understand levels of analysis

---

## Option 3: The Coalition Question (Most Aligned with Readings)
**"If we collapse 'conservative' and 'model minority' into the same category, do we lose the ability to build coalitions with Asian Americans who've internalized the myth but aren't ideologically conservative? Nadal shows how deeply embedded this logic is - can we afford to write off everyone touched by it?"**

*Why this works:*
- Gets at strategic politics of coalition-building
- Engages teacher's question: "what's the point of the distinction?"
- Answer: coalition strategy requires nuance
- Someone influenced by human capital theory ≠ Hayakawa actively crushing activism
- Shows you understand political organizing
- Connects to broader Asian American Studies questions about solidarity
- Most generous/hopeful framing

---

## RECOMMENDATION: Option 2

**Go with Option 2** because:
1. Directly answers teacher's "why distinguish?" question
2. Clear answer: individual vs structural = different fights
3. Synthesizes both readings perfectly
4. Shows analytical sophistication (levels of analysis)
5. Moves toward action/strategy
6. Not too generous (Option 3) or too harsh (Option 1)
7. Opens good debate about how to actually fight this

## Alternative: Option 3 if you want to be generous

Use **Option 3** if the class leans toward organizing/activism and you want to argue for coalition-building over purity politics. This is the most politically sophisticated answer but might be seen as too soft on complicity.
