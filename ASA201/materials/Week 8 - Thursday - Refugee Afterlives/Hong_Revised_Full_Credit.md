## Revised Version for Full Credit (250 words)

The expansive thinking about the Korean War exposes how U.S. imperialism creates a self-perpetuating scaffolding that engineers unending crisis to justify its existence, giving the illusion of progress at home. Hong conceptualizes the war as a "cognitive structure" and "structure of feeling"—not just military bases but a systematic way of perceiving and experiencing reality that differs radically by geography (601). She argues U.S. involvement has become "a formidable, crisis-generating, self-perpetuating institutional architecture that, in the post–cold war era, has provided the United States with the rationale for retaining a forward-deployment infrastructure in Northeast Asia" (600). This reveals that the war's irresolution isn't diplomatic failure but structural necessity: peace would dismantle the scaffolding justifying military spending, bases, and intervention.

The war is "out of joint" temporally through what Hong calls discrepant experiences of "wartime": the war is "cognitively mapped as limited in one setting yet total in another, forgotten in one yet unforgettable in another" (599). Americans experience it as past history (enabling progress narratives), while Koreans exist in suspended time under permanent military threat. Spatially, it's deterritorialized through "distant control"—technology enabling "instant destruction of others" without Americans confronting consequences, rendering Korean lives "immaterial in the sense of readiness for obliteration at any moment" (599).

This framework reveals how imperialism operates through "inversion of cause and effect": defensive responses to U.S. aggression are reframed as "provocations" justifying more intervention, ensuring war never ends (600). For refugees and those in Korea, this cognitive structure creates "group-differentiated vulnerability to premature death"—their lives become structurally disposable to maintain imperial architecture (602-603). Thinking expansively reveals that U.S. imperialism requires not just military force but cognitive frameworks that make permanent crisis appear natural and certain lives expendable.
