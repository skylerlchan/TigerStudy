The expansive thinking about the Korean War exposes how U.S. imperialism creates a self-perpetuating scaffolding that engineers unending crisis to justify its existence, which gives the illusion of progress at home. Reframing the war as existing in multiple temporalities, Hong reviews how Americans are frozen in the 1950s, believing the war has long ended, while in Korea, it is a perpetual present that keeps families separated and ensures a continuous threat of violence. Geographically, the war exceeds the Korean peninsula, allowing the U.S. to place bases surrounding the Pacific, hence creating a stream of borders, bases, and refugee camps. Therefore, Hong argues that the Korean War has fostered "a formidable, crisis-generating, self-perpetuating, institutional architecture." The war's unending nature is not a diplomatic failure but an intentional act of maintaining U.S. imperialism.

Hong's argument is further strengthened by contrasting how Koreans and Americans view the war today. She notes that the war is "cognitively mapped as limited in one setting yet total in another, forgotten in one yet unforgettable in another." This disconnect reveals America strategically forgetting the destruction it caused, as well as the current fear it imposes. Eventually, this evolves into what Hong calls an "inversion of cause and effect that enables its present-day consequences." This allows the U.S. to "defensively" respond to threats, even when those threats were caused by its initial military presence (e.g., North Korea's nuclear weapons). For refugees and those in Korea, this scaffolding creates what scholar Ruth Wilson Gilmore defines as a "group-differentiated vulnerability to premature death," in which their lives are treated as disposable to the engineered crisis of the U.S. military presence. This cognitive framework normalizes perpetual war which allows for the continued destruction of a so-called expendable population.
