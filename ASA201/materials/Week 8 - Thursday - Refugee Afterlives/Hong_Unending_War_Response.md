# Hong - "The Unending Korean War" Discussion Response

## Article Summary

Christine Hong's "The Unending Korean War" argues that the Korean War is not a historical event confined to 1950-53, but an ongoing "cognitive structure" and "structure of feeling" that continues to shape US imperialism and affect lives across the Pacific. Hong examines how the war's technical non-conclusion (armistice, not peace treaty) creates a permanent state of "wartime" that operates out of joint in both time and space.

**Key arguments:**

1. **Temporal Disjunction (Wartime)**: The war creates "wartime" - a suspension of normal time where permanent military presence is justified. In the US, the war is forgotten; in Korea, it's ever-present. This temporal split allows the US to experience "progress" at home while perpetuating devastation abroad.

2. **Spatial Disjunction (Geography)**: The war's effects are "deterritorialized" - violence happens in Korea, but the justifications and consequences are felt globally. The DMZ militarization affects global geopolitics, not just the peninsula.

3. **Imperial Infrastructure**: The "unending" nature of the war serves US imperial interests by justifying: the military-industrial complex, permanent overseas bases, perpetual war economy, and interventionist foreign policy. The war becomes a "formidable, crisis-generating, self-perpetuating institutional architecture."

4. **Racialized Violence**: Hong connects Korean War violence to domestic racial violence in the US, showing how the "collateralization of nonwhite life" abroad is linked to white supremacy at home. The "securitized logic of the target" makes certain (racialized) populations acceptable casualties.

5. **Inversion of Cause and Effect**: Present-day "provocations" (like North Korean nuclear development) are used to retroactively justify past US violence, perpetuating the war's irresolution and enabling continued military presence.

## Sample Response Structure

### Opening: Establish the "Unending" Framework

Begin by explaining what Hong means by the Korean War being "unending" and why this matters:

**Quote to use:**
> "This special issue of *positions: asia critique*, 'The Unending Korean War,' undertakes as its focus the *longue durée* of the unending Korean War as a protean structure, at once generative and destructive, whose formations and deformations, impasses and costs, ruptures and obfuscations, can be traced on both sides of the North Pacific. Naturalized as 'forgotten' in the United States yet seared into national consciousness in both Koreas, the Korean War, as a differentiated structure of feeling, perception, memory, knowledge, and historical ruin, has persisted some six decades after the signing of the Armistice Agreement." (598)

**Analysis:** This quote reveals that the Korean War functions as a "protean structure" - constantly shifting yet persistent - that simultaneously produces effects (generative) and destroys lives (destructive). The crucial insight is the phrase "differentiated structure of feeling, perception, memory, knowledge, and historical ruin." Hong, drawing on Raymond Williams, shows that the war isn't just a set of past events but an ongoing way of experiencing reality that differs radically based on geography. For Americans, the war is "forgotten" - erased from collective memory and public discourse. But for Koreans on both sides of the DMZ, the war is "seared into national consciousness" - an ever-present reality that structures daily life, politics, family separation, and national identity. This temporal and spatial splitting is the foundation of US imperialism: the ability to wage perpetual war elsewhere while experiencing "peacetime" at home. The war's "unending" nature isn't an accident or failure of diplomacy; it's a *feature* of imperial power that enables permanent US military presence in Northeast Asia.

---

### Body Paragraph 1: Temporal Disjunction - "Wartime" as Imperial Tool

Explore how the war's suspension in time serves US imperial interests:

**Quote to use:**
> "The uneven terrain of the Korean War and its violence, with the war cognitively mapped as limited in one setting yet total in another, forgotten in one yet unforgettable in another, has ensured discrepant experiences of what Mary Dudziak has called 'wartime,' in which permanent war leads to 'suspension of[] time itself.' The alienated technological nature of US military intervention—the disjunction between what Chow calls 'distant control' or the one hand, and 'instant destruction of others,' on the other—meant that 'for the ordinary people of Korea and Vietnam in the 1950s and 1960s . . . life became more and more precarious—immaterial in the sense of readiness for obliteration at any moment.'" (599)

**Analysis:** This passage exposes the racialized temporality of US imperialism. The concept of "wartime" - borrowed from legal scholar Mary Dudziak - reveals how permanent war for some populations coexists with peacetime for others, with time itself becoming "suspended" under military occupation. Hong identifies two contradictory temporalities operating simultaneously: in the US, there's the "temporality of progress and order" (capitalist modernity, technological advancement, democratic freedoms), while in Korea there's the "temporality of devastation and chaos" (perpetual military threat, economic extraction, authoritarian rule justified by security).

What's jaw-dropping here is the phrase "immaterial in the sense of readiness for obliteration at any moment." Hong reveals that US military technology creates a terrifying form of precarity where Korean and Vietnamese lives literally don't matter - they're rendered "immaterial," ready to be obliterated instantly through "distant control" (aerial bombing, drone strikes, nuclear threats) without Americans ever having to confront the human consequences. This is the necropolitics of empire: the power to determine who lives in "time" (with futures, development, progress) and who lives in "wartime" (in suspended animation, waiting for destruction). The Korean War's non-conclusion *requires* this temporal split because ending the war would mean acknowledging the humanity and sovereignty of North Korea, which would undermine the entire justification for US military presence in Asia.

---

### Body Paragraph 2: Spatial Disjunction - The "Deterritorialized" Violence

Examine how the war's effects transcend geographic boundaries:

**Quote to use:**
> "Troublingly, the insulated temporality of the Korean War in the United States 'effect[s] a gulf and hypocrisy that shatter[s] any rational pattern of cause and effect.' Indeed, central to the Korean War's disquieting perdurance is an inversion of cause and effect that enables its present-day consequences, for example, in North Korea's push in the past half-decade toward nuclear self-defense, to be decontextualized as 'provocations' that call out for potentially catastrophic preemptive violence. This causal fallacy particular to US interpretations of North Korea—of misreading effects as causes—not coincidentally justifies the deferral of peace, thus perpetuating the Korean War's irresolution. As a mode of imperial consciousness, the disavowal that immorality, even of an obscene war, the unending Korean War serves as the necessary condition for a US militarized presence in Northeast Asia." (600)

**Analysis:** Hong identifies a devastating logical operation at the heart of US imperialism: the inversion of cause and effect. North Korea's nuclear weapons program - a defensive response to six decades of US threats, military exercises, and sanctions - is reframed as an unprovoked "provocation" that justifies *more* US military aggression. This rhetorical move erases history entirely. The massive US aerial bombing campaign that killed 20% of North Korea's population, the ongoing sanctions, the annual war games simulating invasion - all of this disappears, and we're left with the image of North Korea as an irrational, aggressive state that threatens peace.

But here's what's truly jaw-dropping: Hong reveals that this isn't a mistake or propaganda failure - it's "a mode of imperial consciousness," a systematic way of perceiving the world that makes empire possible. The phrase "disavowal that immorality, even of an obscene war" is crucial. The US *cannot* acknowledge the immorality of the Korean War because doing so would delegitimize the entire architecture of military bases, defense budgets, and interventionist policies built upon it. The war's "irresolution" - its never-ending status - is therefore "the necessary condition" for US empire in Asia. Peace would mean withdrawal; withdrawal would mean reduced global power. So the war must remain perpetually "unfinished," with North Korea perpetually cast as a threat, regardless of historical reality. This spatial logic allows violence to be "deterritorialized" - to happen in Korea while its justifications and benefits accrue in Washington, creating what Chalmers Johnson called "blowback" that's "held in abeyance for the imperial present, not yet come full circle."

---

### Body Paragraph 3: Effects on Refugees and Those in Korea - Racialized "Collateralization"

Connect to the continuing impacts on refugees and populations living in the war's wake:

**Quote to use:**
> "In the powerful words of the CRC petition: 'We, Negro petitioners whose communities have been laid waste, whose homes have been burned and looted, whose children have been killed, whose women have been raped have noted with peculiar horror that the genocidal doctrines and actions of the American white supremacists have already been exported to the peoples of Asia.' The terror of '[w]hite supremacy at home,' according to Patterson, 'makes for colored massacres abroad. Both reveal contempt for human life in a colored skin.'" (602)

**Additional quote:**
> "Insofar as the disjunction of wartime turns on the collateralization of human life within a securitized paradigm, the Korean War's foregrounding of the creation of preconditions for what Ruth Wilson Gilmore, in her incisive definition of racism, calls 'group-differentiated vulnerability to premature death.' If invaluable within US-based critical race studies as an analysis of state power toward racially profiled and criminalized subjects, Gilmore's structural analysis of racism merits critical application, beyond domestic US parameters, to the US war machine's relation toward its various 'targets' in the Middle East, Asia, Africa, and Latin America, be it through 'hard,' unequivocally violent means (war, counterinsurgency, 'low-intensity' conflict, drone strikes) or 'soft,' less spectacular methods (sanctions, embargoes, human rights demonization, psychological warfare)." (602-603)

**Analysis:** Hong makes an explosive connection between domestic white supremacy and imperial violence abroad by citing the 1951 "We Charge Genocide" petition, which argued that the racialized violence against Black Americans at home was directly linked to the racialized slaughter of Koreans abroad. This isn't metaphor - it's structural analysis. The same ideological apparatus that renders Black lives disposable in American cities renders Korean lives disposable in US military targeting. Both operate through what Ruth Wilson Gilmore defines as "group-differentiated vulnerability to premature death" - the systematic creation of conditions where certain racialized populations are more likely to die early.

For refugees and those still in Korea, this "collateralization of human life" means their bodies become *collateral damage* - acceptable losses in the pursuit of US "security." The war creates what Hong calls "biopolitical excess" or "war trash" - populations who fall outside the category of grievable life. Korean "war orphans" aren't just children who lost parents; they're "born out of the social and material conditions of U.S. militarism in Korea and South Korean nationalism," products of military prostitution, sexual violence, and economic devastation. Their adoption by American families is framed as humanitarian rescue, but Hong reveals it as another form of imperial extraction - even Korean children become resources to be extracted and repurposed for US benefit (specifically, to consolidate the image of America as benevolent).

For those still in Korea, North and South, the war's continuation means permanent militarization, divided families, authoritarian regimes justified by security threats, and economic subordination. The stunning insight is Hong's final point: "the restoration of dignity for all will be possible only after the unification of North and South Korea." As long as the war continues - as long as Korea remains divided - the conditions of racialized vulnerability persist. Reunification isn't just a political goal; it's a prerequisite for ending the "group-differentiated vulnerability to premature death" that the war has institutionalized for over six decades.

## Conclusion: The Stakes of "Unending"

Thinking about the Korean War as "unending" rather than historical reveals:

1. **US imperialism depends on perpetual war** - not as aberration but as structural necessity
2. **Temporal and spatial violence are racialized** - some populations live in "progress," others in "wartime"
3. **Historical amnesia enables ongoing violence** - forgetting the past justifies repeating it
4. **Imperial power operates through cognitive structures** - ways of seeing and knowing that make certain lives and deaths invisible
5. **Liberation requires historical reckoning** - peace and reunification demand confronting what the war has enabled

The "unending" framework shows us that US imperialism isn't just military bases and economic dominance - it's the power to structure time, space, memory, and knowledge itself in ways that render certain populations perpetually vulnerable to state violence while others remain insulated from its effects.

## Key Terms from Hong

- **Longue durée**: Long duration; historical time
- **Protean structure**: Constantly changing form while maintaining essence
- **Structure of feeling**: (Raymond Williams) Shared social experience at the edge of semantic availability
- **Wartime**: (Mary Dudziak) Permanent war that suspends normal time
- **Deterritorialized**: Effects that transcend geographic boundaries
- **Collateralization**: Rendering populations as acceptable casualties/collateral damage
- **Securitized logic of the target**: Racialized framework determining who is killable
- **Imperial consciousness**: Systematic worldview that makes empire appear natural/necessary
- **Blowback**: (Chalmers Johnson) Unintended consequences of imperial violence held in abeyance
