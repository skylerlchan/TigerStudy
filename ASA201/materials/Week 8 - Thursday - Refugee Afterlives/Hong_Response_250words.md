# Hong "Unending Korean War" - 250 Word Response

## Sample Response

Hong's framing of the Korean War as "unending" reveals how US imperialism operates through temporal and spatial dislocation that renders certain lives permanently precarious. She argues that the war functions as "a formidable, crisis-generating, self-perpetuating institutional architecture that, in the post–cold war era, has provided the United States with the rationale for retaining a forward-deployment infrastructure in Northeast Asia" (600). This isn't just about military bases—it's about how the war's irresolution creates a cognitive structure that justifies perpetual intervention.

The war is "out of joint" temporally because, as Hong observes, it creates "discrepant experiences of what Mary Dudziak has called 'wartime,' in which permanent war leads to 'suspension of[] time itself'" (599). In the US, the war is "forgotten"; in Korea, it's lived daily. This temporal split enables the US to experience progress at home while perpetuating devastation abroad. The war's never-ending status means Koreans exist in permanent "wartime"—under constant threat, militarization, and surveillance—while Americans live in peacetime prosperity built on this violence.

Spatially, the war operates through what Hong calls the "collateralization of nonwhite life" where violence happens in Korea but justifications circulate globally (602). The deterritorialized nature of US military power—"distant control" producing "instant destruction of others"—means Korean lives become "immaterial in the sense of readiness for obliteration at any moment" (599). For refugees, this creates what Hong terms "biopolitical excess" or populations rendered disposable by imperial violence. For those still in Korea, the war's continuation ensures "group-differentiated vulnerability to premature death," where reunification becomes impossible precisely because peace would delegitimize the entire US military infrastructure in Asia (602-603). The war's "unending" nature thus reveals that US imperialism requires perpetual crisis to sustain itself.

## Key Quotes with Context

### Quote 1: The War as Imperial Infrastructure (Most Important)

**Quote:**
> "The unending Korean War, with its massive US military and political investment, has mutated into a formidable, crisis-generating, self-perpetuating institutional architecture that, in the post–cold war era, has provided the United States with the rationale for retaining a forward-deployment infrastructure in Northeast Asia, one with global military and economic dimensions, and one that has shaped US strategic relations in the broader Asia-Pacific region and beyond." (600)

**Why this matters:** This is the thesis quote. Hong reveals the war isn't just unresolved—it's *designed* to be unending because this irresolution is what justifies the entire US military-industrial complex in Asia. The war has become infrastructure, not history. It's "crisis-generating" (it produces the threats it claims to respond to) and "self-perpetuating" (each crisis justifies its continuation). This proves your point about power dynamics: the US *needs* the war to never end because ending it would mean losing military presence, economic leverage, and imperial control in the region.

### Quote 2: Temporal Dislocation - "Wartime" as Racialized Violence

**Quote:**
> "The uneven terrain of the Korean War and its violence, with the war cognitively mapped as limited in one setting yet total in another, forgotten in one yet unforgettable in another, has ensured discrepant experiences of what Mary Dudziak has called 'wartime,' in which permanent war leads to 'suspension of[] time itself.' The alienated technological nature of US military intervention—the disjunction between what Chow calls 'distant control' on the one hand, and 'instant destruction of others,' on the other—meant that 'for the ordinary people of Korea and Vietnam in the 1950s and 1960s . . . life became more and more precarious—immaterial in the sense of readiness for obliteration at any moment.'" (599)

**Why this matters:** This shows the temporal "out of joint" aspect. Time itself operates differently for Americans (who forgot the war) vs. Koreans (who live in its suspension). The phrase "immaterial in the sense of readiness for obliteration at any moment" reveals the necropolitics of empire—Korean lives literally don't matter, are rendered "immaterial," ready to be destroyed instantly through US technological violence. This is how the war continues: through making certain populations exist in permanent precarity while others experience stability.

### Quote 3: Cause and Effect Inversion - Making Effects Into Causes

**Quote:**
> "Troublingly, the insulated temporality of the Korean War in the United States 'effect[s] a gulf and hypocrisy that shatter[s] any rational pattern of cause and effect.' Indeed, central to the Korean War's disquieting perdurance is an inversion of cause and effect that enables its present-day consequences, for example, in North Korea's push in the past half-decade toward nuclear self-defense, to be decontextualized as 'provocations' that call out for potentially catastrophic preemptive violence. This causal fallacy particular to US interpretations of North Korea—of misreading effects as causes—not coincidentally justifies the deferral of peace, thus perpetuating the Korean War's irresolution." (600)

**Why this matters:** This proves your "stalemate" point. The war's irresolution allows the US to invert cause and effect: North Korea's defensive responses to US aggression are reframed as unprovoked "provocations" that justify *more* US military presence. The war can never end because each consequence of US violence is reinterpreted as a new cause requiring intervention. This is spatial dislocation—the actual causes (US bombing, sanctions, threats) disappear while effects (North Korean defense) are deterritorialized into global threats.

### Quote 4: Racialized "Collateralization" - Who Gets to Live?

**Quote:**
> "Insofar as the disjunction of wartime turns on the collateralization of human life within a securitized paradigm, the Korean War foregrounds the creation of preconditions for what Ruth Wilson Gilmore, in her incisive definition of racism, calls 'group-differentiated vulnerability to premature death.' If invaluable within US-based critical race studies as an analysis of state power toward racially profiled and criminalized subjects, Gilmore's structural analysis of racism merits critical application, beyond domestic US parameters, to the US war machine's relation toward its various 'targets' in the Middle East, Asia, Africa, and Latin America, be it through 'hard,' unequivocally violent means (war, counterinsurgency, 'low-intensity' conflict, drone strikes) or 'soft,' less spectacular methods (sanctions, embargoes, human rights demonization, psychological warfare)." (602-603)

**Why this matters:** This directly addresses effects on refugees and those in Korea. "Collateralization of human life" means certain populations become collateral damage—acceptable losses. "Group-differentiated vulnerability to premature death" means the war systematically creates conditions where racialized populations (Koreans, Vietnamese, Middle Easterners) are more likely to die early. This isn't accidental violence—it's structural racism operating through imperialism. For refugees, this means their lives were rendered "collateral" by US violence. For those in Korea, it means permanent militarization and precarity.

### Quote 5: The War as "Cognitive Structure" and "Structure of Feeling"

**Quote:**
> "This special issue of positions: asia critique, 'The Unending Korean War,' undertakes as its focus the longue durée of the unending Korean War as a protean structure, at once generative and destructive, whose formations and deformations, impasses and costs, ruptures and obfuscations, can be traced on both sides of the North Pacific. Naturalized as 'forgotten' in the United States yet seared into national consciousness in both Koreas, the Korean War, as a differentiated structure of feeling, perception, memory, knowledge, and historical ruin, has persisted some six decades after the signing of the Armistice Agreement." (598)

**Why this matters:** This defines what Hong means by "cognitive structure" and "structure of feeling"—not just a past event but an ongoing way of experiencing reality that differs radically by geography. The war is "protean" (constantly changing form) yet persistent. It's a "structure of feeling, perception, memory, knowledge, and historical ruin"—meaning it shapes how people think, feel, remember, and know. Crucially, it's "differentiated": forgotten in US, seared in Korea. This cognitive split is what enables imperialism—Americans can't see what Koreans live daily.

### Quote 6: White Supremacy at Home, Colored Massacres Abroad

**Quote:**
> "In the powerful words of the CRC petition: 'We, Negro petitioners whose communities have been laid waste, whose homes have been burned and looted, whose children have been killed, whose women have been raped have noted with peculiar horror that the genocidal doctrines and actions of the American white supremacists have already been exported to the peoples of Asia.' The terror of '[w]hite supremacy at home,' according to Patterson, 'makes for colored massacres abroad. Both reveal contempt for human life in a colored skin.'" (602)

**Why this matters:** This connects domestic racial violence to imperial violence abroad, showing they're the same structure. The 1951 "We Charge Genocide" petition argued that violence against Black Americans and violence against Asians stem from the same white supremacist logic that renders non-white lives disposable. This proves the point about ongoing US imperialism: it operates through racialized violence that makes certain populations killable both domestically and internationally.
