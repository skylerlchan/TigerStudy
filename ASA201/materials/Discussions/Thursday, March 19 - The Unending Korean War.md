## Discussion Prompt

Prompt: In calling the Korean War "unending," Hong traces numerous ways in which the war is out of joint: in time ("wartime") and space (geography). What does this expansive thinking about the Korean War as a "cognitive structure" or "structure of feeling" (601) rather than just a set of past events confined to history help to reveal to us about ongoing US imperialism and the continuing effects on those who live in its wake (both refugees *and *those still in Korea)?

This is largely due to the “alienated technological nature” of the US, where it has enough military and technological power to intervene in any conflict around the world while keeping its own citizens isolated far from the fight itself. Even though we feel like we are in peacetime, isolated in our safe bubble of the continental US, our constant military presence and intervention in other parts of the world poses a state of constant war. This expansive thinking about the Korean War as not simply the first and last gunshots but rather as a constant state over time shows how war is a permanent condition, not just pages in a history textbook. While we in the US have the privilege to feel safe and learn about wartime in the “past”, people living in places such as the Middle East and Asia still experience wartime in the present through US military occupation and intervention. By displacing devastation “over there” and justifying it as “essential to security over here,” the US creates its own sense of peace at the expense of other countries(600). In a clear show of Mbembe’s necropolitics, the US sacrifices the physical safety and wellbeing of others in order to give its own citizens a sense of disconnect and peace, creating permanent wartime in other parts of the world.

**Skyler Chan** (2026-03-19):

The expansive thinking about the Korean War exposes how U.S. imperialism creates a self-perpetuating scaffolding that engineers unending crisis to justify its existence, which gives the illusion of progress at home. Reframing the war as existing in multiple temporalities, Hong reviews how Americans are frozen in the 1950s, believing the war has long ended, while in Korea, it is a perpetual present that keeps families separated and ensures a continuous threat of violence. Geographically, the war exceeds the Korean peninsula, allowing the U.S. to place bases surrounding the Pacific, hence creating a stream of borders, bases, and refugee camps. Therefore, Hong argues that the Korean War has fostered "a formidable, crisis-generating, self-perpetuating, institutional architecture." The war's unending nature is not a diplomatic failure but an intentional act of maintaining U.S. imperialism. Hong's argument is further strengthened by contrasting how Koreans and Americans view the war today. She notes that the war is "cognitively mapped as limited in one setting yet total in another, forgotten in one yet unforgettable in another." This disconnect reveals America strategically forgetting the destruction it caused, as well as the current fear it imposes. Eventually, this evolves into what Hong calls an "inversion of cause and effect that enables its present-day consequences." This allows the U.S. to "defensively" respond to threats, even when those threats were caused by its initial military presence (e.g., North Korea's nuclear weapons). For refugees and those in Korea, this scaffolding creates what scholar Ruth Wilson Gilmore defines as a "group-differentiated vulnerability to premature death," in which their lives are treated as disposable to the engineered crisis of the U.S. military presence. This cognitive framework normalizes perpetual war which allows for the continued destruction of a so-called expendable population.
