**David Liu** (2026-03-01):

The first factor is that in New Orleans, the Vietnamese and black communities possessed a mutual understanding and respect; Tang argues that “by no means do the Vietnamese Americans and blacks of New Orleans East possess a shared history,” but that their experiences follow a similar theme and resonate with each other(138). In the aftermath of Hurricane Katrina, Vietnamese residents such as Mike Tran were devastated by the damage, but banded together in acts of civil disobedience against “state and private companies looking to dump Katrina debris” in their area (120). After suffering a tragedy, the Vietnamese community was able to consolidate, rebuild, and fight back against government oppression. Similarly, during Hurricane Betsy, it was alleged that levees were intentionally demolished to protect “more privileged areas of the city” at the expense of black neighborhoods, and many white residents abandoned the Ninth Ward, leading to less and less government funding as the area became blacker(136). Despite this, the black community was also able to band together, with black businesses thriving again within a few years. While these are separate histories, they resonate with each other; “black folks understood” that the Vietnamese were trying to rebuild a place that was supposed to be forgotten after Katrina(130). The second factor is the lack of the Vietnamese small business owner/black customer dynamic in New Orleans; in fact, “during the early years of refugee resettlement, the opposite was often true”(141). In New Orleans East, “one finds class heterogeneity in both communities: each consists of the business owners, the home owners, the working poor, the welfare dependent, and the jobless”(121). Because of this class heterogeneity within the Vietnamese and black communities, neither race existed strictly above the other in terms of economic and societal status, so there existed no racially charged tensions between shopkeepers and customers that incited conflict in LA and New York. This helps us to understand that interracial and interclass solidarity is very possible, especially under the correct conditions. Because the Vietnamese and blacks did not exist exclusively in a given status on the social ladder, but rather overlapped and intermingled with each other heavily both economically and socially, it was very easy for the two communities to resonate with each other.

**Winsice Ng** (2026-03-04):

Tang identifies how “both communities were buoyed by respective histories of resilience” and that “Vietnamese American and black residents of New Orleans East were not prone to the class antagonisms that are said to provoke the well-rehearsed black-Asian conflict in the postindustrial city” (120). We see how the interracial solidarity in Nola is grounded in the common ground knowledge that both groups are there to rebuild their lives and thrive within a country that does not fully embrace them. Part of this also involves the movement away from “racial triangulation,” where this narrative of Asian-Americans being pitted against the blacks is not being further pushed by either groups (138). We see this especially in the Vietnamese community after Hurricane Katrina, where much of the media response that came after when they were rebuilding, was aimed to acknowledge the community efforts from both groups from this natural disaster rather than being politicalized as another way to enhance white dominance. Interestingly, we also see coexistence between ethnic groups, as businesses get transferred between the Blacks and Vietnamese and there is no apparent friction between groups who dominate certain industries. In a way, we see that without this narrative of competition and class divisions that whites push, there can exist a community which is diverse and works together to thrive. We saw this through the Vietnamese community’s outreach towards the black community after the disaster, their collaboration in tackling administrative issues together, and the combined motivation to uphold and survive in the place they consider home.

**Alex Tu** (2026-03-05):

The two factors that allowed for Vietnamese and Black people to work closely together is due to their distinct histories of resilience, which fueled their desires to rebuild their community and allowed one another to see genuinity in their efforts (120). Additionally, heterogeneity within the Vietnamese and Black communities made it hard to create a common narrative of Black and Asian conflict where Asian people were small business owners that took advantage of poor Black people since both communities had business owners, poor people, and many other kinds of groups (120-121).

Tang illustrated how much collaboration occurred between the Vietnamese and Black communities. For instance, in New Orleans East, the Mary Queen Church of Vietnam opened its doors to everyone in the community, serving as a home for Black people and as a place of worship (124). Additionally, Vietnamese community leaders were educated by Black people on the detrimental effects of the renewal of the landfill posed by the city to continue dumping Katrina debris in Versailles, allowing for more effective resistance and thus, leading to the landfill permit not being renewed (128).

Thus, this example in New Orleans shows the possibility of interracial solidarity, especially in times of crisis. However, Tang emphasizes that the crisis itself isn’t enough to create long-term alliances (129). Instead, he posits how solidarity is built on “usable pasts”, where Vietnamese and Black people don’t necessarily share the same history but similar ones (Vietnamese people faced many displacements, refusing to be uprooted again from New Orleans; Black people returning to their community after Hurricane Betsy) that can explain one's motives aren’t driven for nefarious purposes (i.e. purely economic motives) (125, 135). Through this situation, we can think about how alliances would form in other places through drawing on resonant pasts, often reflective of the journey that people took to be in that specific space.

**Salvatore Palizzolo** (2026-03-05):

Tang identifies two factors that explain the possibility of the unique solidarity observed between the Vietnamese refugees and black residents of New Orleans East: the first being their "responsive histories of resilience" which mutually resonated among both groups, and the second being the absence of clear "class antagonisms" that generalize racial groups as either exploitative business owners or the jobless poor (120). 

The use of these two factors of the New Orleans East racial landscape allowed its constituents to circumvent deeply rooted white systems of power by directly countering its known mechanisms of oppression. For example, the solidarity seen in Nola directly countered racial triangulation, which relies on Asian Americans to be valorized above black people and for Asian Americans to discipline black populations. In the case of the post-Katrina crisis in New Orleans, Vietnamese Americans and black people were adamant in asserting the "multiracial character" of their efforts, such as the effort to prevent the landfill in Versailles (128). In this way, the Vietnamese American population was unable to be characterized as an exceptional model minority to be valorized above their black counterparts. These groups were thus able to counter white imperial dominance which so often silently benefits from the conflict of minority groups.

Referring to the situation in New Orleans East, Tang tells us that "though such class complexity, on its own, does not make for multiracial alliances, it does clear a path for it" (145). I think the unique conditions in New Orleans East that allowed for such strong solidarity post-Katrina are demonstrative of what generally needs to be achieved in the relationship between minority groups if they are to unite in opposing white systems of suppression.

**Skyler Chan** (2026-03-05):

Perhaps a unique factor that kept New Orleans different from other urban centers was economic stagnation. New Orleans continued an "older, diversified economy" which may "have resulted in the spreading out of black and Vietnamese American workers across multiple industries." This ultimately reduced competition with one another, which is a stark contrast to other urban centers which tried to abandon the rust belt. This directly challenges the neoliberal assumption that growth and development benefits everyone. Tang shows that economic restructuring not only intensifies racial competition but solidifies it into rigid structures. The criticism of "backwardness" was an unintentional buffer against racial triangulation. Another factor that set New Orleans East apart was the way Vietnamese refugees interacted with Black-owned businesses. Rather than becoming competitors, "the vast majority of whom were receiving public assistance ... shopped primarily at black-owned grocery stores in New Orleans East." While the LA uprisings often talked about immigrants exploiting local communities, Vietnamese families eventually purchased businesses from Black owners who had done well.

The interracial solidarity in New Orleans can be best said by how they both possessed "resonant histories". Tang makes it clear that solidarity does not require identical experiences. Yet, they both understood "counterdiscourse, challenging the discursive power of racial triangulation that would seek to valorize the former over the latter in an effort to sustain white dominance." Tang furthers his contribution by mentioning about "class complexity" which undermines the simplistic narrative that produces racial conflict. This seems to disperse the ability for those in power to racialize conflict as Blacks and Vietnamese see each other as coworkers and peers.

**Nia Young** (2026-03-05):

One of the two factors that Tang identifies is a shared histories of resilience, resistance, and marginality, and at a certain point, shared histories of abandonment by the US. They recognized each others' marginality within a world of "white institutional power" (122) and desire to survive economically. In addition, the economic differences within each community meant that neither group felt like the other was seeking to displace the other, compared to dynamics in urban centers where Black and Asian Americans were pitted against each other for economic success, often through taking advantage of a merchant/customer relationship. Even after Katrina, Tang writes, "New Orleans East continues to maintain class heterogeneity" (139). Tang focuses on the unique historical trajectories and class heterogeneities that allowed the Vietnamese and Black residents of New Orleans East to resist the racial triangulation that had already succeeded in cities like NY and LA, as well as the agency exhibited by these communities to recognize their shared interests and mobilize for each other's wellbeing. 

The depiction of the New Orleans East communities allows us to understand the ways in which the discursive and structural effects of racial triangulation can be resisted: Recognition of shared structural positions, as well as political mobilization not just for the sake of airing grievances against the state but also to aid the people directly. The histories and communities that allowed for the South's inter-racial solidarity were unique, but not improbable, demonstrating that inter-racial tensions and prejudice are not inevitable consequences of coexistence within white hegemony.
