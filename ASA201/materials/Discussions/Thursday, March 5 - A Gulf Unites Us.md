**David Liu** (2026-03-01):

The first factor is that in New Orleans, the Vietnamese and black communities possessed a mutual understanding and respect; Tang argues that “by no means do the Vietnamese Americans and blacks of New Orleans East possess a shared history,” but that their experiences follow a similar theme and resonate with each other(138). In the aftermath of Hurricane Katrina, Vietnamese residents such as Mike Tran were devastated by the damage, but banded together in acts of civil disobedience against “state and private companies looking to dump Katrina debris” in their area (120). After suffering a tragedy, the Vietnamese community was able to consolidate, rebuild, and fight back against government oppression. Similarly, during Hurricane Betsy, it was alleged that levees were intentionally demolished to protect “more privileged areas of the city” at the expense of black neighborhoods, and many white residents abandoned the Ninth Ward, leading to less and less government funding as the area became blacker(136). Despite this, the black community was also able to band together, with black businesses thriving again within a few years. While these are separate histories, they resonate with each other; “black folks understood” that the Vietnamese were trying to rebuild a place that was supposed to be forgotten after Katrina(130). The second factor is the lack of the Vietnamese small business owner/black customer dynamic in New Orleans; in fact, “during the early years of refugee resettlement, the opposite was often true”(141). In New Orleans East, “one finds class heterogeneity in both communities: each consists of the business owners, the home owners, the working poor, the welfare dependent, and the jobless”(121). Because of this class heterogeneity within the Vietnamese and black communities, neither race existed strictly above the other in terms of economic and societal status, so there existed no racially charged tensions between shopkeepers and customers that incited conflict in LA and New York. This helps us to understand that interracial and interclass solidarity is very possible, especially under the correct conditions. Because the Vietnamese and blacks did not exist exclusively in a given status on the social ladder, but rather overlapped and intermingled with each other heavily both economically and socially, it was very easy for the two communities to resonate with each other.
