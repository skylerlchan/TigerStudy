## Discussion Prompt

Prompt: While Roh et al. identify the way in which "techno-Orientalism" is connected with earlier forms of Orientalism (the general cultural-political form of constructing an "Eastern Other" in order to shore up the boundaries of  Western self-identity; eg. p. 7-8), they also hint numerous times at the way in which the post-war rise of Japan and post-90s rise of China have changed the calculus of techno-Orientalism from something that is indelibly authored by the West to something that is co-authored or even willingly appropriated, for better or worse, by some Asian nations.

What possible ramifications might this techno "self-Orientalizing" by some Asian nations as a way to claim expertise in techno-capitalist production/innovation have for how we discuss Asian racial identity in Asian America?

**Natalie Gelman** (2026-03-24):

A possible ramification that techno self-Orientalizing may alter is the way we discuss stereotypes within the Asian identity. Further, techno self‑Orientalizing in Asia reshapes how we talk about stereotypes in Asian American racial identity. According to Roh, Huang, and Niu, techno-orientalism is “imagining Asia and Asians in hypo-or hypertechnological terms in cultural productions and political discourse,” (p. 2). Inherently, this definition imposes stereotypes onto Asian communities and Asian countries as proposing Asians as technology forward or backward. In the eyes of the West, the authors explain that “Japan creates technology, but China is the technology.” These stereotypes show that racist ideals of Asian countries in terms of technology are born from this idea of the Japanese signifying innovators in tech, and China signifying labor in tech. The authors also explain that techno-orientalism is “structured by the East and West alike” (page 3). Images in both Western and Japanese media such as portraying a  cyberpunk can “further ethnocentric or nationalist projects” (page 8). In turn these stereotypes are portrayed by both sides of the spectrum, however, can dehumanize Asians, portraying the communities as “superhuman”, (page 1) which are similar to stereotypes of Asians that are dangerous, such as Chinese railroad workers being viewed as robotic, and the attack on Vincent Chin in the 80s being viewed as “factory machine” (page 11). All in all,  techno self‑Orientalizing in Asia reshapes how we talk about stereotypes in Asian American racial identity, and shows the progression of how these stereotypes are not just harmful and show patterns of dehumanization, but are also promoted by Asian nations.

The emergence of a techno “self-Orientalizing” among Asian countries complicates the understanding of Asian American identity by altering techno-Orientalism from an external stereotype to a partially internalized and globalized discourse. As Japan and China utilize their representations of technology to secure economic and geopolitical power, they still utilize a similar logic to racialize Asians as either mechanical or efficient. For Asian Americans, this change further reinforces the model minority stereotype, portraying them as innately suited to technical labor while reifying them to a functional position in a system of techno-capitalism. This is a “positive” stereotype, and yet it is dehumanizing in that it reduces Asian bodies to an extension of a machine or system. Moreover, self-Orientalization also contributes to intra-Asian hierarchies, in which those with an identity tied to technology and innovation, such as those from East Asia, are valued over others, and this further undermines Asian American solidarity. Self-Orientalization also heightens the state of "contact without encounter" that Roh et al. discuss, in which, while technology from Asian cultures becomes more globally visible, there is no concomitant increase in understanding of Asian culture. Most importantly, however, is that Asian Americans are caught up in transnational anxieties, perceived as representatives of foreign technology, rather than as Americans, and therefore self-Orientalization does not overcome techno-Orientalism, but rather reconfigures it, so that racial identity is tied to global production, competition, and perception.

**Salvatore Palizzolo** (2026-03-24):

Simultaneously critics and optimists, Roh et. al. "see an opportunity for critical reappropriations in texts that self-referentially engage with Asian images," but also understand the "danger that Asian and Asian American creators might internalize techno-Orientalist patterns and uncritically replicate the same dehumanizing model" (7). After all, the original motivations for the emergence of techno-Orientalism can be encompassed as the "West's project of securing dominance as architects of the future, a project that requires configurations of the East as the very technology with which to shape it," and thus appropriations of this identity run the risk of essentializing Asian racial identity in deleterious ways (2).

In the context of Japan, Roh et. al. present both sides of the coin. Japan's leading position in the speculative fiction genre led to reinforcement of nihonjinron - an idea of Japanese exceptionalism, which in turn further reinforced the Western construction of the techno-Orient and the building of ethnocentric or nationalist projects, according to Sato. However, Gibson posits that it is Japan's encounter with the West that has made it a site of the future that counters the West. After Western technological and medical intervention during the Industrial Revolution, Japan became "technologically advanced and therefore culturally fascinating" insofar as it "threatens the foundational fiction of the West as Future" (9). Skeptics Ueno and Oda contend that techno-Orientalism produces "contact without encounter" which fundamentally leads to inability of the West and Japan to truly understand each other.

Roh et. al. also argue that the "U.S. techno-Orientalist imagination is thus rooted in this view of the Asian body as a form of expendable technology," showcasing a clearly harmful effect of techno-Orientalist essentialisms (11). This aspect of techno-Orientalism is particularly seen through Chinese subjects because of their unique history in the context of manufacturing in the U.S. and abroad. Through examples of the brutal murder of Vincent Chin and the Citizens Against Government Waste commercial titled "Chinese Professor," Roh et. al. show that techno-Orientalism produces the narrative that "U.S. jobs and manufacturing are being stolen by inorganic, technologically infused persons who threaten not only our economic but humanistic integrity" (12). 

The question remains whether authorship by Asians is sufficient in repurposing techno-Orientalism toward a discourse that is not entirely harmful toward Asian Americans. Roh et. al. make a strong case for the disadvantages for techno-Orientalism in this reading despite their proclaimed support of the potential in reappropriations of techno-Orientalism.
