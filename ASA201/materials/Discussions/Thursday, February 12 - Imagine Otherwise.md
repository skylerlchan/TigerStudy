## Discussion Prompt

Prompt: As many theorists have pointed out, becoming a "subject" (eg. a citizen legible to the state) is a desirable outcome for many marginalized people, but it also enmeshes us within the exclusionary and violent structure of the nation-state. As a deconstructionist theorist, Chuh argues that rather than focusing on the importance of subjecthood, we should turn towards a "subjectless discourse," which focuses on how process of knowledge production make terms (like "citizen") come to have meaning or power at all. 

Chuh also argues that instead of focusing on pinning down and defining Asian American identity, we should value difference/dissensus for "demonstrating the impossibility of objectivity" and how the constructedness and artificiality of Asian America foregrounds "contingency, irresolution, and nonequivalence" (8). 

In your estimation, how do these arguments by Chuh contribute to "Asian American politics" as we've discussed it thus far?

**Ethan Do** (2026-02-12):

Kandice Chuh’s talk of “subjectless discourse” prompts Asian American politics to transcend its present character as merely attaining recognition within the nation-state. For some social groups, becoming a recognizable “subject,” particularly a citizen, has long been accepted as one of the main ideals of politics. In the context of Asian Americans, this is indeed true, given the long history of exclusion. But Chuh problematizes this by engaging us in the way that “the law requires subjection/subjectification” (10). This phrase is noteworthy, as it points out how, in trying to attain subjectoid recognition, we are not simply becoming subjects but are simultaneously becoming subjectified by the very rules and constructs of that same law. Recognition, then, is not absolute; it is predicated on becoming consistent with norms set by the state.

As such, Chuh claims that “subjectivity itself, alone, cannot remedy injustice” (10). In other words, “Asian Americans” may achieve citizenship or political representation, yet “Asian American” justice is not necessarily served. Injustice pervades at more profound levels, including economic disparities, racism, or global power structures. Moreover, Chuh argues that instead of attempting to identify or solidify the definition of “Asian American,” or rather its identity, the need to increase awareness of “contingency, irresolution, and nonequivalence” is prominent. The use of these terms above requires no further analysis, to the degree that Chuh is deliberately belaboring or bringing attention to the instability that hinders “Asian American” from becoming static or manageable. With such instability, Chuh proposes that instead of proving who is or who is not “Asian American,” the importance of understanding how “Asian American” initially received its power is at hand. Moreover, Chuh’s work is essential to the field of Asian American politics due to her contributions to moving beyond inclusion or recognition. Chuh’s work provokes the need to think politically or to consider the foundational level of “Asian American,” or rather subjecthood in general.

Chuh's most radical argument regarding "Asian American politics" revolves around the "subjectless discourse." Her definition of the subject reveals its exclusionary nature, stating that a subject "only becomes recognizable and can act as such by conforming to certain regulatory matrices". Hence, striving for inclusion in such a system is paradoxical, as becoming a subject allows for people to be excluded, as seen previously with nonwhites and women. Under her framework of "subjectless discourse," the discussion shifts toward prioritizing differences and how power is distributed. At the same time, it draws criticism of citizenship and its ability to create the "inequitable distribution of power and resources that results from capitalist practice," which briefly touches on the struggles anarchists faced.  On the topic of "subjectless discourse," a recurring tension in Asian American politics is the divide between activists and academics as we see in the previous reading. Chuh breaks down this divide by stating that "universities are sites of investment for corporate capital and military interest; they are shaped and sustained by government investments; and in these and myriad other ways, are precisely Ideological State Apparatuses". Hence, her work becomes political as it confronts the very essence of this divide. She posits that Asian American literature reveals "a theory of the materiality and partiality of knowledge itself," validating theory as a political tool to challenge state power’s ability to manage and control its own subjects. Another relevant point is Chuh's analysis of cultural nationalism through her discussion of the novel Blu's Hanging. Her evidence, drawn from the conflict between Filipino Americans and Japanese Americans, demonstrates that dissensus transforms Asian American politics into a "field of collaborative antagonisms, collaborative in the doubled sense of working together and working subversively against, and antagonistic in the ways in which diverse approaches to knowledge critique and identify each other’s limits". This introduction of strategic anti-essentialism provides a contrast to Lowe's support of a unified identity to push political action.

Chuh asks us to rethink what Asian American politics is trying to achieve. For a long time the goal was to become recognized as full subjects which that meant citizenship, rights, legal protection, and being seen as “real Americans.” Given the long history of exclusion, that made sense. But Chuh questions whether being fully accepted by the nation state actually solves the deeper problem. When she talks about a “subjectless discourse,” she’s saying we shouldn’t treat recognition by the state as the ultimate victory. Instead of trying to perfectly define who counts as Asian American, she wants us to look at how categories like “citizen” or “American” get their power in the first place. For example, who decides what those words mean? Who gets included automatically and who has to fight for it?

When Asian Americans do stay out of political debates, they get criticized by activists for not using their platform or privilege. The model minority status can feel like some validation like we are tolerated as long as we perform, succeed, and don’t disrupt anything. We want to prove we belong, to protect the little stability we’ve gained, even if that means not challenging the system too openly.

I think this is where Chuh’s argument clicks. She suggests that chasing recognition as a “good” or “successful” minority keeps us invested in the very structure that defines and limits us. Instead of just proving we deserve to be here, she pushes us to question why  belonging depends on performance at all. The desire for validation makes sense historically, but it also shows how unstable and conditional our place in the nation-state really is. **Salvatore Palizzolo** (2026-03-14):

Chuh’s arguments promoting a “subjectless discourse” counter previous ideas of strategic essentialism and panethnic entrepreneurship, and, in my view, provide an account of the more modern problems faced by Asian American studies.

Chuh argues that subjecthood is no longer the ideal. With “the end of the dominance of the nation-state as the preeminent unit of global organization” and the fact that justice takes different forms at different times, Chuh calls for a new strategy: a “deconstructive attitude” (3). In a shift from previous ways of thinking among Asian American intellectuals, Chuh claims “difference as anterior to and irresolvable in identity” (8). She emphasizes that by reducing away these differences, “a ‘subject’ only becomes recognizable and can act as such by conforming to certain regulatory matrices” (9). Further, adherence to subjectivity can lead to “problematic assumptions of essential identities” (10). Thus, Chuh recommends “strategic anti-essentialism,” allowing a reinvestigation into what political justice may mean for whom (10).

Chuh also critiques the “Asian American” label. For one, Chuh highlights its promotion of “foreign within” and paradigms like that of the model minority (11). It follows US nationalism’s “promotion of identity over difference” (13). It is a panethnic identifier that allowed a claim to America during a time of perpetual alienism. However, its use as a tool of panethnic entrepreneurship is increasingly inadequate and “homogenizes diversity such that recognizing ‘differences among’ fractures its intelligibility” (21). Connecting back to the idea of subjectless discourse, Chuh claims that the term “implies a normative subject” (21). I think Chuh as such finds an important weakness of panethnicity while addressing the Asian American label, which Chuh says “sustains norms of behavior and identity” (22).

Chuh clarifies her goal: “The point is not to work toward resolving differences … but is instead to insist on the productiveness of dissensus in demonstrating the impossibility of any objectivity” (28). I appreciate that Chuh recognizes the pitfalls of attempting to reconcile differences within heterogeneous Asian America. I do agree that fitting into subjecthood and citizenship should no longer be the end goal, but I am uneasy about Chuh’s focus on difference, something I wonder could lead to further fracturing.
