# Trask Discussion Prep — "Settlers of Color and 'Immigrant' Hegemony"

## Breaking Down the Question

Your teacher is asking three connected things. Here's why each part matters:

**1. How does Trask oppose the "hard-working immigrant" narrative?**
The teacher wants you to see that Trask isn't just disagreeing with a story — she's exposing how a *specific myth* about Asian immigrants erases the reality of colonization. The "hard-working immigrant" story sounds positive, but Trask argues it actually *justifies* the ongoing theft of Native Hawaiian land and power.

**2. How does she oppose inclusion into the US state?**
This is about statehood itself. Most people treat Hawai'i becoming a US state as progress. Trask says the opposite: inclusion was never something Hawaiians chose, and celebrating it means celebrating dispossession.

**3. Where do Asian settlers stand relative to white haole?**
The teacher is pushing you toward Trask's most provocative argument: that Asians in Hawai'i are not simply victims of racism — they are *also* settlers who benefit from and participate in the colonial system, even if they sit below haole in the hierarchy.

## The "Hard-Working Immigrant" Narrative — What It Hides

Trask's central move is reframing what sounds like an inspiring story as colonial ideology. She writes that settler ideology "weaves a story of success: poor Japanese, Chinese, and Filipino settlers supplied the labor for wealthy, white sugar planters during the long period of the Territory." On its surface, that's a story about overcoming. But Trask says "exploitative plantation conditions thus underpin a master narrative of hard work and the endlessly celebrated triumph over anti-Asian racism." The word *underpin* is doing the work here — the suffering on plantations becomes the moral foundation for claiming belonging. If you suffered and worked hard, you earned your place. But Trask asks: earned your place *on whose land*?

She's direct about the consequence: "For our Native people, Asian success proves to be but the latest elaboration of foreign hegemony." Asian political rise didn't liberate Hawai'i — it just replaced one layer of foreign control with another. The history becomes "a twice-told tale, first of discovery and settlement by European and American businessmen and missionaries, then of the plantation Japanese, Chinese, and eventually Filipino rise to dominance in the islands."

## Opposing Inclusion and Statehood

Trask frames statehood not as liberation but as a deepening of colonization. She points out that "the post-statehood euphoria stigmatizes Hawaiians as a failed indigenous people whose conditions, including out-migration, actually worsen after statehood." In other words, Hawaiians are made to look like failures for not thriving inside a system that was built on taking everything from them.

She also dismantles the idea of "power sharing" — the notion that Hawaiians should just wait their turn in a multiethnic democracy. She writes: "Given that Hawaiians are indigenous, that our government was overthrown, and that we are entitled, as a nation, to sovereignty, the argument that we should share power with non-Natives who benefit from theft of our sovereignty is, simply, grotesque."

The statehood plebiscite itself was illegitimate in her view: "The plebiscite in 1959 allowed only one choice — statehood — other than territorial status. By not including other choices, the United States violated international human rights law."

## Asians in Relation to Haole

This is where Trask's argument gets most uncomfortable — and most important. She does not let Asians off the hook just because they also experienced racism. She describes a dynamic where Asians and haole compete for power *over* Hawaiians, not alongside them: "This intrasettler competition between haole and Asians is a hallmark of colonial situations. Such contests serve, especially if severe, to mask even further the dispossession and marginalization of Natives."

Asians claim the term "local" precisely to distinguish themselves from haole — but Trask says this is still a settler move: "the 'local' identity tag blurs the history of Hawai'i's only indigenous people while staking a settler claim." Calling yourself "local" instead of "settler" is, in her framing, a way to deny complicity. She writes that the "local" ideology "tells a familiar, and false, tale of success: Asians came as poor plantation workers and triumphed decades later as the new, democratically elected ruling class."

The bottom line: haole set up the colonial structure, and Asians inherited and maintain it. Both benefit from Hawaiian dispossession, and the competition between them distracts from the indigenous claim underneath.

## How to Use This in Discussion

Lead with the structural argument — Trask isn't attacking Asian people, she's attacking a *narrative* that makes colonialism invisible. The "hard-working immigrant" story transforms settlers into heroes and makes Hawaiians' dispossession look like their own failure. When you speak, ground every claim in the land: Trask's whole framework is that your relationship to the land determines whether you are indigenous or a settler, and no amount of hard work changes that equation.
