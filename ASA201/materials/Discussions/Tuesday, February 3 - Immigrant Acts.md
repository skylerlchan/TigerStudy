## Discussion Prompt

Prompt: 

In discussing difference within the Asian American community, Lowe critically distinguishes between the *political uses of *Asian American identity (eg. Claiming the identity of “Asian American” to achieve a political goal) and what that identity risks erasing when it collapses so much difference into a homogenized “cultural nationalism” (eg. arguing for a purity and unity despite disparate interests of South East Asian, East Asian, South Asian, West Asian people as well as different classes, genders, sexualities). 

In the end, Lowe points to Gayatri Spivak’s notion of “strategic essentialism” as a middle ground between these two options. What is “strategic essentialism” and what are some of the possibilities (usefulness and drawbacks) of engaging in this strategy?

**David Liu** (2026-02-01):

Strategic essentialism is the concept of using a group identity for a “scrupulously visible political interest”, while simultaneously remaining aware of the heterogeneity within the group itself (82). While an identity, such as Asian American, may be useful for uniting distinct groups such as Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, etc. for the benefit of all, it is imperative that the “differences and differential relationships” within the movement itself are kept in consideration (67). I believe that in theory, this strategy is very logical; minority groups can unite as one to oppose the dominant culture, while maintaining and respecting individual boundaries within the group itself. However, I believe that this strategy is difficult to implement in reality and may be innately flawed. As Lowe mentions, it is “difficult to act exclusively in terms of a single valence or political interest” due to multiplicity (74). Race is not the only factor. Gender, cultural differences, and other factors all contribute to each individual’s priorities; it is difficult for a heterogeneous group to act homogeneously. Lowe gives the example of minority women, torn between two identities. Asian American feminists are often labeled as “assimilationist” for undermining “the power of Asian American men” by opposing misogyny in Asian culture; while they feel like they should support their male counterparts in fighting for their culture, their culture itself may be misogynistic, giving some women the uncomfortable choice of choosing between their culture or their gender (76). While strategic essentialism is in theory a good idea, it is difficult to implement because it is easy for the multiplicity of identities and potential discrimination within minority cultures themselves to lead to disagreements.

From my understanding, Spivak’s “strategic essentialism” refers to the idea that the Asian American identity can be used as a rally to enter spaces and discussions that historically excluded them. Upon entering these spaces, the heterogeneity and instability of the Asian American identity is acknowledged, preventing the Asian American identity from being reduced to a fixed identity.

     Some advantages to strategic essentialism is the ability to recruit people, a prerequisite for enacting change. Hence, it is imperative that the Asian American label isn’t one of exclusion but openness. With numbers, people are able to make their voices heard. Additionally, by recruiting a diverse group of Asian Americans, a more comprehensive political agenda can be created to delineate what needs to be changed. 

     Some disadvantages to strategic essentialism is the possibility of incohesiveness. To fight against the system, a sense of unity needs to be felt. However, if the people rallied differ widely in experiences as a result of race, gender, socioeconomic class, etc., it can make it harder for people to unite against a single cause. Furthermore, as a result of diverse interests and experiences, disagreements are bound to arise as to how the system should change. For example, Asian Americans of higher socioeconomic status may be less inclined to change the system. Thus, in order to use “Asian American” as a rally, compromise and discussion will be a necessary component to spearhead a movement that aims at changing the system.

Lowe derives the concept of “Strategic Essentialism” from the work of Spivak, who argues that grouping into Asian Americans allows for “scrupulously visible political interest.” This platform, enabled by visibility, enables internal issues to rise and be discussed as well. Lowe posits that the “Strategic Essentialism” is not an admission that the group conforms to one mold, but rather that there are tactical advantages within politics to behave as one cohesive group. 

Using Asian American as a signifier allows for mainly the “contesting and disrupting the discourses that exclude Asian Americans.” Lowe argues this identity was a culmination of “hard-earned unity that has been achieved in the last thirty years of Asian American politics” Even more than a signifier, Asian Americans can be used as a way of positioning through “horizontal” affiliations, meaning that Asian Americans can group with other minority groups to push for systemic change. 

However, some disadvantages with this strategy are that this movement may inadvertently conform all Asians into one homogenous group. For example, a central issue is also the tendency to generalize Asian American identity as predominantly male, which removes the experiences of women. Lowe found that this identity often presumes three major assumptions: "masculinity, American birth, and the speaking of English.” However, this alienates other groups who have different gender, sexuality, education, and class. Lowe further argues that this identity can also “discourage laboring Asian Americans from joining with workers of other colors,” or in broad terms, allying with other women of color for any issue in particular.

**Salvatore Palizzolo** (2026-02-03):

Gayatri Spivak's notion of “strategic essentialism” describes the concept of a group accepting an essentializing, uniform identity. Importantly, this identity is a "socially constructed unity" and is not necessarily established to represent the group as homogeneous, but exists as a means of political positioning (82). The advantages of such a strategy include utilizing the signifiers that may be already associated with that group as a unifying force to be used in the deconstruction of systems that seek to exclude that group. Additionally, this strategy allows for the possibility to display the variety in the constituent heterogeneous identities of the group, which could lead to the ultimate prevention of future essentialisms. As a drawback of the strategy, engaging in "strategic essentialism," involves a presumption of similarities, which can lead to erasing particularity in the interest of preserving unity among the group. This leads to a lack of recognition of differences in "national origin, class, gender, and sexuality" (83). However, an over-emphasis of the differences and heterogeneity within the group can also be dangerous to the unity which grants the group political leverage.
