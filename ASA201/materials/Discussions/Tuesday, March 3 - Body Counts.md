**David Liu** (2026-03-01):

According to Espiritu, the dual utility of militarized refugees is to serve as both an “antidote to the ‘rescue and liberation’ myths and memories” and as a justification for “necessary, just, and successful” US military actions in Vietnam(1, 2). The US wishes to be always portrayed as “triumphant and moral,” where the US is always seen as the undisputed so-called “good guys” who are ultimately victorious and justified in their military efforts(1). However, the Vietnam war was a difficult memory as the US ended the war as neither “victor nor liberator”(1), and incidents such as the My Lai Massacre tainted the image of US benevolence. As such, by framing the Vietnamese as helpless refugees and the US as a safe haven where the Vietnamese refugees were able to thrive, the US simultaneously provided justification for its military actions and portrayed itself as the savior and liberator. It is important to understand the instrumentalization of militarized refugees in multiple ways, such as why they are viewed as a problem; Espiritu agrees with critical refugee study scholars that refugees are not a problem in that they are “pathetic victims who drain the state’s resources” but because they represent a “transgression of the social contract between a state and its citizen” (12). When shifting focus only to humanitarian needs of refugees, we take away attention from the broader underlying reasons as to why these refugees exist in the first place; US militarization in various other parts of the world directly causes these problems, yet the US frames itself as the savior and liberator who provides sanctuary from these very problems that they caused.

**Winsice Ng** (2026-03-02):

Espiritu coined the term "militarized refuge(es)" as a way to “encapsulate this symbiotic relationship: that refuge and refugees are co-constitutive, and that both are the (by)product of U.S. militarism” (2). It emphasizes the root of the supposed refugee problem that persists, militarism, but also reemphasizes how refugees are also created from this act of providing refuge in the first place. In the context of the Vietnam War, because of this, it allows us to revisit its history to critically examine the full story of what happened to the resulting Vietnamese refugees. For instance, it sheds light on how the U.S. is a “military aggressor[]” rather than the current narrative of them being “magnanimous rescuers” (10). As we begin to dive deeper into the implications of this, we can see how this narrative has helped push the resulting image of the Vietnamese being the “good refugee,” accepting work below their pay-grade and above typical labor, widening the economic and social gap between the Vietnamese community and the Americans (7). We also see that acknowledging this militarization also helps acknowledge the loss the Vietnamese experienced in the war as well since currently, much of the Vietnam War history is commemorated through the American lens, mourning the loss of the soldiers in the war, without regarding the lives the Vietnamese also lost. Thus, this term is necessary in rewriting how we view America in terms of its supposed heroic endeavors, humanizing the Vietnamese, and deconstructing the notion of refugees as a way to control the overall perception of a group rather than acknowledging the reason for their refugee-state in the first place.

**Salvatore Palizzolo** (2026-03-03):

Espiritu asserts the importance in engaging with the Vietnam War and its consequences even today, not to detract from the study of Vietnamese Americans themselves and their complexities, but to "critically engage the war as an important historical and discursive site of Vietnamese subject formation and of the shaping and articulation of U.S. nationhood" (16). Thus is the motivation for Espiritu's employment of the term "militarized refuge(es)," one that avoids decoupling of war studies and refugee studies. Through this joint study, Espiritu argues, we achieve two conclusions. The first is that, despite its image as the "nation of refuge," the U.S. and its wars play central roles in "structuring the displacements, dispersions, and migrations of refugees to the United States and elsewhere" (17). The second is that the resulting exodus of refugees and their postwar condition is actually used as justification for their intake by the U.S. and, thereby, also for U.S. wars.

Vietnamese refugees have been historically viewed as a "problem" for the nation, specifically through the proliferation of concerns surrounding a "refugee resettlement crisis." Beyond this instrumentalization, Espiritu affirms the importance of understanding the internal juxtaposition within "militarized refuge(es)," claiming that such an understanding "expose[s] the hidden violence behind the humanitarian term 'refuge,' thereby challenging the powerful narrative of America(ns) rescuing and caring for Vietnam's discarded that erases the role that U.S. foreign policy and war played in inducing the 'refugee crisis' in the first place" (18). We cannot continue to simply consider refugees as a "problem" for the nation because that line of thought distracts from the global conditions and U.S. actions that play critical roles in bringing about the refugee condition. In addition, this reasoning pigeonholes refugees as helpless figures in need of rescue, an idea that is often used as justification for the further incapacitation of refugees.
