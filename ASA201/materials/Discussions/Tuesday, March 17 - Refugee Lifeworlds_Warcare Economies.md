## Discussion Prompt

Prompt: These readings from Zahzah and Troeung give us different views of the difficulties many refugees face in the "afterlives" of war after relocation to the West. While Zahzah traces how state programs like the "CVE" continue to repress and criminalize Middle East and African refugees even in San Diego under the guise of "care economies," Troeung gives us a powerful look into how refugees face different problems with "care" when they are identified as subjects of charity and pity. How does Troeung's concept of "refugee lifeworlds" describe different possibilities for self-expression and political change beyond state "care" for refugees in San Diego and Canadian, respectively? (Zahzah does not use the term itself but what they describe resonates with Troeung's concept).

**Ethan Do** (2026-03-15):

Troeung’s concept of refugee lifeworlds is an understanding of the social and political worlds that refugees build as they live within a system that is influenced by war, displacement, and resettlement. Troeung’s work focuses on the social and political worlds that refugees build as a way to show the opportunities for self-expression and resistance that exist outside of the state’s notion of care.

Troeung’s work on the Cambodian refugees in Canada illustrates the state’s care through its humanitarian programs that construct the refugee as a grateful recipient of Western care and charity. The public narrative is one that celebrates Canada as a good actor in the world, and the refugee is constructed as an object of charity and pity. Troeung’s work suggests that such a notion of care silences the refugee as she is encouraged to enact her gratitude instead of speaking about the violence that she experienced. The refugee lifeworlds construct a narrative that focuses on the refugee’s attempts to hold on to their family memory and their alternative histories and interpretations of the past.

A similar phenomenon is also seen in Zahzah’s analysis of CVE (Countering Violent Extremism) programs in San Diego, though from a security perspective instead of a charity perspective. CVE programs are represented as a support mechanism to prevent radicalization among Middle Eastern and African refugee populations. Instead, however, CVE programs are represented as a surveillance mechanism that views refugees as a potential threat and monitors them through schools and social services. Though Zahzah’s analysis is not explicitly represented as a refugee lifeworlds perspective, the populations she discusses fit into Troeung’s definition of refugee lifeworlds. Refugees in San Diego develop a lifeworld of solidarity, culture, and politics in spite of the surveillance and monitoring by the state. Together, both analyses demonstrate that refugees are not just a product of the state’s care but also develop their lifeworlds as a mechanism of survival and politics.

**Nia Young** (2026-03-17):

Zahzah's descriptions of "warcare economies" focuses on the co-constitutive relationship between militarism in the global South and counter-terroristic humanitarianism. Through this programming, Zahzah argues, community members, often women, are surveilled and isolated from their communities while non-state violence is delegitimized and individualized. According to Zahzah, these CVE programs allow the United States to decide which militarization movements are legitimate, and which are acts of terrorism (surveilling and criminalizing any who might be connected to or sympathetic to them), through the lens of which movements are supported by the West and which are a reaction to the West's imperialism and exploitation of the region. The women who act as the focus of humanitarian programming are divided into "victims, extremists, or potential catalysts for "change" in their communities" (Zahzah 40). In a different outlook on refugee care, Troeung touches on the suffering experienced by refugee communities due to a lack of access to proper psychological, disability, and health support. She argues that the stereotype of the depressed, disabled refugee is a reflection of their new country's "divestment" and "power over life" regime on top of the suffering experienced in the home country. Similarly to Zahzah, Troeung argues that the Western portrayal of the depressed, disabled refugee strengthens (Canadian) exceptionalism and obscures the role of Western imperialism in creating that disability. Through the concept of "refugee lifeworlds," Troeung argues that a lens of critical disability studies can allow refugee status to be seen not as a tragedy but rather as a point of "gain," as the lifeworld "strives to reactivate and reanimate refugee ways of knowing and being that have been destroyed, depoliticized, and evacuated of meaning in the service of maintaining the liberal imperial status quo" (Troeung 10).

**Skyler Chan** (2026-03-17):

In both cases, Zahzah and Troeung reveal how care systems function as mechanisms of control rather than support. In Zahzah's case, it's CVE surveillance and in Troeung's case, it's charitable pity. This fundamental paradox reveals how care is a power play. Troeung's concept of refugee lifeworlds is best described by Thien's account of the Cambodian genocide, stating that, "We need to look at it, think about it, mourn it, be angry about it, and at least live in it for a little while." By simply remembering the past, Troeung is saying that is how refugees fight back against being controlled. The state is threatened because their version is attacked by the first-person account of the refugees. In Canada, one way that Cambodian refugees fight back is by maintaining oral history. These "lifeworlds" become a form of resistance using memory and community, turning refugees into analysts and witnesses rather than victims or threats. Zahzah's analysis of San Diego's CVE program reveals the same dynamic in her term "warcare economies." Under CVE, the very public assistance that Muslim and African refugees use becomes a source of surveillance data and control. Specifically, Zahzah explains that CVE creates "the illusion of benevolent, good-faith humanitarianism to build trust with community members, encouraging support for patriotic agendas to freely access intel. In turn, communities believe there is a safe space and open up, as programmers identify political threats and criminalize the opinions and actions of those presumed threats." Yet, refugees can resist by maintaining what Troeung calls illegible practices: organizing autonomously, preserving community solidarities, and refusing total transparency.

**Salvatore Palizzolo** (2026-03-17):

In exploring multiple facets of the afterlives of Cambodian refugees, Troeung builds the concept of "refugee lifeworlds" to elucidate some of the ways that refugees can dictate the direction of their own post-war lives. While Zahzah's text centers more on the actions of the state in its mission to confine refugees within "warcare economies," the Troeung text also gives us some ways that Cambodian refugees can harness agency through self-expression and political change. For one, Troeung addresses the "overrepresentation" of Cambodian people with trauma, mental health issues, and mental illness, suggesting a shift from a conception of trauma as "individual, knowable impairment" to a "system of differential impairment of racialized and gendered bodies" (13). Identifying with this idea, Troeung claims, will bring a necessary shift to consideration of the geopolitical and imperialistic influences on refugee disability. Thus, we see Troeung link self-expression and political change. Further, Troeung discourages the understanding of refugees merely as "horrific war remnants" and is hopeful that there is "gain" to be found in the refugee experience that "has the potential to disrupt the taken-for-granted order of power" (14, 17). As an important example of collective self-expression borne under the pressure of oppressive regimes, Troeung provides a description of the Khmer people's deliberate silence - the "act of planting the kapok tree," as the proverb goes - as part of her "Cambodian Refugee Archive" (28). This came to be cemented as a "way of knowing, being, and surviving in a system of genocidal violence and its afterlife" for Cambodian refugees (32). The connotation of "rebirth and "regeneration" present in the proverb informs the persevering determination found in the more recent Cambodian renaissance of the arts.
