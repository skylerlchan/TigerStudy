## II. THESIS STATEMENT (Draft)

*Everything Everywhere All at Once* enacts what Kandice Chuh theorizes as "subjectless discourse" by fracturing the coherent Asian American subject across infinite multiversal iterations, refusing the liberal mandate for stable identity while making visible the "scattered hegemonies" (Grewal & Kaplan) that produce "Asian American" as simultaneously an impossible and necessary formation. Through its formal strategy of narrative multiplication, the film performs the very deconstructive attitude Chuh demands—holding "Asian American" under erasure to reveal how coherent subjectivity itself is the apparatus through which U.S. nationalism regulates racialized difference.

**Why this thesis works:**
- Uses specific terminology from Chuh (subjectless discourse, deconstructive attitude, under erasure)
- Connects formal strategy to theoretical argument
- Shows how the text *performs* the theory rather than just *depicting* it
- Sets up sophisticated analysis beyond representation politics

## III. DETAILED OUTLINE

### A. OPENING: The Crisis of the Subject

**Hook:** Begin with the laundromat scene—Evelyn Wang confronting infinite versions of herself across the multiverse. This is not metaphor; this is **materially what subjectlessness looks like**.

**Key Move:** Immediately distinguish this from liberal multiculturalism's "celebration of diversity." Cite Chuh directly:

> "Subjectlessness, as a conceptual tool, points to the need to manufacture 'Asian American' situationally. It serves as the ethical grounds for the political practice of what I would describe as a strategic anti-essentialism" (Chuh, 401).

The film doesn't offer multiple "diverse" Asian American subjects—it **explodes the very possibility of Asian American as a stable subject position**.

---

### B. FORMAL STRATEGY #1: Multiverse as Anti-Essentialist Apparatus

**What the film does formally:**
- Rapid cutting between universes (chef Evelyn, movie star Evelyn, hot-dog-fingers Evelyn)
- Refusal to privilege ANY version as "authentic" or "real"
- The "verse-jumping" mechanic requires doing something "out of character"—literalizing how subjectivity is always a regulatory constraint

**Theoretical connection to Lowe's heterogeneity:**

Lowe argues that "heterogeneity, hybridity, and multiplicity are not used here as rhetorical or literary terms but are attempts at naming the **material contradictions** that characterize Asian American groups" (Lowe, 290, emphasis mine).

The multiverse structure makes visible what Lowe theorizes: that "Asian American" never refers to a unified subject but to **incommensurate subject positions** produced by:
- Different waves of immigration (Evelyn as post-1965 small business owner)
- Different class positions (laundromat vs. movie star universes)
- Different gender expectations (wife/mother vs. kung fu master)
- Different relationships to "homeland" (Chinese opera singer universe)

**Jaw-dropping insight:** The film's formal innovation isn't just creative—it's **epistemological**. It asks: what if we stopped trying to resolve these contradictions into ONE coherent "Asian American identity" and instead recognized that the **pursuit of coherence itself** is what Lowe calls "the aestheticizing commodification of Asian American cultural differences" (Lowe, 138)?

---

### C. FORMAL STRATEGY #2: The "Everything Bagel" as Nihilistic Critique of Liberal Subjectivity

**What the film does:**
- Joy/Jobu Tupaki creates the "everything bagel"—literally everything everywhere all at once, resulting in nihilistic meaninglessness
- This is the **endpoint of liberal multiculturalism's logic**: if everything matters equally, nothing matters

**Theoretical connection to Chuh's critique:**

Chuh writes: "Multiculturalism, contradictorily, attempts to retain a liberal conception of subjectivity while simultaneously claiming to take seriously radical critiques of precisely the liberal subject. In so doing, it **occludes and effaces the historicity of racism**" (Chuh, 214, emphasis mine).

The everything bagel visualizes this contradiction:
- It includes everything (multicultural inclusion)
- But flattens all difference into equivalence (liberal universalism)
- Resulting in nihilism/meaninglessness (the failure of representation politics)

**Critical juxtaposition (Week 1 concept):** The film juxtaposes:
- Joy's nihilistic "nothing matters" (liberal multiculturalism's endpoint)
- vs. Evelyn's "then I will choose to matter" (strategic essentialism/situational subjectivity)

This is NOT resolution through identity—it's what Chuh calls "imagining otherwise": "not about imagining as the other, but rather, is about **imagining the other differently**" (Chuh, 339).

---

### D. FORMAL STRATEGY #3: Transnational Failures and the "Perpetual Foreigner"

**What the film does:**
- IRS audit as bureaucratic violence against immigrant small business
- Evelyn's broken English, failed American dream
- Father Gong Gong's refusal to accept Evelyn's choices
- The laundromat itself as site of racialized labor

**Theoretical connection to transnationalism and empire:**

The film makes visible what Espiritu, Lowe, and Yoneyama theorize as "transpacific entanglements"—the ways Asian American subjectivities are produced through:
- U.S. imperialism in Asia (the context that produces post-1965 immigration)
- Global capitalist flows (the laundromat as low-wage service economy)
- Racial capitalism (Asian Americans as "differential inclusion"—included as labor but excluded from full citizenship)

**Differential inclusion (your existing term):** The Wangs are:
- Economically included (they run a business)
- But perpetually marginal (failing business, IRS audit, can't understand forms)
- And affectively excluded (Gong Gong's disappointment = the impossibility of "success")

**Connecting to Lowe on hybridity:**

"The materialist concept of hybridity conveys that the histories of forced labor migrations, racial segregation, economic displacement, and internment are left in the **material traces of 'hybrid' cultural identities**... Hybridization is not the 'free' oscillation between or among chosen identities. It is the **uneven process through which immigrant communities encounter the violences of the U.S. state**" (Lowe, 847-854).

Evelyn's multiverse hopping isn't freedom—it's the traumatic survival strategy of someone for whom NO subject position offers stability or recognition.

---

### E. THE "LIGHTBULB MOMENT" SYNTHESIS

**Here's where you blow your professor's mind:**

The film's genius is that it **formally enacts Chuh's theoretical argument**. Chuh writes:

> "Deconstruction takes place... It is an event that does not await the deliberation, consciousness, or organization of a subject... 'Asian American' is/names racism and resistance, citizenship and its denial, subjectivity and subjection—at once the becoming and undoing—and, as such, is a designation of the **(im)possibility of justice**" (Chuh, 308-314).

The multiverse structure IS deconstruction taking place:
- Every universe simultaneously affirms and negates "Asian American" as category
- No version of Evelyn achieves stable subjecthood
- Yet the film insists on mattering anyway ("This is how I fight")

**This is strategic anti-essentialism in cinematic form.**

The film doesn't resolve into:
- ❌ Nativism (return to "authentic" Chinese identity)
- ❌ Assimilation (become successfully "American")
- ❌ Hybrid resolution (celebrate "both" identities)

Instead, it holds Asian American **under erasure**—using it as necessary fiction while revealing its constructedness.

**Connection to Lowe's critique of generational conflict:**

Lowe argues that "interpreting Asian American culture exclusively in terms of the master narratives of generational conflict and filial relation **essentializes Asian American culture, obscuring the particularities and incommensurabilities** of class, gender, and national diversities among Asians" (Lowe, 132-134).

The film SEEMS to be about mother-daughter conflict (Joy vs. Evelyn). But the multiverse reveals this is actually about:
- Queer refusal (Joy's rejection of heteronormative marriage expectations)
- Transnational class anxieties (Evelyn's failed American dream)
- Gender under capitalism (Evelyn's unpaid care labor vs. Waymond's emotional labor)
- The impossibility of intergenerational transmission when there's no stable "culture" to transmit

**This refuses the "privatized familial opposition" Lowe critiques.**

### F. COUNTERARGUMENT & RESPONSE

**Potential objection:** Isn't this film still about representation? It features Asian American stars, was commercially successful, and many viewers celebrated it as "representation."

**Your sophisticated response:**

This is precisely the trap Chuh warns against. She writes that we must recognize "Asian American studies as unfolding within the spaces of the U.S. university, an institution... that 'continues to organize crucial social functions'" (Lloyd qtd. in Chuh, 508-509).

The film's commercial success and Oscar wins demonstrate what Chuh calls "the cooptability of the Asian/American dynamic"—how "Asian American" can be deployed "in the service of conservative ends" (Chuh, 496-497).

BUT: The film's formal radicalism—its refusal of coherent subjectivity—**exceeds its cooptation**. This is what Lowe means by Asian American culture as "site that shifts and marks alternatives to the national terrain by occupying other spaces, imagining different narratives" (Lowe, epigraph in Chuh).

**Key distinction:**
- Representation politics: "We need Asian Americans in Hollywood"
- Subjectless critique: "What if 'Asian American' as stable category is the problem?"

The film does both—and the tension is productive, not contradictory.

---

### G. CONCLUSION: Why This Matters Beyond the Text

**Returning to Chuh's larger argument:**

Chuh argues that Asian American studies must become "a field of **collaborative antagonisms**, collaborative in the doubled sense of working together and working subversively against, and antagonistic in the ways in which diverse approaches to knowledge critique and identify each other's limits" (Chuh, 1121-1123).

*EEAAO* models this:
- It collaborates with (uses) the category "Asian American"
- While antagonizing (deconstructing) its coherence
- Showing how difference is constitutive, not resolvable

**Final insight:** The film suggests that justice—what Derrida calls "yet, to come" (qtd. in Chuh, 30)—is not achieved through achieving stable subjectivity (citizenship, representation, recognition). Instead, justice requires what Chuh theorizes as "a persistent and principled crisis" of Asian American as category.

The multiverse isn't chaos—it's the **ethical practice of refusing to settle** on any single configuration of power/knowledge that would inevitably exclude some "others."

**This is what it means to "imagine otherwise."**
