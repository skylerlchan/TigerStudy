# Accelerating Learning and Eliminating School as a Bottleneck

## What's Likely Slowing You Down

Based on what I can see -- you're building software, managing engineering workflows in Linear, configuring AI tooling, running multiple projects -- you're operating like a builder. Builders learn by doing. School forces you into a consumption model: read, memorize, regurgitate on a schedule someone else set.

The friction isn't that you're slow. It's that school's input/output format is mismatched with how you actually process and retain information. You're probably:

1. **Context-switching constantly** between building (high-engagement, self-directed) and school (passive, externally-directed). Every switch costs 15-25 minutes of refocus time ([Mark et al., UC Irvine](https://www.ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf)).
2. **Using inefficient study methods** -- most school culture pushes re-reading, highlighting, and note-taking, which are among the lowest-yield learning strategies that exist.
3. **Bottlenecked on output format** -- writing essays and assignments longhand or even typing them is slow when your bottleneck isn't thinking, it's transcription.
4. **Carrying cognitive overhead** from managing two parallel lives (school obligations + projects you actually care about) without a unified system.

## The Science of Fast Memorization

There is serious research on this. The field is called **cognitive psychology of learning**, and the findings are clear and replicable.

### Tier 1: Highest-Evidence Methods

**Spaced Repetition**

The single most effective memorization technique with over a century of research behind it. Your brain forgets on a predictable curve (Ebbinghaus forgetting curve). Spaced repetition systems (SRS) show you material right before you'd forget it, which strengthens the memory trace each time.

- Use **Anki** (free, open source). It's ugly but it works better than anything else.
- For every lecture/reading, create flashcards immediately. Keep them atomic -- one fact per card.
- Review daily. 15-20 minutes/day can maintain thousands of cards.
- After 3-4 review cycles, material moves into long-term memory with minimal ongoing effort.
- Research shows SRS produces 2-3x better retention than massed study (cramming) for the same time investment ([Cepeda et al., 2006](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2006-10247-006)).

**Active Recall (Testing Effect)**

Testing yourself on material is dramatically more effective than re-reading it. Every time you successfully retrieve information from memory, the memory gets stronger. Every time you passively re-read, it barely registers.

- After reading a chapter: close the book, write down everything you remember. Then check what you missed.
- Use practice problems and past exams as your primary study tool, not review sheets.
- The discomfort of trying to remember and failing is the point -- that struggle is what encodes the memory ([Roediger & Karpicke, 2006](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2006-11628-002)).

**Interleaving**

Don't study one subject for 4 hours. Study 3 subjects for 80 minutes each, alternating. Your brain learns to discriminate between concepts and builds more flexible retrieval pathways. It feels harder in the moment but produces significantly better long-term retention and transfer ([Rohrer & Taylor, 2007](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2007-07951-015)).

### Tier 2: High-Evidence Methods

**Elaborative Interrogation**

For every fact, ask "why?" and "how does this connect to what I already know?" Forcing yourself to explain *why* something is true creates deeper encoding than just noting *that* it's true.

**Concrete Examples**

Abstract concepts don't stick. For every principle, generate 2-3 concrete examples from your own experience or projects. The act of generating them is what encodes the concept.

**Dual Coding**

Combine verbal and visual representations. Draw diagrams, create mind maps, sketch processes. Two encoding channels > one.

### What Does NOT Work (Despite Feeling Productive)

- **Re-reading** -- feels familiar, doesn't encode. ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266))
- **Highlighting** -- gives an illusion of engagement with almost zero retention benefit.
- **Summarizing** (passive) -- slightly better than re-reading but far worse than active recall.
- **Cramming** -- works for 24-48 hours, then it's gone. Worst ROI on time.

## Solving the Writing Speed Bottleneck

If writing speed is a physical bottleneck, there are concrete solutions:

**Voice-to-text for first drafts.** Speak your essays/assignments into a transcription tool. Most people speak at 130-150 wpm vs typing at 40-80 wpm. Use:
- macOS built-in dictation (Fn Fn)
- Whisper (OpenAI's model, free, runs locally) for higher accuracy
- Then clean up with AI

**AI as a drafting accelerator.** For school assignments:
1. Voice-record your thinking on the topic (5-10 minutes of rambling)
2. Transcribe it
3. Feed it to AI: "Turn this into a structured [essay/report/analysis] with proper citations"
4. Edit and refine the output -- this is where your actual thinking goes
5. Total time: 30-45 min instead of 3-4 hours

This isn't cheating if you're doing the thinking. The bottleneck you're describing isn't understanding -- it's transcription. AI eliminates the transcription tax.

**For exams/timed writing:** Practice with active recall (above) so you know the material cold. Speed of writing under pressure is a function of how quickly you can *retrieve*, not how quickly you can *think*. SRS makes retrieval near-instant.

## A Practical System for School Specifically

**Daily (30 min total):**
- Anki review: 15 min (maintains everything you've ever learned)
- After each class: 5 min to create Anki cards from key concepts
- Before bed: 10 min active recall of the day's material (close eyes, reconstruct)

**For assignments:**
- Voice-draft + AI cleanup pipeline (described above)
- Keep a running "assignment queue" in your task system so nothing surprises you
- Batch similar assignments together (all writing in one block, all problem sets in another)

**For exams:**
- Start Anki cards 3-4 weeks before exam (not the night before)
- Practice with past exams under timed conditions (active recall + interleaving)
- The night before: light review only. Sleep is more valuable than cramming -- memory consolidation happens during sleep.

**For reducing overall school overhead:**
- Identify which courses actually matter to you and invest deeply there
- For the rest, find the minimum viable effort for the grade you need
- Use AI to accelerate every piece of busywork that doesn't contribute to actual learning
- Strategic about what you commit to memory (Anki) vs. what you just need to pass through (AI-assisted output)

## The Bigger Picture

The real question isn't "how do I get faster at school." It's "how do I minimize school's claim on my cognitive bandwidth so I can allocate more to the things I'm actually building." The system above is designed for exactly that -- maximum retention per minute invested, minimum time spent on output formatting, and a clear boundary between "school time" and "build time" so you stop bleeding attention between the two.
