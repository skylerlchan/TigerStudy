## Discussion Prompt

Prompt: While we began the course thinking of how the global idea of Third World liberation helped to inspire the beginnings of Asian American Studies in the 1960s, we turn in this unit in the opposite direction: towards analyzing how *domestic* ideas of liberalism, liberation, and freedom have long informed US "benevolent" domination in Asia. We are not often taught these histories together, or only taught them piecemeal, or not at all. 

For Lowe, Espíritu, and Yoneyama, the *ideology* (ie. a structuring set of beliefs that can wield material power) of "American exceptionalism" is an important hinge point between US actions at home against domestic populations and abroad in Asia. What conclusions can we draw from this global argument—that Asians in the US and "Asians in Asia" are bound together under US power? Does this change how you understand our relation to the rest of the world?

**David Liu** (2026-02-16):

Espiritu, Lowe, and Yoneyama argue that the image of the US as a “benevolent liberator and reformer of the people” is used to justify its military expansion and occupation of other parts of the world, and that in doing so, America transformed freedom, an essential right, into a privilege (181). Although America was the one invading countries such as Vietnam and enslaving blacks in America, by also being the ones to rescue Vietnamese refugees and free black slaves, America was able to use its self-proclaimed image as liberators and saviors to relate emancipation with indebtedness, such that “to be free was to be a debtor” to the US (181). Through this argument, we can see how many Asians in the US and Asians in Asia are related to each other; many Asian Americans are refugees who never asked to be here, as in the case of the Vietnam war. Although they came here as a result of American military expansion, the US was able to frame the assimilation of Vietnamese refugees into American society as a benevolent venture, portraying the US as a “good refuge” that liberates and saves people around the world from their own war efforts (178). Asian Americans in the US are bound by the dominant American society and by their awkward mediating position in the binary of white/black race relations, while Asians in Asia, such as in Guam and Okinawa after WWII, are bound by either direct American military occupation or close proximity to American military bases. While the US generally portrays itself as the “good guys” whose military operations are almost a Machiavellian “end justifies the means” effort to liberate the rest of the world, we must realize that this portrayal is often false, and that US liberation and rescue efforts are sometimes only necessary due to US military expansion itself.

     These “national security imperatives” have led to the United States to invest more in the military and policing than social welfare (185). The privatization of health care and schooling and increased policing have disproportionately impacted people of color (185-186). 

     Thus, Asian people, whether in the United States or abroad, are connected through the history of United States actions displacing Asian people to come to the country. Expansion of militarist actions abroad has fueled domestic policing and a disregard for people of color wellbeing. These neocolonized spaces associated with heteronormativity and patriarchy that flourish are used as arguments for viewing various minorities as inferior (184).

**Ethan Do** (2026-02-16):

This text demonstrates how the same ideology of "American exceptionalism" underlies both the racial management at home and the imperial violence abroad. The crucial point is that these are not two separate systems but rather mutually constitutive: the "rescue" of Vietnamese refugees both legitimates the war that led to their displacement and turns military bases on stolen Indigenous land into a signifier of humanitarian refuge. The authors demonstrate how the discourse of "rescue, liberation, and rehabilitation" establishes indebtedness rather than obligation. They use Saidiya Hartman's ideas about how being "liberated" by the U.S. imposes an unpayable debt, which precludes the question of reparations. The Vietnamese refugees are forced to enact their gratitude, which legitimates American benevolence even as the bombs from the same bases (Guam's Andersen, the Philippines' Clark) destroyed their homeland. The "model minority" trope similarly locates Asian Americans as the grateful beneficiaries of American freedom, while forgetting the violence of exclusion, wars, and exploitation that defined Asian migration.

This is a radical shift in our understanding of our relationship to the world. We can no longer think of Asian American identity as merely domestic. It is formed through transpacific circuits of U.S. military power. The refugee route through bases (from Vietnam to Philippines to Guam to Camp Pendleton on Luiseño/Kumeyaay land) lays bare the interconnected dispossessions of Vietnamese, Filipino, Chamorro, and Native peoples. Several pressing conclusions follow: solidarity must be relational, not merely identitarian, acknowledging "incommensurable yet linked" struggles across the geography of U.S. empire. We must refuse inclusion on imperial terms. If Asian American "success" legitimates U.S. wars abroad, assimilation itself becomes complicit. Domestic racial justice is inextricable from anti-imperialism, as militarized policing at home and military bases abroad function through the same logics. Memory becomes resistance through refusing the "good war" narrative, tracing routes of violence, and connecting struggles. Liberation cannot be achieved through structures that claim to liberate us. The task is to imagine freedom outside the liberal-humanist frameworks (development, modernization, rescue) that have legitimated empire.

**Salvatore Palizzolo** (2026-02-17):

For Lowe, Espíritu, and Yoneyama, the US is particularly defined by its belief in "American exceptionalism," an ideology that motivates the types of actions it takes towards "Asians in Asia" and Asian Americans, making these two groups inextricably linked in their experiences that come about as a result of US power.

"Transpacific Entanglements" outlines that "American exceptionalism has rationalized U.S. military and capitalist interventions in Asia and the Pacific Islands as necessary for the 'national security' of the United States and for the humanitarian 'rescue' of Asian peoples" (176). On this note, the text explores the journey that millions of Vietnamese refugees took across the Pacific after the Vietnam War. Despite the fact that the US "spurred the refugee exodus in the first place," it was the US that gained the image of benevolent hero as it took refugees in to military bases in the Philippines, Guam, and California (180). Thus, even as the US "disavowed its histories of colonialism and military takeover," it was able to continue exerting global influence by touting its commitment to "the liberal tenets of freedom, consensus, private property, and self-determination" (181). The text explains that this "gift of freedom" creates indebtedness for the "liberated," maintaining a lack of agency for other nations, such as Korea after Japanese colonial rule.

Shifting to a domestic view, the text argues that American exceptionalism establishes a "neoliberal racial social ordering within the United States as well" (183). Asian populations in the US were racialized as "noncitizen labor and barred from the political and cultural spheres in the development of the national economy" until the social movements of the 1960s, after which the racialization of Asians took on the form of the model minority (183). Reflecting this, in a phenomenon labeled "rising Asia" by sociologist Hijin Park, discourse "racialized" Asian nations as "model" modernity nations. The text also shows that the violence used internally within the US in the interest of national security imperatives—seen in "policing, drug enforcement, immigration and border patrol, and prisons"—reflects the violence and militarism seen abroad during instances such as the Vietnam War (185).

I think it is important to understand from these arguments that the two groups—Asians in the US and "Asians in Asia"— are not so different from each other, and, further, that they are not so different from other groups subjugated under US racial empire. "Transpacific Entanglements" indicates that the characterization of Asians as the model minority in current times is "an index of the present U.S. settler racial order that reduces and dehumanizes blackness as surplus population, constructs Indigenous peoples as extinct or vanishing, and frames Muslims as threatening violence" (186). As the text explains, the US racial empire consists of "dynamic, multiple relations of rule and resistance," and so we must consider how American exceptionalism is weaponized against both Asian in the US and "Asians in Asia," as both instances contribute to the maintenance of the US racial empire (186). When considering our relation to the rest of the world, I think the idea Yoneyama provides regarding our approach to battling the US racial empire is one of the most important to remember: that anti-imperial endeavors can be furthered by a "critical juxtaposition" of the experiences and struggles of different yet linked groups in the Asia-Pacific region and on the US mainland.
