## Discussion Prompt

Prompt: Viet Thanh Nguyen makes the argument that Asian American racial identity has become *reified* (when, for example, human social relations such as "race" become treated as essential parts of human nature or the result of objective laws of nature) and thus commodified under global capitalism. This, in his view, has resulted in a binary choice between being assimilationist (model minority/a self-marketing "panethnic entrepreneur") and being an Asian American intellectual claiming oppositionality to power ("bad subject"/trading on the radical historical inheritance of the TWLF and 1968). What are some of the solutions he proposes to this limited choice? What is his assessment of the future of Asian America as a political category?

**David Liu** (2026-02-08):

Viet Thanh Nguyen argues that due to the differences in mobility, much of Asian America has become separated and “divided domestically into between the model minority and the bad subject” (21). While some Asian Americans, especially panethnic entrepreneurs, are flexible due to their economic and social capital, other “compulsorily mobile aliens” are not mobile by choice (21). With this increasingly polarizing separation, Nguyen argues that domestic citizens will be caught in between these two opposite categories and that Asian American unification is growing weaker without “the compelling narrative of assimilation and political resistance against exclusion” (19). In looking for a solution, Nguyen admits the limits of Asian American politics; he openly acknowledges that much of Asian America is open to commodification and may even benefit from it, and that the Asian American population has a very diverse “ideological heterogeneity” (23). He suggests that rather than trying to homogenize and unify baseline Asian American ideologies such as in strategic essentialism, we should instead “deliberately bring ourselves into a moment of crisis” and embrace our dissensus (23). By accepting our differences, Nguyen believes that we can better “articulate our political goals” and “move towards other constructions of political identity” that would be more useful in his eyes (23). However, Nguyen is very pessimistic about the future of Asian America as a political category, explicitly stating that this “world of strife” does not bode well for Asian America being a “political category with a clear ideological orientation” (28). While I agree that Asian Americans should not ignore their internal differences, I have a more optimistic view; I believe that if we learn more about each other and understand more about each other under the context of the common goal of gaining more political representation and power as a minority, Asian America will become more unified in the long run rather than fall apart.

**Skyler Chan** (2026-02-09):

Nguyen’s solutions to the limited choices include embracing what he coins “flexible strategies.” He posits that the current approaches of “resistance and accommodation are actually limited, polarizing options,” and that embracing “flexible strategies” can include Asian American experiences that do not fit the mold. And although “compliancy and accommodation” were and remain important political choices, by labeling them as survival tactics, Nguyen effectively dismantles the hierarchy between intellectuals and immigrants. Intellectuals should also begin understanding that advocating for Asian American studies is a form of “panethnic entrepreneurship,” as it is “reaping the particular and peculiar rewards of academia by using the symbolic capital of race and resistance.” As such, they should remove the moral high ground above “assimilating immigrants,” especially since intellectuals are also “selling” their race for professional advancement. While discussed more during discussions, having a unified “Asian American” front also silences ideological conflict. Nguyen claims that “By recognizing these limits and by encouraging a move to dissensus rather than consensus, we can deliberately bring ourselves into a moment of crisis, disrupting the equilibrium of racial identity and its commodification that we have begun to take for granted.” Such a shift will enable “ideological heterogeneity” and will allow for more realistic political goals that reflect a more diverse population.

The future of Asian American studies will shift due to cultural and societal changes. For instance, Asian Americans might play into the “model minority” trope by being “'negatively articulated' with whites, that is, against Blacks.” As Asian Americans become increasingly autonomous, their successes could be at the cost of cross-racial solidarity. He also sees that Asian Americans may be shaped by “panethnic entrepreneurs.” He predicts that “it may be the case that in the next few decades, some of the most important Asian Americans, in terms of transforming American perceptions of Asian Americans, will not only be Asian American intellectuals but also Asian Americans who are capitalists or who are unabashed panethnic entrepreneurs.” This will diminish the Asian American political category in the pursuit of economic mobility, shifting priorities and political goals toward more economic ones.

**Ethan Do** (2026-02-10):

According to Viet Thanh Nguyen, in current society, Asian American racial identity is seen as reified. Therefore, Asian American identity is able to be commodified in the global capitalistic society. Ultimately, this reification instigates a binary field in which Asian Americans can be situated: assimilationist model minorities/pan ethnic entrepreneurs or oppositional intellectuals/bad subjects who derive their legitimacy from opposition and the radical tradition of the Third World Liberation Front of 1968.

Nguyen offers a number of strategies to transcend this dichotomy. Firstly, he underlines the significance of developments that recognize flexible strategies in Asian American history and literature that are linked to accommodation and resistance. Secondly, he argues that there is a problem with regarding oppositional politics as inherently moral or good, contending that such a tendency overlooks how oppositional politics gets incorporated or mobilized by late capitalism.

He writes that there is a need to practice self-criticism among Asian American intellectuals. He is promoting an ethics after idealism, which calls for self-acknowledgment on the part of the intellectuals as to whether they are participating in the institutional and economic systems or denying such participation. This will allow the intellectuals to gain a better understanding of the political conditions of Asian American studies.

Nguyen critiques the idea of political consensus in Asian America. In this regard, political engagement is rooted in the recognition of internal differences in terms of ideology, class, and ethnicity. Since the idea of Asian American identity is rooted in both pluralism and capitalism, a level of skepticism by Nguyen is evident regarding the likelihood of radical social change through it. Nevertheless, the idea of embracing dissensus and crises enables the formulation of a future political identity with transformative potential.

In this piece, Viet Thanh Nguyen defines a binary that Asian Americans have to choose. On one side of the coin, it is this identity of assimilating and embracing the model minority stereotype. On the other hand, there is the “bad subject” (page 28) who is seen as resistant and a threat to the status quo and the racial hierarchy of the U.S. Ultimately, the bad subject is viewed as dangerous, and the model minority is viewed docile, sort of like the two polar binaries that Asian Americans can fit into. However, Nguyen proposes a solution to these limited choices, including dismantling this framework of categorizing Asian Americans. He explains that Asian American literature such as this peice have proven that these binaries fall through, as Asian Americans are multifaceted in their identities and experiences. According to Nguyen, Asian Americans “fluctuate between resistance and accommodation,” meaning critics should not decide if something is good only if they are resisting and bad if they are trying to fit in, rather people are weaving in and out of these choices, and this must be taken seriously (page 22). Additionally, Nguyen is doubtful of the future of Asian Americans as a political category, as the identity in his belief had become commodified in a capitalist society, although it has served some importance in terms of political organizing. Because of the many fractures that exist in the identity, Nguyen explains that an Asian American identity with a “clear ideological orientation is doomed.” (page 28) He explains that by “encouraging a move to dissensus rather than consensus,” there can be a “move toward other constructions of political identity.” (page 23) By accepting the pluralistic nature of the Asian American identity and experience, sufficient organizing tactics for political issues, for example, can persist and thrive, making more significant change.

**Bonnie Peng** (2026-02-10):

Nguyen is worried that Asian American identity, while once a tool for political survival, has become reified under global capitalism. It is treated as something stable and natural, and because of that, it is easy to commodify. Asian Americans are then pushed into a limited choice to either assimilate as a model minority or position themselves as oppositional intellectuals who claim resistance. Both rely on a fixed idea of identity. Nguyen’s solution is not to reject Asian American identity altogether, but to unstabilize it.

One way he does this is by challenging the resistance vs accommodation binary. He argues that Asian Americans have historically operated within capitalism while also resisting it, often at the same time which is ironic. Treating accommodation as failure ignores the reality that survival often requires flexibility. This matters because identity becomes weaker as a political tool once it is seen as “settled” or successful. When identity is packaged and sold like the expectation of a Japanese store selling sushi or a lifestyle for example, it feels less urgent

Nguyen also converses with strategic essentialism in a way. When you collapse all Asian experiences into one body that condenses race, class, gender, and sexuality creates the illusion of unity while hiding internal contradictions. Looking forward, Nguyen is pessimistic about Asian America as a unified political category. He suggests it may fracture rather than hold, but sees that instability as necessary for more honest politics.
