The dual utility of "militarized refuge(es)" is best illustrated first by the utility of refugees to transform the military failure into a success. As these refugees are positioned as the beneficiaries of American aid, this phrasing insidiously obfuscates the consequences of war, and hence as Espiritu describes, "ironically become the featured evidence of the appropriateness of U.S. actions in Vietnam: that the war, no matter the cost, was ultimately necessary, just, and successful." This, however, sets the stage for further war-making capacity as it creates a cycle allowing refugees to reinforce military interventions. The second utility is the erasure of the crimes of war. The refugees' "war sufferings remain unmentionable and unmourned in most U.S. public discussions of Vietnam" even though paradoxically, their existence was spotlighted as proof of American liberating intervention. With millions of gallons of chemical defoliation released, it is hard to forget such atrocities, but the US somehow turned the refugee suffering into the necessary "cost" for freedom.

The article proposes that instead of approaching refugees as a "problem," we should see them as the "solution" for the united States. The "problem" strips refugees from their ability to shape politics, making them seem as the inevitable byproduct of war and that their accidental appearance should be gratefully received. The "solution" allows a causal analysis which foregrounds how U.S. political and military actions abroad have produced the very refugee populations that they purport to save. This dismantles American imperial ideology and exposes the circular logic that explains America's continuous intervention into countries. Yet the more devastating insight is that this enables the US to maintain a military presence globally under the guise of humanitarian aid. By effectively manufacturing consent for intervention, America can position itself as savior of the world's downtrodden.
