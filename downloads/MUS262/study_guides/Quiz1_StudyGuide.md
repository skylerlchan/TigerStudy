## WEEK 1: Early Jazz (Origins & New Orleans)

### Key Concepts
- Jazz emerged from **New Orleans** around the turn of the 20th century, as a confluence of different musical styles
- Roots: African rhythmic traditions, blues, ragtime, work songs, European harmony
- African music = rhythmic; European music = structured/melodic; jazz = combination of both
  - Written notation and structuring phrases in 2–4 bars are European traits
- **"Blues could not exist if the African captives had not become American captives"** – Amiri Baraka, *Blues People*
- **Congo Square**: where enslaved people in 19th-century New Orleans could congregate to make music
- **Collective improvisation**: early jazz ensemble approach; trumpet, clarinet, and trombone formed the core (reduction of the marching band)
- **Heterophony**: New Orleans jazz principle — no one plays the same melodic line at the same time
- **Blues structure**: A, A, B form; each section = 4 bars (12 bars total)
- **Blues scale**: pentatonic scale + tritone
- **Call and response**: one of the biggest elements of jazz ("trading eights")
- In classical rhythm, beats **1 and 3** are primary; in jazz, **beats 2 and 4** are primary
- **Swing rhythm**: a dotted eighth note + sixteenth, or a triplet with a tie on the first two notes
- The "sound of surprise" – jazz at its best takes risks; now it's considered too "safe" after institutionalization (Gunther Schuller was one of the first to codify jazz)

### Key Figures & Facts

**Scott Joplin** – "King of Ragtime"; ragtime is a precursor to jazz, characterized by syncopated piano melodies; "Maple Leaf Rag" (1899, piano roll 1916) was one of the first sheet music hits to sell over a million copies

**W.C. Handy** – "Father of the Blues"; "St. Louis Blues" (published 1914, recorded 1922) is one of the most recorded songs in history; he popularized the blues form through written notation; highly composed, polyphony to monophony

**Freddie Keppard** – Famous Black cornet player offered the *first* chance to record jazz; refused because he didn't want people to copy his music; this opened the door for the ODJB

**Original Dixieland Jazz Band (ODJB)** – Recorded the **first commercially released jazz recording** ("Dixie Jass Band One-Step," 1917); an all-white band; the recording has marching band influence, clearly rehearsed, with little improvisation

**Jelly Roll Morton** – Creole pianist and composer; claimed to have invented jazz; Creoles were mixed-race (Black + European), often classically trained, but barred from white orchestras; "Dead Man Blues" (1926) opens with a dialogue mimicking African American speech; his recordings with the Red Hot Peppers were highly arranged yet swinging

**Bessie Smith** – "Empress of the Blues"; classic blues singer of the 1920s; "Backwater Blues" (1927) — two call-and-responses happening simultaneously; her ability to bend and slide into notes created a pattern that instruments would later mimic in jazz

**Louis Armstrong** – **The First Great Soloist** (per Schuller Ch. 3); trumpet/cornet virtuoso; transformed jazz from ensemble music to a soloist's art; moved jazz from the South to the North; **"Heebie Jeebies"** = the first recorded song with scatting; "West End Blues" (1928) opens with an unaccompanied trumpet cadenza — he was the first to play vibrato only at the *end* of the note, influencing every instrument since; played with his Hot Five and Hot Seven groups
- The **Harlem Renaissance** context: African Americans asserting intellectual identity; conflict between "old Negro" (jovial, subservient image) and "new Negro" (serious, professional)

## WEEK 2: The Big Bands / Swing Era

### Key Concepts
- Swing era: approximately **1935–1945**; swing was a reaction to the end of WWI — a celebratory tone
- Big bands: large ensembles (15–20 musicians), written arrangements, dance-oriented
- **Riff**: a short melodic idea, ~2 measures long; riff-based playing is central to Basie's style
- **Head melody**: improvising "off the head" — an unwritten arrangement worked out on the spot
- Kansas City swing vs. New York swing: KC was bluesier, riff-based (Basie); NY more compositionally sophisticated (Ellington)
- **32-bar AABA** form: the second most common jazz structure after 12-bar blues; B section = the bridge
- **Four on the floor**: bass drum hitting every quarter note; Basie liberated this to the hi-hat cymbal
- Swing was the first jazz style to achieve mass commercial popularity
- African American performers at venues like the Cotton Club were given agency on stage, then stripped of dignity the moment they stepped off

### Key Figures & Facts

**Duke Ellington** – If Armstrong gave jazz its soloist/improvisational form, **Ellington gave jazz its compositional art form**; bandleader, pianist, composer; resident at the **Cotton Club** (Harlem, 1920s–30s) — Black performers could perform there but not patronize it; dancers had to be lighter than a paper bag; the Cotton Club allowed Ellington to write abstract, non-functional music beyond just dance music; wrote for specific musicians in his band ("beyond category"); five distinct areas of his music: *song to accompany dancing, jungle-style, mood pieces, pop tunes, abstract musical compositions*
- **"Take the A Train"** (1941) – written by **Billy Strayhorn**; 32-bar AABA; drums playing four on the floor; became the band's theme song
- **"Cotton Tail"** (1940) – AABA + solo section; saxophone solo by **Ben Webster** (breathy tone, loved ballads); based on "I Got Rhythm" changes

**Count Basie** – Kansas City bandleader; born 1904, died 1984; focused on the **riff** (contrast: Ellington focused on complex texture); blues-based, riff-driven style; liberated the four on the floor from the bass drum to the hi-hat cymbal
- **"One O'Clock Jump"** (1937) – started as a head melody; guitar on every quarter note; blues melody; light, buoyant rhythm section
- **"Splanky"** – blues melody; the three-chord riff at the end became ubiquitous in modern popular jazz

**Lester Young** – Tenor saxophonist with Basie; **horizontal style** (plays melodic lines that float over the music); light, cool, lyrical tone — almost like an alto sax on a tenor; nicknamed **"Prez"** by Billie Holiday; hugely influential on later cool jazz; considered the "father of cool jazz"

**Coleman Hawkins** – "Father of the Tenor Saxophone"; nickname **"Bean"**; **vertical style** (played arpeggios, outlining the chords); "Body and Soul" (1939) is one of jazz's landmark recordings — almost entirely improvised, considered one of the first great tenor saxophone solos

**Benny Goodman** – Clarinetist; "King of Swing"; his **1938 Carnegie Hall concert** brought jazz to a concert hall setting for the first time; led integrated bands; Charlie Christian played electric guitar with him

**Billie Holiday** – Greatest jazz vocalist; horn-like but fragile timbre; nicknamed **"Lady Day"** by Lester Young; reinterpreted songs with deep emotional intelligence; "God Bless the Child" (1942) – co-written by Holiday; "Strange Fruit" – addressed racial violence

**Ella Fitzgerald** – "First Lady of Song"; had a **5-octave range**; started performing with a small band and became a superstar; scatting is a signature element (hear in "Blue Skies")

### Readings Covered
- Schuller, *Early Jazz*: Ch. 7 (Ellington)
- Szwed, *Jazz 101*: Ch. 14 (1925–1940: Swing)
- Jones, *Blues People*: Ch. 10 (Swing – From Verb to Noun) – how "swing" shifted from an action to a commodity/noun; commercialization and racial dynamics
- DeVeaux & Giddins, *Jazz*: Ch. 9 (A World of Soloists) – rise of individual improvisers within the big band context

## WEEK 3: Bebop

### Key Concepts
- Bebop emerged in the early **1940s** (New York, Harlem jam sessions – **Minton's Playhouse**)
- Just as swing was a reaction to the end of WWI, **bebop was a reaction after WWII** — jazz as serious art, not entertainment
- Deliberately **un-danceable**: meant to be contemplated, like going to the symphony to hear Beethoven
- **Contrafact**: writing a new melody over existing chord changes (e.g., "Ko Ko" over "Cherokee" changes)
- Shifted jazz from dance music to **art music** / "musician's music"; musicians seen as artists, not entertainers
- Small groups (combo) replaced big bands
- Characteristic techniques: chord substitutions (harmonic substitution), altered chords, fast tempos, complex rhythms, use of the **tritone** (called the "flatted fifth")
- **"Head – solos – head"** form: state the melody, improvise, restate the melody
- **Rhythm changes**: colloquial term for the 32-bar AABA chord progression from Gershwin's "I Got Rhythm"
- Upper chord extensions: 7th, 9th, 11th, and 13th tones used to create floating melodies (Parker's innovation)
- Bebop rhythm section: piano, bass, drums — became very prominent; instruments in **conversation** with each other (players hear something they like and respond)
- Jazz became plagued by the heroin epidemic — musicians wanted to emulate Parker and adopted his drug use

### Key Figures & Facts

**Charlie Parker** ("Bird") – Alto saxophonist; the central genius of bebop; at age 16, got a cymbal thrown at him by Basie's drummer for missing chord changes; locked himself in a room and practiced 16 hours a day for 4 months; nicknamed "Yard Bird" (shortened to Bird) after stopping a bus to retrieve a chicken he ran over; "Ko Ko" (1945) based on "Cherokee" chord changes — blazing bebop technique; "Moose the Mooche" (1946) — named after his heroin dealer; died at age 34

**Dizzy Gillespie** – Trumpet; co-creator of bebop with Parker; known for cheeks puffed like a bullfrog; his trumpet bell is upturned because someone knocked it off a stand; jokester, extrovert — the public face of bebop, opposite of Parker's stoic personality; incorporated **Afro-Cuban rhythms** into jazz (Cubop); virtuosic in the upper register; "Dizzy Atmosphere" (1945) with Parker

**Thelonious Monk** – Pianist and composer; house pianist at **Minton's Playhouse**; largely self-taught; played with flat fingers (gave a percussive sound); used tonal clusters and the damper pedal creatively; hugely idiosyncratic, angular style; didn't reach critical acclaim until the late 1950s; considered the next greatest jazz composer after Duke Ellington
- **"Criss Cross"** (1951) – per Gunther Schuller, the first jazz piece with musical coherence comparable to European classical music; features Milt Jackson on vibraphone
- **"Round Midnight"** – AABA with an introduction and coda

**Bud Powell** – The defining bebop pianist; influenced by Art Tatum; suffered from schizophrenia; was struck in the hand by a police billy club, damaging his playing; moved to Paris (racially progressive); most emblematic pianist of the bebop era
- **"Glass Enclosure"**: no improvisation — all through-composed; **first jazz piece to use slash chords** (a triad over a bass note)

**Kenny Clarke** ("Klook") – First bebop drummer; lighter texture on the kit; accented the bass drum in unexpected places — called **"dropping bombs"**

**Art Tatum** – Pianist; used stride technique with very melodic lines; predecessor of Bud Powell; was legally blind

**Jimmy Blanton** – Bassist with Ellington; first bassist to have a sound that dominated the band (hear in "Jack the Bear")

## LISTENING RESPONSE – How to Write the Paragraph

Since name, composer, and year are provided, focus your paragraph on **what you hear**:

1. **Instrumentation** – What instruments? Soloist? Ensemble size?
2. **Style/Era** – Does this sound like ragtime, swing, bebop? What clues tell you?
3. **Rhythm/Tempo** – Fast/slow? Swinging? Syncopated? Beats 2 and 4?
4. **Melody/Improvisation** – Is the melody stated? Is this an improvised solo? Angular or lyrical?
5. **Texture/Dynamics** – Dense or sparse? Polyphony? Heterophony? Loud/soft shifts?
6. **Emotional quality / cultural context** – What feeling does it convey? How does it connect to what you've read?

**Aim for ~5 strong observations** (per the instructor's hint of "aim for five points").

### WEEK 1 – Early Jazz

**Scott Joplin – "Maple Leaf Rag" (1916, Piano Roll)**
1. Joplin is known as the **"King of Ragtime."**
2. Originally published in **1899** as sheet music — one of the first pieces to sell over 1 million copies in sheet music form.
3. This recording is a **piano roll** (1916), not a live performance — it was played back mechanically on a player piano.
4. Ragtime is characterized by **syncopated melodies** against a steady bass line; it is a direct precursor to jazz.
5. Ragtime has **no improvisation** — it is fully composed and notated; this distinguishes it from jazz.

---

**W.C. Handy – "St. Louis Blues" (1922)**
1. Handy is known as the **"Father of the Blues."**
2. Originally **composed and published in 1914**; this is the 1922 recording.
3. Handy was **formally trained** and one of the first to transcribe and publish blues music in written notation — moving it from oral tradition to sheet music.
4. The piece has a **habanera (tango) rhythm** in its middle section, reflecting Cuban musical influence.
5. Handy was a **bandleader**, not a rural folk blues musician — he came from the professional, literate Black musician class.

---

**Original Dixieland Jazz Band – "Dixie Jass Band One-Step" (1917)**
1. The ODJB made what is widely considered the **first commercially released jazz recording** (1917, Victor Records).
2. The band was **all white** — they brought jazz to mainstream white American audiences.
3. **Freddie Keppard**, a Black cornet player, was offered the first chance to record jazz and **refused**, fearing people would copy his style — this opened the door for the ODJB.
4. The recording is **clearly rehearsed with minimal improvisation** and shows strong marching band influence.
5. The band was from **New Orleans** but made their commercial breakthrough in Chicago and New York.

---

**Bessie Smith – "Backwater Blues" (1927)**
1. Bessie Smith (1894–1937) was known as the **"Empress of the Blues"** — the most commercially successful blues artist of the 1920s.
2. "Backwater Blues" was inspired by the **Great Mississippi Flood of 1927**.
3. The piece features **two simultaneous call-and-responses** — one between the vocal and piano, another embedded in the vocal phrasing itself.
4. Smith's style of **bending and sliding into notes** created a vocal pattern that jazz instrumentalists would later imitate.
5. She recorded for **Columbia Records** and was accompanied by pianist **James P. Johnson** on this recording.

---

**Jelly Roll Morton – "Dead Man Blues" (1926)**
1. Morton (born Ferdinand Joseph LaMothe, 1890–1941) was **Creole** — mixed-race, often classically trained, but barred from white orchestras by Jim Crow.
2. He controversially **claimed to have invented jazz** — historically significant even if disputed.
3. "Dead Man Blues" opens with a **spoken dialogue mimicking African American speech** — an unusual theatrical device.
4. Recorded with the **Red Hot Peppers** for Victor Records; Morton's recordings were **highly arranged yet swinging** — a blend of written structure and jazz feel.
5. Morton was a pioneering jazz **composer/arranger** who brought written structure to early New Orleans jazz.

---

**Louis Armstrong – "West End Blues" (1928), Louis Armstrong and His Hot Five**
1. The recording opens with a famous **unaccompanied trumpet cadenza** — a landmark moment in jazz history.
2. Armstrong was the **"First Great Soloist"** (per Schuller, Ch. 3) — he transformed jazz from a collective ensemble art into a **soloist's art**.
3. He was the **first to play vibrato only at the *end* of a note**, influencing every instrument in jazz since.
4. The **Hot Five and Hot Seven recordings (1925–1928)** are considered some of the most important in all of jazz history.
5. "West End Blues" was written by **King Oliver**, Armstrong's mentor and the man who brought him to Chicago.

---

### WEEK 2 – The Big Bands / Swing Era

**Duke Ellington – "Take the A Train" (1941)**
1. "Take the A Train" was written by **Billy Strayhorn**, Ellington's longtime collaborator — not by Ellington himself.
2. It became Ellington's **signature theme song** after its 1941 recording.
3. The title refers to the **A subway line in New York City**.
4. It is in **32-bar AABA form** with drums playing **four on the floor**.
5. If Armstrong gave jazz its improvisational soloist form, **Ellington gave jazz its compositional art form** — he wrote for specific musicians in his band.

---

**Duke Ellington – "Cotton Tail" (1940)**
1. "Cotton Tail" features a famous tenor saxophone solo by **Ben Webster** (known for his breathy tone and love of ballads).
2. It is a **contrafact** — a new melody written over the chord changes of Gershwin's **"I Got Rhythm."**
3. Structure: **AABA + extended solo section**.
4. Ellington was the **resident bandleader at the Cotton Club** (Harlem, 1920s–30s) — Black performers could perform there but not patronize it.
5. Recorded for **Victor Records** in 1940.

---

**Count Basie – "One O'Clock Jump" (1937)**
1. "One O'Clock Jump" is Count Basie's (1904–1984) **signature theme**.
2. It originated as a **head melody** — an unwritten arrangement worked out on the spot.
3. Basie represented the **Kansas City** big band style — bluesier and riff-driven, as opposed to Ellington's compositionally sophisticated New York style.
4. Basie's style centered on the **riff** (a short, ~2-measure melodic idea) and a **light, buoyant rhythm section**.
5. Basie **liberated the four-on-the-floor beat** from the bass drum to the hi-hat cymbal.

---

**Count Basie / Lester Young – "Oh! Lady Be Good" (1936)**
1. Lester Young (1909–1959) had a **light, cool, lyrical tone** — almost like an alto saxophone on a tenor — contrasting sharply with Coleman Hawkins' heavy, vertical style.
2. Young's style is described as **horizontal** — he played long melodic lines floating over the music, rather than outlining chords (Hawkins' vertical/arpeggio approach).
3. Billie Holiday gave Young the nickname **"Prez"** (short for President).
4. Young's cooler, lighter style was a **major influence on the cool jazz movement** of the 1950s — he is considered a "father of cool jazz."
5. "Oh! Lady Be Good" is a tune from the **1924 Gershwin musical** of the same name.

---

**Coleman Hawkins – "Body and Soul" (1939)**
1. Hawkins (1904–1969) is known as the **"Father of the Jazz Saxophone"** — he essentially invented jazz saxophone playing; nickname: **"Bean."**
2. "Body and Soul" (1939) is considered one of the most important recordings in jazz history — Hawkins improvises **almost entirely without reference to the original melody**.
3. Hawkins' style is **vertical** — he played arpeggios, outlining the chords, rather than melodic lines.
4. This recording is an early landmark in **chordal improvisation** — improvising off the harmony rather than the tune.
5. It was recorded for **Bluebird/RCA Victor** in 1939 and became an immediate hit.

---

**Benny Goodman – "Sing Sing Sing" (1937)**
1. Goodman (1909–1986) was known as the **"King of Swing."**
2. "Sing Sing Sing" was written by **Louis Prima** but became Goodman's most famous recording.
3. Goodman's **January 16, 1938 Carnegie Hall concert** — featuring this piece — is often cited as the moment jazz achieved mainstream cultural legitimacy in a concert hall setting.
4. **Gene Krupa's drum solo** on this recording is one of the most famous in jazz history.
5. Goodman was notable for leading one of the **first racially integrated jazz groups** in the United States.

---

**Billie Holiday – "God Bless the Child" (1942)**
1. "God Bless the Child" was **co-written by Billie Holiday herself** — one of the few songs she composed.
2. Holiday (1915–1959) had a **horn-like but fragile timbre**; she sang **behind the beat** and bent notes in deeply personal ways.
3. Lester Young gave her the nickname **"Lady Day."**
4. Holiday was one of the first jazz artists to directly address racial injustice in music — most famously with **"Strange Fruit"** (1939), about lynching in the South.
5. The song's lyrics deal with **money, family, and self-reliance** — themes drawn from a personal argument with her mother.

---

**Ella Fitzgerald – "Blue Skies" from *Ella Fitzgerald Sings the Irving Berlin Songbook***
1. Fitzgerald (1917–1996) was known as the **"First Lady of Song"** and the **"Queen of Jazz."**
2. The *Irving Berlin Songbook* (1958) was part of a landmark series of **Songbook albums** Fitzgerald recorded for **Verve Records** covering the Great American Songbook.
3. "Blue Skies" was originally written by **Irving Berlin in 1926**.
4. Fitzgerald was famous for her **extraordinary vocal purity, precise intonation, and wide range** (reportedly five octaves).
5. She was renowned for her **scat singing** — improvising vocally using nonsense syllables — which is prominently featured in this recording.

---

### WEEK 3 – Bebop

**Charlie Parker & Dizzy Gillespie – "Dizzy Atmosphere" (Live), *Diz N' Bird at Carnegie Hall***
1. "Dizzy Atmosphere" was **composed by Dizzy Gillespie** (1917–1993).
2. The **Carnegie Hall concert (September 29, 1947)** was one of the landmark bebop performances — bebop entering the high-culture concert hall setting.
3. **Charlie Parker (1920–1955) and Dizzy Gillespie** were the two primary architects of bebop.
4. Bebop emerged in the early 1940s at **Minton's Playhouse** (Harlem) — as a reaction against commercial swing, reclaiming jazz as serious art rather than entertainment for dancing.
5. Characteristic bebop sounds: **fast tempos, complex harmonies, altered chords, use of the tritone ("flatted fifth"),** and virtuosic improvisation.

---

**Charlie Parker – "Ko Ko" (1945), *The Complete Savoy Recordings***
1. "Ko Ko" is a **contrafact** — a new bebop melody written over the chord changes of **"Cherokee"** by Ray Noble.
2. It is one of the most important bebop recordings — a showcase of Parker's **blazing technique and harmonic sophistication**.
3. Recorded for **Savoy Records in November 1945** — one of Parker's first sessions as a leader.
4. **Miles Davis** plays trumpet on this recording, before Davis's own rise to fame.
5. The tempo is extremely fast (~300 bpm) and it lasts **less than three minutes**, packing enormous density into a short performance.

---

**Charlie Parker – "Parker's Mood" (1948), *The Complete Savoy Recordings***
1. "Parker's Mood" is a **slow blues** — demonstrating that bebop was not only about speed, but could also be deeply lyrical.
2. It follows a **12-bar blues structure** in the key of C.
3. Recorded for **Savoy Records in 1948**.
4. Parker's nickname was **"Yardbird"** (shortened to **"Bird"**) — reportedly from stopping a tour bus to retrieve a chicken he had accidentally run over.
5. Parker **died at age 34** (1955) from health complications related to his severe heroin and alcohol addiction.

---

**Bud Powell – "Tempus Fugue-it," *Jazz Giant***
1. Bud Powell (1924–1966) is considered the **most important bebop pianist** — he translated Parker and Gillespie's vocabulary to the piano.
2. "Tempus Fugue-it" (also known as "Tempus Fugit") was **composed by Powell himself**; the title is a play on the Latin phrase *tempus fugit* ("time flies").
3. Powell **abandoned the left-hand stride patterns** of earlier jazz piano, using the left hand instead for sparse chord "comping" or sudden chord "bombs."
4. Powell suffered from **schizophrenia** throughout his life, partly attributed to a severe head injury from a police beating in 1945.
5. Powell eventually **moved to Paris**, which was considered racially more progressive for Black American musicians.
