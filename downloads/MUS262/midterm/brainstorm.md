# MUS 262 Midterm Paper -- Topic Brainstorm

## Paper Requirements
- 5 pages, double-spaced
- Analyze a specific movement or artist (Early Jazz through Modal Jazz)
- Formats: album critique, artist profile with stylistic analysis, or instructor-approved topic

---

## Topic Ideas

### 1. *Kind of Blue* (1959) -- Album Critique

**Angle:** How Miles Davis's *Kind of Blue* broke from bebop's chord-change-driven improvisation and introduced modal jazz as a new framework for spontaneity.

**Why it works:** The album is the best-selling jazz album of all time and represents the clearest single pivot point in the course material. You could analyze how "So What" uses the Dorian mode instead of chord progressions, contrast it with bebop's approach (Parker's "Ko Ko" running through "Cherokee" changes at breakneck speed), and discuss how the album freed soloists -- Bill Evans, Coltrane, Cannonball Adderley -- to explore melody and texture rather than harmonic obstacle courses. The course readings (Szwed Ch. 22-23, Berliner Ch. 3) directly support this. A potential risk is that it's the obvious choice -- many students may pick it.

**Sources to draw on:** Szwed Ch. 22-23 (Post-Bebop Improvisation; 1959: Multiple Revolutions), Berliner Ch. 3 (A Very Structured Thing), liner notes by Bill Evans.

---

### 2. Thelonious Monk -- Artist Profile with Stylistic Analysis

**Angle:** How Monk's angular, percussive, deliberately "wrong"-sounding piano style made him one of jazz's most original composers despite years of critical neglect.

**Why it works:** Monk is a fascinating figure who was present at the birth of bebop (house pianist at Minton's Playhouse) but didn't achieve recognition until the late 1950s. His flat-finger technique, use of silence/space, and dissonant tone clusters set him apart from every other pianist. You could compare "Criss Cross" (which Schuller called the first jazz piece with coherence comparable to European classical music) with "Round Midnight" (his most famous ballad) to show his range. You could also position him as the bridge between bebop and modal jazz -- his compositions anticipate the move away from standard chord changes. Less commonly chosen than a Miles or Parker paper.

**Sources:** Szwed Ch. 16-17, Schuller's analysis of "Criss Cross," Jones Ch. 12 (The Modern Scene).

---

### 3. The Evolution of Jazz Improvisation: From Collective to Solo to Modal

**Angle:** Trace the three major shifts in how jazz musicians improvise -- from New Orleans collective improvisation, to bebop's virtuosic chord-running, to modal jazz's open spaces -- arguing that each shift reflected broader cultural changes.

**Why it works:** This is a movement-based paper that ties together the full scope of eligible material. You'd argue that (1) collective improvisation in early jazz reflected communal New Orleans culture, (2) bebop's hyper-individual soloing reflected post-WWII Black artistic assertion (Jones, *Blues People*), and (3) modal jazz's open, meditative approach reflected a search for new artistic freedom after bebop's harmonic dead end. This lets you engage with multiple readings (Schuller, Jones, Berliner, Szwed) and multiple listening examples across all six weeks. Ambitious for 5 pages -- would need tight focus.

**Sources:** Schuller Ch. 1-3, Jones Ch. 3/10/12, Berliner Ch. 3, Szwed Ch. 12/17/22-23.

---

### 4. Coleman Hawkins vs. Lester Young -- The Hot/Cool Divide

**Angle:** Compare and contrast Hawkins' vertical/hot style with Young's horizontal/cool style, arguing that this split in the 1930s defined the two fundamental approaches to jazz saxophone that shaped everything after -- hard bop descends from Hawkins, cool/modal jazz from Young.

**Why it works:** The Hawkins/Young dichotomy is a core concept in the course. You could do close listening analysis of "Body and Soul" (1939) vs. "Oh! Lady Be Good" (1936), then trace their influence forward: Hawkins' vertical, arpeggiated approach feeds into bebop (Parker, though Parker synthesized both) and hard bop (Sonny Rollins, Coltrane's "Giant Steps"), while Young's cool, melodic, horizontal approach feeds into cool jazz and modal jazz (Miles, Bill Evans). This paper has a clear thesis and uses material from weeks 2, 3, 5, and 6.

**Sources:** Szwed Ch. 14, DeVeaux & Giddins Ch. 9/12, Jones Ch. 10, Monson Ch. 3.

---

### 5. Charlie Parker -- Artist Profile

**Angle:** Parker as the central figure who remade jazz improvisation, examining how his innovations (upper chord extensions, all 12 keys, rhythmic displacement) changed what was possible on the instrument -- and how his mythology (drug addiction, early death) has both elevated and distorted his legacy.

**Why it works:** Parker is arguably the single most important figure in the eligible material. You could analyze "Ko Ko" for technical innovation, "Parker's Mood" to show his lyrical/blues side, and use Jones (*Blues People* Ch. 12) to frame bebop as a cultural statement, not just a musical one. The risk is similar to *Kind of Blue* -- it's an obvious pick. But if done with genuine listening analysis rather than biographical summary, it can stand out.

**Sources:** Szwed Ch. 16-17, Jones Ch. 12, Berliner Introduction.

---

### 6. *Saxophone Colossus* (1956) by Sonny Rollins -- Album Critique

**Angle:** Analyze *Saxophone Colossus* as the quintessential hard bop album, focusing on how Rollins synthesized bebop's harmonic language with blues feeling and thematic (motivic) improvisation.

**Why it works:** Less commonly chosen than Miles or Parker. "St. Thomas" is on the listening list and is a great entry point -- its calypso melody shows Rollins bringing Caribbean roots into hard bop. You could contrast his "thematic improvisation" (developing motifs rather than running through chord changes) with Parker's approach, and argue that Rollins represented a third way between the hot/cool divide. The album also features Max Roach, connecting it back to bebop. Demonstrates engagement with Week 5 material specifically.

**Sources:** DeVeaux & Giddins Ch. 12, Monson Ch. 3, Szwed Ch. 20.

---

### 7. John Coltrane's Journey: From Hard Bop to Modal Jazz

**Angle:** Trace Coltrane's stylistic evolution from "Giant Steps" (1959) -- the ultimate expression of chord-change-based improvisation -- to *A Love Supreme* (1965) and modal/spiritual jazz, arguing this arc mirrors jazz's broader shift away from harmonic complexity toward spiritual and melodic exploration.

**Why it works:** Coltrane appears in both Week 5 (hard bop) and Week 6 (modal jazz), giving you material from both eras. "Giant Steps" pushes chord changes to their logical extreme (Coltrane changes), while "My Favorite Things" and *A Love Supreme* move toward modes, drones, and spiritual intensity. This is a compelling narrative arc for 5 pages. The risk is that *A Love Supreme* might be on the boundary of the eligible material (it appears in the Week 6 listening list, so it should be fair game).

**Sources:** Szwed Ch. 20/22-23, DeVeaux & Giddins Ch. 12, Berliner Ch. 3.

## Recommendations

**Strongest choices for a standout paper:**

- **Topic 2 (Monk)** or **Topic 6 (Rollins / *Saxophone Colossus*)** -- less obvious picks that let you demonstrate genuine engagement with the material rather than summarizing well-known narratives
- **Topic 4 (Hawkins vs. Young)** -- gives you a clear, arguable thesis and spans multiple eras
- **Topic 3 (Evolution of Improvisation)** -- the most ambitious; best if you want to show command of the full course arc, but requires discipline to keep it focused at 5 pages

**Safe, strong choices:**
- Topic 1 (*Kind of Blue*) or Topic 5 (Parker) -- proven subjects, but need original analysis to stand out
- Topic 7 (Coltrane's arc) -- compelling narrative, strong listening examples on both ends
