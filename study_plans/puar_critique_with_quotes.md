# Critical Engagement with Puar: A Quote-Based Analysis

## Introduction

Puar's *Terrorist Assemblages* offers crucial insights into the complicity between queer politics and U.S. imperialism. However, several claims warrant critical scrutiny. This critique engages directly with Puar's text, using her own words as the basis for questioning her framework.

---

## 1. The Paralysis Problem: What Politics Remain Possible?

### The Quote

Puar writes:

> "If we are to resist resistance, reading against these binaries to foreground a broader array of power affiliations and disaffiliations that are often rife with contradiction should not provide ammunition to chastise, but rather generate greater room for self-reflection, autocritique, and making mistakes."

And later summarizes:

> "Allowing for complicities signals not the failure of the radical, resistant, or oppositional potential of queernesses, but can be an enabling acknowledgment."

### The Critique

**"Resist resistance"?** This is a striking formulation, but what does it enable politically? Puar calls for "self-reflection, autocritique, and making mistakes" as outcomes of recognizing complicity. But for activists facing deportation, violence, or state repression, **how does "resisting resistance" translate into action?**

The phrase "generate greater room for self-reflection" positions political activity as primarily introspective and academic. While self-critique is valuable, movements also need to mobilize, organize, and strategize. Puar's framework excels at identifying what's problematic but offers little guidance on what affirmative politics might look like.

Moreover, if "allowing for complicities" is "enabling," **enabling of what?** The sentence structure suggests that acknowledgment itself is the political act. But acknowledgment without transformation risks becoming a comfortable academic posture—we admit we're complicit, sit with that discomfort, and then what?

**The stakes**: For communities under immediate threat, "resisting resistance" could be read as quietism. The theoretical sophistication may inadvertently produce political withdrawal rather than more accountable organizing.

---

## 2. The Generalization Problem: Which Queers?

### The Quote

Puar makes sweeping claims about queerness as a category:

> "This brand of homosexuality operates as a regulatory script not only of normative gayness, queerness, or homosexuality, but also of the racial and national norms that reinforce these sexual subjects."

And:

> "Queerness as automatically and inherently transgressive enacts specific forms of disciplining and control, erecting celebratory queer liberal subjects folded into life (queerness as subject) against the sexually pathological and deviant populations targeted for death (queerness as population)."

### The Critique

Notice the slippage: Puar moves from "this brand of homosexuality" (specific) to "queerness" (universal). **Which queerness?** She writes about "queerness as automatically and inherently transgressive" as if this is what queerness *is* rather than one ideological formation within diverse queer politics.

The claim that queerness "enacts specific forms of disciplining and control" is presented as a structural feature rather than an empirical observation about specific organizations, movements, or subjects. **Where is the evidence that this applies broadly?**

Puar provides examples—OutRage!, World Pride in Jerusalem, mainstream gay organizations post-9/11—but then extrapolates to make claims about "queerness" writ large. The gap between her specific examples (elite organizations, largely white formations) and her universal claims (queerness operates as regulatory) is significant.

**What about**:
- Queer abolitionist organizing?
- Trans justice movements?
- Queer immigrant rights work?
- Black queer organizing explicitly against homonormativity?

Do these all "erect celebratory queer liberal subjects folded into life"? Or does Puar's framework flatten the heterogeneity of queer politics to make her theoretical point?

---

## 3. The Agency Problem: Who Speaks for Queer Muslims?

### The Quote

Puar argues:

> "Queer secularity understands observance of religious creed, participation in religious public spaces and rituals, devotion to faith-based or spiritual practices, and simply residence within an Islamic nation-state [...] as marks of subjugated and repressed sexuality void of agency."

And:

> "The agency of all queer Muslims is invariably evaluated through the regulatory apparatus of queer liberal secularity."

### The Critique

Puar is absolutely right that queer secularity forecloses certain possibilities for understanding queer Muslim lives. Her critique of how western queer frameworks deny agency to religious queers is important.

**But here's the tension**: In making this argument, **doesn't Puar herself become the arbiter of what counts as agency for queer Muslims?** She tells us that leaning on religious community post-9/11 is agentic. That claiming "I am a homosexual also" challenges binaries. That observance of religious creed shouldn't be read as repression.

These are persuasive readings, but they're still *Puar's* readings. The quote claims "the agency of **all** queer Muslims" is evaluated through secular frameworks—but now it seems to be evaluated through Puar's anti-secular framework instead.

**What if some queer Muslims do experience Islam as repressive?** What if some do embrace secular queerness and view it as liberation? Puar's framework risks positioning these subjects as duped by Orientalism or false consciousness, thereby reproducing a different kind of denial of agency.

The broader question: **How do we critique oppressive discourses without ventriloquizing the subjects those discourses harm?** Puar makes queer Muslims the evidence for her theoretical claims about queer secularity, but this risks treating them as objects to think with rather than subjects with their own complex, potentially contradictory politics.

---

## 4. The Intersectionality Critique: Straw Person or Real Problem?

### The Quote

Puar writes:

> "Queer of color and queer immigrant communities (not to mention queer of color critique) are always beyond reproach, an untenable position given the (class, religious, gender-queer, national, regional, linguistic, generational) tensions within, among, and between queer diasporic, immigrant, and of color communities."

And:

> "Conundrums abound even with the fluidity of resistances and complicities, for intersectional models cannot account for the simultaneous or multifarious presences of both or many."

### The Critique

**Who actually claims queer of color communities are "always beyond reproach"?** This feels like a straw person. Most intersectional scholars—including Cathy Cohen, whom Puar cites approvingly—are deeply attentive to intra-community tensions and power dynamics.

When Puar claims intersectionality "cannot account for the simultaneous or multifarious presences" of complicity and resistance, **what is she basing this on?** Intersectionality as developed by Kimberlé Crenshaw and expanded by Black feminist scholars is precisely about how multiple systems of power operate simultaneously and in contradiction.

Puar invokes assemblage theory as an alternative, but **the distinction remains unclear**. She writes that intersectionality can't handle simultaneity, but doesn't demonstrate why assemblage can. Without more development, this reads as theoretical one-upmanship rather than substantive critique.

Moreover, by positioning queer of color critique as potentially regulatory (it holds communities to "impossible standards," makes them "always beholden to spaces and actions of resistance"), Puar may inadvertently weaken one of the most robust frameworks for analyzing race and sexuality together.

**The question**: Is Puar critiquing intersectionality as actually practiced, or a caricature of intersectionality that makes naive claims about pure resistance?

---

## 5. The Violence Quote: Whose Violence?

### The Quote

One of Puar's most striking claims:

> "Violence, especially of the liberal varieties, is often most easily perpetrated in the spaces and places where its possibility is unequivocally denounced."

### The Critique

This is a powerful insight about how liberal denial of complicity enables harm. But **the phrasing obscures important questions about scale and responsibility**.

When Puar writes that liberal spaces perpetrate violence "most easily," **compared to what?** Is violence in liberal queer spaces really easier or more significant than:
- State violence (deportation, incarceration, border militarization)?
- Economic violence (dispossession, labor exploitation)?
- Physical violence (hate crimes, police brutality)?

The formulation risks making liberal queer movements the primary site of violence in queer life, which seems empirically questionable. Yes, mainstream LGBTQ organizations can be complicit with imperialism and white supremacy. But **are they the worst perpetrators, or just the ones Puar finds theoretically interesting?**

Additionally, the passive construction—violence "is perpetrated"—elides questions of agency. **Who is perpetrating this violence?** Individual queer liberals? Organizations like HRC? The structure of liberal thought itself?

Without greater specificity, the claim becomes so abstract that it's hard to contest or verify. It sounds profound, but **what would count as evidence for or against it?**

---

## 6. The Secular vs. Religious Binary: Does Puar Flip It?

### The Quote

Puar critiques the secular/religious binary:

> "What is at stake in defusing queer liberal binaries of assimilation and transgression, secularity and religiosity?"

But also writes extensively about how:

> "The queer agential subject can only ever be fathomed outside the norming constrictions of religion, conflating agency and resistance."

### The Critique

Puar rightly identifies how queer liberalism positions secularism as the necessary condition for queer agency. But **does her critique simply invert the binary rather than dismantling it?**

Throughout the introduction, Puar presents religiosity (particularly Islam) as a site of resistance to queer liberal norms. The image she analyzes—"I am a homosexual also"—is read as "reclaiming" queerness through religion, "staging further defiance" against secular norms.

But this risks romanticizing religion as inherently counter-hegemonic while positioning secularism as inherently regulatory. **Isn't this just the mirror image of the problem she identifies?**

The call to "defuse" binaries suggests moving beyond them, but Puar's analysis often reinforces the terms of the secular/religious divide even as it revalues them. A truly post-binary analysis might ask: **Under what conditions do both secular and religious frameworks enable or foreclose queer life?** Rather than siding with religiosity against secularity, we might need more supple analytics that attend to the power dynamics within and across both formations.

---

## 7. The Biopolitics of Life and Death: Are These the Right Stakes?

### The Quote

Puar frames her analysis in terms of biopolitics:

> "Queerness as automatically and inherently transgressive enacts specific forms of disciplining and control, erecting celebratory queer liberal subjects folded into life (queerness as subject) against the sexually pathological and deviant populations targeted for death (queerness as population)."

### The Critique

This invocation of Foucauldian biopower—"folded into life" vs. "targeted for death"—dramatically raises the stakes. But **is this framework appropriate for analyzing the dynamics Puar describes?**

When mainstream gay organizations support Israeli nationalism or remain silent on Palestinian liberation, this is certainly a form of political complicity. But is it best understood through the life/death binary of biopower?

**The problem**: Puar's framework suggests that queer liberalism actively produces populations "targeted for death." But the relationship seems more indirect—complicity with, rather than direct production of, violence. The biopolitical language may overstate the causal role of queer politics in imperial violence.

Moreover, if we accept that queer liberalism helps determine who lives and who dies, **what's the alternative that doesn't?** Puar's critique implies the possibility of a queer politics outside biopower, but she never articulates what this would look like. If all formations of queerness are regulatory and biopolitical, **how do we escape the bind?**

The Foucauldian framework is theoretically sophisticated, but it may impose a structure that doesn't quite fit the political dynamics at issue. Not every form of complicity is biopolitical; not every political problem is best understood as life vs. death.

## 8. The Ascendancy of Whiteness: Whose Theory?

### The Quote

Puar builds on Rey Chow:

> "The ascendancy of whiteness is a description of biopolitics proffered by Rey Chow, who links the violence of liberal deployments of diversity and multiculturalism to the 'valorization of life' alibi that then allows for rampant exploitation of the very subjects included in discourses of diversity in the first instance."

### The Critique

Puar's deployment of Chow is insightful, but there's an irony here worth noting: **Who gets to theorize?**

Chow's point, as Puar notes, is that scientific observation creates "distinctions between subjects and objects (and populations), or, for Chow, between those who theorize and those who are theorized about."

**But isn't Puar doing exactly this?** She theorizes about queer Muslims, queer immigrants, queer people of color—using them as evidence for her claims about homonationalism and regulatory queerness. The queer Muslims in her text are quoted, photographed, analyzed, but they don't theorize their own positions within the framework she constructs.

This isn't necessarily a fatal flaw—all scholarship involves making others objects of analysis. But given Puar's attention to who gets to be subjects vs. objects, **there's a tension between her methodological practice and her theoretical claims**.

The ascendancy of whiteness operates through making some people theorizers and others theorized-about. Puar is attentive to this dynamic in queer liberalism but less reflexive about how her own scholarly position might reproduce it, even with the best anti-racist intentions.

## 9. The Empirical Gap: Theory Without Ground

### The Quote

Puar frames her project:

> "I explore these three imbricated manifestations—sexual exceptionalism, queer as regulatory, and the ascendancy of whiteness—and their relations to the production of terrorist and citizen bodies."

### The Critique

The language is telling: "explore," "imbricated manifestations," "relations to the production of." This is the language of theoretical exploration, not empirical demonstration.

Puar provides:
- One photograph (Poulomi Desai's work)
- Brief references to OutRage! and World Pride
- A few quotes from queer Muslims post-9/11
- Extensive theoretical argumentation drawing on Foucault, Agamben, Deleuze

**But where is the systematic evidence that these dynamics are widespread?** To substantiate claims about how "queerness operates as a regulatory script," we would need:
- Discourse analysis of mainstream LGBTQ organizations
- Ethnographic research in queer communities
- Historical analysis of how homonationalism emerged
- Comparative work across different national contexts

The theoretical apparatus is so elaborate that it risks **floating free from the material conditions it purports to explain**. Puar's claims become difficult to falsify—**what would count as evidence that homonationalism isn't dominant? Or that queerness isn't primarily regulatory?**

This is a common problem in high theory: concepts become so abstract and flexible that they can explain everything, which means they explain nothing specifically. Without empirical grounding, we can't assess whether Puar's framework describes widespread phenomena or relatively marginal elite formations.

## 10. The Audience Problem: Theory for Whom?

### The Quote

Consider the density of passages like this:

> "The fleeting sanctioning of a national homosexual subject is possible, not only through the proliferation of sexual-racial subjects who invariably fall out of its narrow terms of acceptability, as others have argued, but more significantly, through the simultaneous engendering and disavowal of populations of sexual-racial others who need not apply."

### The Critique

This sentence requires multiple readings to parse. Terms like "fleeting sanctioning," "proliferation of sexual-racial subjects," "simultaneous engendering and disavowal" demand familiarity with poststructuralist vocabulary and concepts.

**For whom is this text written?** If the goal is to challenge homonationalism in queer movements, shouldn't the critique be accessible to activists, organizers, and community members?

The communities Puar wants to defend—queer Muslims, queer immigrants, working-class queers of color—are least likely to have access to:
- The theoretical literacy required to read this text
- The academic institutional affiliation needed to access it
- The time and resources to wade through its density

**The irony**: Puar critiques elite cosmopolitan queerness while writing in a register that requires elite academic training. She analyzes how "queerness can be an elite cosmopolitan formulation contingent upon various regimes of mobility," but **isn't theoretical sophistication its own regime of mobility?**

This isn't to say scholarship should be "dumbed down." But there's a difference between necessary complexity and performative erudition. When Puar critiques accessibility and elite formations, **the medium becomes the message**—her own text reproduces exclusions it purports to challenge.

---

## 11. The "Also" Problem: Regulatory Frameworks All the Way Down?

### The Quote

Puar's summary:

> "Within that orientation of regulatory transgression, queer operates as an alibi for complicity with all sorts of other identity norms, such as nation, race, class, and gender, unwittingly lured onto the ascent toward whiteness."

### The Critique

If queerness is regulatory, intersectionality has blind spots, and even queer of color critique can hold communities to impossible standards, **what framework isn't regulatory?**

The logic of Puar's argument suggests that **all frameworks become regulatory**, including her own. She writes about how queerness "unwittingly" becomes complicit—but isn't assemblage theory, her proposed alternative, equally vulnerable to unwitting complicity?

**The performative contradiction**: If Puar is right that frameworks inevitably become regulatory, then her framework of homonationalism and regulatory queerness will itself become a new orthodoxy in queer studies (which arguably it has).

She acknowledges this partially: "Allowing for complicities signals not the failure of the radical, resistant, or oppositional potential of queernesses, but can be an enabling acknowledgment." But **this acknowledgment doesn't solve the problem—it just kicks it up a level**.

If we acknowledge our complicity and that acknowledgment itself becomes regulatory, do we then acknowledge the regulatory nature of acknowledgment? This risks infinite regress, where every critical move is immediately suspected of reproducing what it critiques.

**What's missing**: Puar needs to articulate what would make a framework *less* regulatory, or what political practice escapes the regulatory bind. Without this, her critique becomes so totalizing that it offers no exit.

---

## Conclusion: Taking Puar Seriously Means Questioning Her

The strongest engagement with Puar is neither dismissal nor worship, but **critical attention to her text's tensions and gaps**. These include:

1. **The action problem**: "Resist resistance" and "sit with contradiction" don't translate into organizing strategy
2. **The evidence problem**: Sweeping claims about "queerness" based on limited examples
3. **The subject/object problem**: Theorizing about queer Muslims while claiming to defend their agency
4. **The intersectionality critique**: Potentially a straw person; assemblage remains underdeveloped
5. **The scale problem**: Is liberal queer violence really the "easiest" or most significant?
6. **The binary problem**: Puar inverts secular/religious rather than dismantling it
7. **The biopower problem**: Is life/death the right framework for these political dynamics?
8. **The reflexivity problem**: Puar's own theorizing may reproduce subject/object hierarchies
9. **The empirical problem**: Theory without systematic evidence risks unfalsifiability
10. **The accessibility problem**: Critiquing elite formations in elite theoretical language
11. **The regulatory regress problem**: If all frameworks become regulatory, including this one, where does that leave us?

These critiques don't invalidate Puar's project—they're the price of taking her seriously. The real question is: **Can we build on her insights while addressing these limitations?** That would mean:

- Demanding empirical grounding for theoretical claims
- Asking what affirmative politics her framework enables
- Insisting on accessibility without sacrificing rigor
- Refusing both uncritical celebration and paralyzed self-flagellation
- Recognizing complicity *and* organizing for transformation

Puar is brilliant at diagnosis. The task is to think with and beyond her toward frameworks that enable both critique and collective action.
