### Artists Covered: Hard Bop & Soul Jazz

- Cannonball Adderley (Julian Adderley) — alto sax, born 1928 in Florida
- Nicknamed “Cannonball” (originally “Cannibal” — ate a lot)- Schoolteacher before moving to NYC in 1955; sat in with Oscar Pettiford at Café Bohemia- Joined Miles Davis 1958–59; left to form quintet with brother Nat (cornet)- Pioneer of soul jazz — fused hard bop/bebop with sanctified Black church music and blues- Died 1975, age 46, stroke (likely linked to obesity)- Instrument: King Super 20 saxophone — contributes to his distinctive “thick” sound
- Miles Davis Quintet (Prestige sessions, 1956)
- Recorded 4 albums over 2 days to fulfill Prestige contract before moving to Columbia- Lineup: Miles (trumpet), Coltrane (tenor), Red Garland (piano), Paul Chambers (bass), Philly Joe Jones (drums)- Mostly first takes; no editing — what was played is what was released- Recorded at Rudy Van Gelder Studios, Hackensack, NJ
- Van Gelder engineered the majority of the most important jazz records of the 1950s–60s
- John Coltrane — born 1926, North Carolina; raised in Philadelphia
- Started on alto, sounded like Parker; switched to tenor with Dizzy Gillespie (1950)- Joined Miles 1955; notable growth between first Miles record and the Prestige sessions- Kicked heroin habit ~1957; became deeply associated with spirituality- *Giant Steps* (1959) — first album of entirely his own compositions
- Chord progression divides the octave into 3 equal parts, linked by ii-V progressions- One of the hardest tunes to play in jazz- Tommy Flanagan (piano) widely criticized for struggling with the changes — not rehearsed beforehand- Flanagan re-recorded it as a trio in the mid-1980s to prove he could play it- *Naima* — ballad from *Giant Steps*, written for his first wife; played throughout his career until 1967- Died 1967, age 40, liver cancer
- Charles Mingus — bassist/composer, born 1922, Los Angeles
- Influenced by Duke Ellington; active in bebop from mid-1940s- Founded Debut Records (1952); released *Quintet at Massey Hall* (1953) featuring Parker, Gillespie, Roach
- Bass wasn’t captured in the original recording — Mingus overdubbed it at home- Known for “organized chaos” — collective improvisation, multiple soloists layering simultaneously- *Mingus Ah Um* (1959): most famous record; “Better Git It in Your Soul” features sanctified church and blues elements- Died 1979, age 56, ALS- Autobiography: *Beneath the Underdog* — mostly fictional, set in a therapist’s office; not recommended for learning about his music

### Key Listening & Discussion

- *Sack o’ Woe* — Cannonball Adderley at the Lighthouse, 1960
- Class noted: churchy piano voicings, audience yelling out (preacher/congregation dynamic), clapping on the four, shift from swing to straight eights, return to the blues- Cannonball’s sound described as “bright but thick” — contrasts with Parker’s grainier tone
- *Relaxin’* — Miles Davis Quintet (Prestige)
- Based on “I Got Rhythm”; harmon mute synonymous with Miles’s sound- Class noted: Paul Chambers as standout bassist; solos exposed by band dropping out; Coltrane’s tone shift toward the end
- *Giant Steps* — Coltrane
- Class noted: Coltrane “blasting off,” Flanagan’s minimalist/lost solo as unintentional contrast; Art Taylor (drums) comfortable despite speed
- *Better Git It in Your Soul* — Mingus
- Class noted: big sound despite small group, driving triplet feel, celebratory energy, trombone stabs over saxophone solo, reminiscent of swing era
