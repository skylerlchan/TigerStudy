## Summary

<h3>Jazz in the 1980s - Hard Bop Resurgence</h3>
<ul>
<li>1970s context: Free jazz declined in audience, civil rights era ended, black power movement embraced funk/R&amp;B over jazz</li>
<li>Dexter Gordon’s 1976 return from Europe (14 years in Paris/Copenhagen) sparked hard bop revival
<ul>
<li>Village Vanguard performances shocked audiences with pristine bebop sound</li>
<li>“Homecoming” album featured “Gingerbread Boy” with Woody Shaw</li>
<li>Sound retained 50s/60s hard bop core but incorporated free jazz emotional expression techniques</li>
<li>Used varied saxophone tones, altissimo register screaming/wailing</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
<h3>Miles Davis vs. Wynton Marsalis Conflict</h3>
<ul>
<li>Miles Davis trajectory:
<ul>
<li>Stopped playing trumpet 1976-1980 (“stopped hearing music”)</li>
<li>Returned playing instrumental pop (Michael Jackson covers)</li>
<li>“Human Nature” cover (1985) influenced by wife Cicely Tyson</li>
<li>Criticized as “smooth jazz” sellout, defended as musical evolution</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Wynton Marsalis emergence:
<ul>
<li>Born 1961, New Orleans musical family (father Ellis, brothers Branford/Delfeayo/Jason)</li>
<li>Juilliard classical training + Art Blakey Jazz Messengers experience</li>
<li>Columbia Records contract at age 19 (1980)</li>
<li>First musician to win Grammy in both jazz and classical same year (1983)</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
<h3>Wynton’s Musical Philosophy and Impact</h3>
<ul>
<li>Conservative approach vs. Miles’ forward movement philosophy
<ul>
<li>Criticized fusion, free jazz, popular music, rap as degenerate</li>
<li>Viewed jazz as structured “like a clock” - everything has precise place</li>
<li>Represented new African-American artist ideal: suited, articulate, institutionally embraced</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Famous confrontation: Interrupted Miles’ performance mid-solo, was cursed out and ejected</li>
<li>Key recordings analyzed:
<ul>
<li>“No Smoking” (1983) - Grammy-winning jazz performance</li>
<li>“Delfeayo’s Dilemma” (1985) - influenced by Miles’ second quintet</li>
<li>“Autumn Leaves” - later conservative period arrangement</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
<h3>Institutional Jazz Movement</h3>
<ul>
<li>First generation of university jazz programs (1970s)</li>
<li>Wynton appointed Lincoln Center Jazz artistic director (1991-2024, recently stepped down)</li>
<li>First jazz Pulitzer Prize winner: “Blood on the Fields” (1997)</li>
<li>Brother Branford and pianist Kenny Kirkland left band to join Sting after “Black Codes” album
<ul>
<li>Reportedly broke Wynton’s heart, never fully recovered</li>
<li>Shifted to more conservative Duke Ellington/Louis Armstrong influences</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Movement labeled “neoclassical” or “neoconservative”</li>
</ul>
