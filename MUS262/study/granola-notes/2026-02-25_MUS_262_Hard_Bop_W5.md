## Summary Rewrite

### Birth of the Cool Movement

- Miles Davis collaboration with arranger Gil Evans on flagship cool jazz album “Birth of the Cool” (1949)
- Revolutionary departure from bebop’s intensity and speed- Miles wanted to “shake people’s ears more gently” compared to Charlie Parker’s “furious” playing style- Miles said he “wanted to quit every night” when playing with Parker due to the intensity
- Musical characteristics:
- Slower tempo, gentler approach than bebop- More melodic and lyrical solos with deliberate pauses- Lighter tone, more singable melodies- Miles used muted trumpet for mellow sound with strategic spacing
- Instrumentation and arrangement:
- Nonet (9-piece band): trombone, French horn, tuba, alto sax, baritone sax- Gil Evans’ orchestral approach to jazz arrangement- Integration of improvisation with pre-composed material (harking back to big band era)- Nothing really left middle register, creating laid-back feel
- Social context:
- Miles criticized for hiring white musicians (Lee Konitz on alto sax, Gerry Mulligan)- Defended stance: “I don’t care what color someone is. If they can play, they can play. I don’t care if they have green breath with red tongue”- Significant position in 1949, before civil rights era
- Musical innovation:
- Gil Evans said Miles “changed the sound of the trumpet” in jazz- Shifted from bright, strident Armstrong/Dizzy Gillespie style to laid-back, warm, middle register sound- Miles played middle register “like every note could play an octave lower”

### Jeru Analysis

- Composed by Gerry Mulligan from “Birth of the Cool” album
- Musical characteristics:
- Faster tempo than other cool jazz tracks but maintained restraint- Still closer to bebop rhythm but gentler overall approach- More legato phrasing contributed to cooler aesthetic- Restraint primarily in volume rather than speed
- Orchestration:
- Brass section (tuba, French horn, trombone) created lush, warm feeling- Orchestral sound with integrated ensemble playing- Smooth, accented phrasing throughout
- Relationship to bebop:
- Took “DNA from bebop” and transposed to slower tempo- Allowed all musical pieces to emerge clearly- Maintained lyrical complexity in solos and orchestration

### Sonny Rollins - Hard Bop Pioneer

- Personal background:
- Born 1930, still alive at age 95 (as of class date)- Tenor saxophonist from New York City- First recordings with Bud Powell in 1949 at age 19- Became lead tenor voice in 1950s jazz scene
- Musical influence and style:
- Disciple of Miles Davis in hard bop school- Influenced by Coleman Hawkins’ warm, big, gruff, romantic tenor sound- Charlie Parker was “their God” according to Rollins- Transferred Parker’s language and fluidity to tenor saxophone
- Professional recognition:
- When John Coltrane first played with Miles (Rollins was in Miles’s band), Miles said “Sonny Rollins set John Coltrane’s ass on fire”- Brilliant composer in addition to saxophone mastery
- Recommended listening:
- “Collector’s Items” - session with Charlie Parker and Miles Davis (more obscure but significant)

### St. Thomas

- Composition details:
- Sonny Rollins’ most famous composition (recorded 1956)- Melody learned from his mother who had lineage from St. Thomas- Personal tribute incorporating Caribbean heritage
- Personnel:
- Sonny Rollins (tenor saxophone)- Doug Watkins (bass)- Max Roach (drums)- Piano player (mentioned but not named in transcript)
- Musical characteristics:
- Caribbean/Afro-Latin beat incorporated into swing- More relaxed field than former bebop but maintained technical flair- Economical, fragmented solo style similar to Count Basie’s approach- Bass line alternated between two notes rather than traditional walking bass- Drums tuned melodically to match key of the piece- Volume dynamics: drums dropped during piano solo (sensitive accompaniment)
- Performance style:
- Rollins’ open, sonic approach to saxophone playing- Technical complexity while maintaining groove-based foundation- Integration of Caribbean sounds with hard bop language

### Blues March - Art Blakey

- Art Blakey background:
- Born 1919 Pittsburgh, died 1990 New York (age 71)- Led Jazz Messengers for 35 years starting 1954- Co-led initially with pianist Horace Silver- Major talent scout in jazz history- Bebop drummer with Thelonious Monk band 1944-1947
- Performance characteristics:
- Known as possibly “loudest drummer in jazz history”- Signature press roll between solos (personal stamp)- Incredible feel and phenomenal technique
- “Blues March” (1958) from album “Moaning”:
- Personnel: Lee Morgan (trumpet), Benny Golson (tenor sax), Bobby Timmons (piano), Jimmy Merritt (bass), Art Blakey (drums)- Musical approach: play on march rhythm emphasizing 1 and 3 vs typical jazz 2 and 4- Militaristic, disciplined drumming approach- Solos very much “on the nanodrome” (structured)- High-hat work reminiscent of earlier jazz styles
- Jazz Messengers legacy:
- 35-year talent development program- Notable alumni: Wayne Shorter, Lee Morgan, Curtis Fuller, Wynton Marsalis, Branford Marsalis, Terence Blanchard- Artists got “feet wet” in jazz through Blakey’s mentorship

### Hard Bop Movement Context

- Historical positioning:
- Revival of bebop with “hotter” edge by mid-1950s- East coast school in “direct opposition” to cool jazz’s “arid precincts”- Miles Davis switched direction in 1954, put off by “overintellectualized” cool jazz derivatives
- Musical characteristics:
- Reflected “intensity and Houston temple of urban city life”- Walked harmonic complexity down in favor of simpler chord elements- Incorporated elements from sanctified church and rhythm & blues- Created “soul jazz” subset of hard bop
- Cultural significance:
- Made jazz more accessible and listenable for wider audiences- Competing with early rock and roll in early 1950s- Musicians wanted people to “groove to and dance to and have a good time”- Long-playing record era: recordings getting longer (Rollins record was 7 minutes)

### Song for My Father - Horace Silver

- Horace Silver background:
- Born 1928 Connecticut, died 2014 New York (age 85)- Joined Stan Getz band in 1950- Co-founded Jazz Messengers with Art Blakey in 1954- Left in 1956 to create own groups
- “Song for My Father” (1964):
- Title track from album of same name- Tribute to his father from Cape Verde- Quintessential hard bop track alongside “St. Thomas” and “Blues March”
- Musical characteristics:
- Latin/Afro-Latin beat throughout- Repetitive, simple bass line (not walking bass, alternated between notes)- Piano comping throughout saxophone solo with precise triple thirds- More pointed, percussive piano style vs smoother approaches- Positive, groove-oriented feel with deliberate pauses in solos- All solos played around the pause structure
- Performance details:
- Personnel: Horace Silver (piano/composer), Carmell Jones (trumpet), Joe Henderson (tenor sax), Teddy Smith (bass), Roger Humphries (drums)
