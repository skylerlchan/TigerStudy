## Summary

### 1959 as a Jazz Turning Point

- Three landmark 1959 albums each resolved bebop’s tension between structure and freedom in different ways:
- *Kind of Blue* (Miles Davis) — modal jazz; scales replace chord progressions, giving musicians more expressive freedom- *Giant Steps* (Coltrane) — opposite approach; extremely fast, complex chord changes cycling through multiple keys- *Time Out* (Dave Brubeck) — experimental time signatures, groove-focused rather than harmony-focused
- Pre-1959 bebop increasingly felt like it prioritized technical speed over expression
- 1959 didn’t push jazz in one direction — it diverged into multiple paths simultaneously

### Louis Armstrong & the Rise of the Soloist

- *West End Blues* (1928) considered one of the most important recordings in jazz history
- Armstrong shifted jazz from collective improvisation (everyone equal) to soloist-centered performance
- Opening cadenza of *West End Blues* — no rhythm section, no harmony, just Armstrong alone — established the soloist as leader from the first note
- Earlier recordings (*Heebie Jeebies*, 1926) show scat as rhythmic/melodic expression, not lyric-focused
- *Weather Bird* (with Earl Hines) shows flexible, interactive rhythm — timing shifts, phrases overlap, no fixed structure
- By *West End Blues*, all these ideas are fully realized in one piece

### Jazz & Black Liberation

- Hard bop and free jazz emerged as the soundtrack to the civil rights movement (1950s–60s)
- Darker, heavier, more impassioned sound; also known as avant-garde jazz- Emphasis on going outside traditional jazz boundaries — freedom reflected in the music itself
- John Coltrane (*Alabama*, written in response to the 16th Street Baptist Church bombing, 1963)
- Somber tone mimics a sermon structure — quieter opening building to intense emotional climax
- Sonny Rollins (*Freedom Suite*, from album *Freedom*, ~1958)
- Longer-form composition intertwining structured melody sections with improvisation- Max Roach on drums — aggressive, prominent role, drums moving beyond purely supportive function
- Philip Larkin (white English poet/jazz critic) criticized jazz becoming “excessively coded Black”
- Illustrates how jazz was becoming racialized — shifting from a shared genre to one interpreted as distinctly Black

### Pharoah Sanders & D’Angelo

- Pharoah Sanders — born Little Rock, Arkansas, 1940; died 2022 (age 82)
- Started on clarinet (~age 10, bought one for $17 from a church member); later switched to tenor sax- Moved to New York in 1962 to “learn the scales” — notably humble about his own ability- Joined Coltrane’s group ~1964 after Coltrane heard his debut album; key figure in free jazz development- Defining period: 1969–1973, ~11 albums (mostly for Impulse) — fused free jazz intensity with meditative, spiritual pacing- Later 1970s shifted toward a funkier sound; known for leaving long silences mid-set- Final album: *Promises* (2021, with Floating Points and London Symphony Orchestra)
- D’Angelo — born mid-1970s, Virginia; died ~4 months ago, age 51 (cancer)
- Not a jazz musician, but heavily jazz-influenced; credited with creating neo-soul- *Brown Sugar* (1995) — largely self-recorded; brought hip hop and R&B together with gospel influence- *Voodoo* (2000, Grammy-winning) — featured Questlove, Pino Palladino, Roy Hargrove- Retreated from public life after *Voodoo*; gained weight, was arrested, went through turbulent period- *Black Messiah* (2014) — released in response to the killing of Trayvon Martin; Grammy-winning- Mixed vocals low intentionally — subversive choice; cover features raised fists, echoing civil rights imagery- w
