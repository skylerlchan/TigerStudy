### Q-TYPE 1: Three-Week Comparisons (HIGHEST PRIORITY - ~30% of quiz)

**Q: How does Hard Bop (Week 5) differ from Modal Jazz (Week 6)? (Two differences)**

A:
1. **Harmony:** Hard Bop uses complex, rapid chord changes; Modal Jazz uses minimal harmony (one or two modes per section)
2. **Rhythm/Feel:** Hard Bop emphasizes strong, locked-in groove; Modal Jazz has more spacious, floating feel

**Q: What are two musical differences between Modal Jazz (Week 6) and Free Jazz (Week 7)?**

A:
1. **Harmony:** Modal Jazz uses modes/scales as structure; Free Jazz has no predetermined harmony (atonal)
2. **Form:** Modal Jazz still has structure (modes, pulse); Free Jazz abandons all predetermined forms

**Q: How does Free Jazz (Week 7) differ from Hard Bop (Week 5)? (Two ways)**

A:
1. **Rhythm:** Hard Bop has strong, steady groove; Free Jazz often has no steady pulse (free time)
2. **Structure:** Hard Bop uses traditional forms (AABA, blues); Free Jazz abandons all fixed forms

**Q: Compare the role of the rhythm section across Weeks 5, 6, and 7**

A:
- **Week 5 (Hard Bop):** Locked-in groove; walking bass; driving, interactive drums
- **Week 6 (Modal Jazz):** Spacious, supportive; pedal tones/ostinatos; subtle drumming
- **Week 7 (Free Jazz):** Free, textural; no walking bass; non-metrical drums; no timekeeping function

**Q: How did Modal Jazz (Week 6) emerge from Hard Bop (Week 5)?**

A: Musicians sought freedom from Hard Bop's complex chord progressions. Modal jazz simplified harmony by using modes/scales instead of rapid chord changes, allowing more space for melodic exploration and contemplation. Miles Davis and John Coltrane led this shift.

**Q: Why did Free Jazz (Week 7) emerge from Modal Jazz (Week 6)?**

A: Even modal structures felt limiting to some musicians. In the Civil Rights era context, they sought complete artistic freedom by abandoning all predetermined structures - no harmony, no form, no meter. Musical freedom reflected social/political freedom.

### Q-TYPE 2: Reading Concepts (VERY IMPORTANT - Know These!)

**Q: What does Ingrid Monson mean by "saying something" in jazz? (From *Saying Something*, Chapter 3)**

A: Monson describes how jazz musicians communicate meaning through their playing - "saying something" means playing with emotional depth, cultural references, and conversational quality. It's about musical language and how musicians interact, respond, and express cultural identity through improvisation.

**Q: What does Paul Berliner mean by "thinking in jazz"? (From *Thinking In Jazz*, Chapter 3)**

A: Berliner describes how jazz musicians develop an internalized vocabulary of patterns, phrases, and harmonic concepts that become second nature. "Thinking in jazz" means having a mental library of musical ideas that can be spontaneously recalled and recombined during improvisation - it's learned through intensive practice and listening. He calls it "A Very Structured Thing."

**Q: According to Szwed, what is "hard bop"? (Chapter 20)**

A: Hard bop is a style that emerged in the mid-1950s as a reaction to cool jazz. It took bebop's foundation and added influences from gospel, blues, and R&B, creating a funkier, more soulful sound. It was particularly associated with Black musicians on the East Coast and emphasized groove and accessibility while maintaining bebop's virtuosity.

**Q: What is "soul jazz" according to Szwed? (Chapter 20)**

A: Soul jazz is a substyle of hard bop that particularly emphasized funky, gospel-influenced grooves. It was closely tied to Black church music and R&B, featuring simpler harmonies, repeated riffs, and organ-based groups. Artists like Horace Silver epitomized this style.

**Q: What innovations did 1959 bring to jazz according to Szwed? (Chapter 23: "1959: Multiple Revolutions")**

A:
1. **Modal jazz:** *Kind of Blue* used modes instead of chord changes as primary harmonic structure
2. **Spontaneity:** Most tracks were first takes with minimal rehearsal; created a spacious, contemplative sound that became hugely influential

### Q-TYPE 3: Compare/Contrast Musical Characteristics

**Q: How does Hard Bop differ from Bebop musically? (Two differences)**

A:
1. **Tempo/Feel:** Hard Bop is generally slower and more groove-oriented than bebop's blazing fast tempos; emphasizes a funkier, blues-based feel
2. **Influences:** Hard Bop incorporated gospel, blues, and R&B influences, making it more accessible and "soulful" compared to bebop's purely virtuosic approach

**Q: What are two musical differences between Modal Jazz and Hard Bop?**

A:
1. **Harmonic approach:** Modal jazz uses scales/modes as the basis for improvisation rather than rapid chord changes; Hard Bop still uses complex chord progressions
2. **Texture:** Modal jazz often features simpler, more spacious accompaniment allowing soloists more freedom; Hard Bop has denser, more active rhythm sections

**Q: How does Modal Jazz differ from Bebop in terms of improvisation?**

A:
1. **Harmonic basis:** Modal jazz improvises over scales/modes; Bebop improvises over rapidly changing chord progressions
2. **Speed/space:** Modal jazz allows for more space and contemplation; Bebop emphasizes virtuosic speed and complex harmony

**Q: What are two ways Free Jazz broke from traditional jazz conventions?**

A:
1. **Harmony:** Free jazz abandoned traditional chord progressions and tonal centers entirely
2. **Form:** Free jazz rejected standard song forms (AABA, 12-bar blues) in favor of collective improvisation and open structures

### Q-TYPE 4: Historical Evolution

**Q: How did bebop evolve into Hard Bop in the 1950s? (Two ways)**

A:
1. **Cultural shift:** Hard bop reasserted Black cultural identity by incorporating gospel and blues elements; it was a response to cool jazz's "whitening" of bebop
2. **Musical approach:** Hard bop slowed down bebop's frantic tempos and emphasized groove and feeling over pure technical virtuosity

**Q: Name two ways Modal Jazz (1959) differed from Hard Bop of the 1950s**

A:
1. **Harmonic complexity:** Modal jazz simplified harmony to one or two modes; Hard Bop retained complex chord progressions
2. **Improvisation approach:** Modal jazz gave soloists more freedom to explore melodically; Hard Bop required navigating specific chord changes

**Q: How did Free Jazz break from bebop traditions? (Two ways)**

A:
1. **Abandoning tonality:** Free jazz rejected traditional harmonic structures entirely; bebop still operated within functional harmony
2. **Collective improvisation:** Free jazz emphasized group interaction without predetermined forms; bebop had clear head-solos-head structure

**Q: What is the timeline progression from Bebop to Free Jazz?**

A:
- **Bebop (early 1940s):** Complex harmony, fast tempos, virtuosic improvisation (Parker, Gillespie)
- **Hard Bop (mid-1950s):** Added soul, gospel, blues; funkier grooves (Blakey, Silver)
- **Modal Jazz (1959):** Simplified harmony to modes; more space and freedom (Davis, Coltrane)
- **Free Jazz (early 1960s):** Abandoned all traditional structures; complete freedom (Coleman, Taylor)

### Q-TYPE 5: Artist Comparisons

**Q: How does Thelonious Monk's piano style differ from Bud Powell's?**

A:
1. **Touch/technique:** Monk played with flat fingers creating a percussive, angular sound; Powell used conventional technique with flowing lines
2. **Harmonic approach:** Monk used dissonant tone clusters and unexpected note choices; Powell played bebop vocabulary with clear harmonic logic

**Q: Compare Miles Davis's approaches in "Oleo" (Hard Bop) vs. "So What" (Modal Jazz)**

A:
1. **"Oleo":** Fast bebop tempo with rapid chord changes (rhythm changes); requires virtuosic navigation of harmony
2. **"So What":** Slow, spacious modal approach with just two modes; emphasizes melodic development and space over technical display

**Q: How does John Coltrane's playing differ between "Giant Steps" and "My Favorite Things"?**

A:
1. **"Giant Steps":** Complex chord changes moving through three tonal centers; showcases "sheets of sound" and harmonic mastery
2. **"My Favorite Things":** Modal improvisation over vamp; uses soprano sax; more repetitive, trance-like quality

**Q: How do Sonny Rollins and John Coltrane differ as tenor saxophonists?**

A:
1. **Rollins:** More playful, rhythmically inventive, uses calypso and Caribbean influences ("St. Thomas"); focuses on melodic development
2. **Coltrane:** More spiritually intense, harmonically complex; used "sheets of sound" and modal exploration; deeper, darker tone

**Q: How does Ornette Coleman's approach to jazz differ from Charlie Parker's bebop?**

A:
1. **Harmony:** Coleman abandoned chord changes entirely (free improvisation); Parker was a master of navigating complex harmony
2. **Tone:** Coleman used a raw, vocal-like plastic alto sax sound; Parker had a pure, controlled bebop tone

**Q: Compare Horace Silver's piano style to Thelonious Monk's**

A:
1. **Silver:** Funky, gospel-influenced, accessible; clear melodic ideas; founded soul jazz
2. **Monk:** Angular, dissonant, idiosyncratic; used space and unexpected harmony; more challenging

### Q-TYPE 6: Movement Characteristics

**Q: Name two defining characteristics of Hard Bop**

A:
1. **Gospel/blues influence:** Incorporated church music, R&B, and blues into bebop's foundation; funkier, more soulful
2. **Strong rhythm section:** Emphasized groove and swing; drummers like Art Blakey drove the band with powerful, interactive playing

**Q: Name two defining characteristics of Modal Jazz**

A:
1. **Scale-based improvisation:** Used modes (scales) instead of chord progressions as harmonic foundation
2. **Spaciousness:** Slower tempos, less harmonic density, more room for melodic exploration and contemplation

**Q: Name two defining characteristics of Free Jazz**

A:
1. **No predetermined harmony:** Abandoned chord progressions and tonal centers entirely; completely free improvisation
2. **Collective improvisation:** All musicians improvised simultaneously without soloist/accompanist roles; no fixed forms or structures

**Q: What is "cool jazz" and how does it relate to Hard Bop?**

A: Cool jazz was a West Coast style emphasizing lighter tones, subdued emotions, and classical influences (Miles Davis's "Birth of the Cool"). Hard Bop emerged on the East Coast as a direct reaction to cool jazz - reasserting Black cultural identity, incorporating gospel and blues, and emphasizing groove and soul.

**Q: What innovations did the 1959 album "Kind of Blue" bring to jazz?**

A:
1. **Modal jazz:** First major album to use modes instead of chord changes as primary harmonic structure
2. **Spontaneity:** Most tracks were first takes with minimal rehearsal; created a spacious, contemplative sound that became hugely influential

**Q: What is the "sheets of sound" technique associated with John Coltrane?**

A: "Sheets of sound" describes Coltrane's technique of playing rapid cascades of notes that create a dense, waterfall-like effect. He would play arpeggios and scales at extremely fast tempos, especially in his Hard Bop period ("Giant Steps"), creating an almost overwhelming harmonic density.

## Key Terms to Know

**Musical Terms:**
- **Modal improvisation:** Improvising using scales/modes rather than chord changes
- **Dorian mode:** A mode commonly used in modal jazz (minor scale with raised 6th)
- **Vamp:** A repeated chord progression or rhythmic pattern (common in modal jazz)
- **Ostinato:** A repeated musical phrase (often bass line in modal jazz)
- **Polytonal/polyrhythmic:** Using multiple tonal centers or rhythms simultaneously (Free Jazz)
- **Atonality:** Music without a tonal center (Free Jazz)
- **Collective improvisation:** All musicians improvising simultaneously
- **Pedal point:** A sustained bass note while harmony changes above it
- **Time signature changes:** Unusual meters like 5/4 ("Take Five"), 6/8, 3/4

**Stylistic Terms:**
- **Hard bop:** 1950s bebop + gospel/blues/R&B influences
- **Soul jazz:** Funky, gospel-influenced subgenre of hard bop
- **Cool jazz:** West Coast style with lighter tones, classical influences
- **Modal jazz:** Style based on modes rather than chord progressions
- **Free jazz:** Complete abandonment of traditional jazz structures
- **Avant-garde:** Experimental, boundary-pushing approach

**Artists & Nicknames:**
- **"Prez"** - Lester Young
- **"Bird"** - Charlie Parker
- **"Monk"** - Thelonious Monk
- **"Trane"** - John Coltrane
- **"Cannonball"** - Julian Adderley

#### **Art Blakey – "Blues March" (1958)**
*Album: Moanin'*
*Art Blakey and the Jazz Messengers*
*Personnel: Lee Morgan (trumpet), Benny Golson (tenor sax), Bobby Timmons (piano), Jymie Merritt (bass), Art Blakey (drums)*

- **Call and response:** Horns play riff patterns that answer each other
- **Gospel influence:** Church-like feel in the harmonies and rhythm
- **12-bar blues form:** Traditional blues structure
- **Hard-swinging rhythm section:** Demonstrates the hard bop aesthetic
- **Lee Morgan's bright trumpet:** Young Lee Morgan (18 years old!) plays with fire and precision
- **Group sound:** Ensemble unity - the Jazz Messengers' signature tight, powerful approach

**Key Terms:** blues march, hard bop, gospel influence, call and response, powerful drumming, Jazz Messengers, ensemble playing

**Historical Context:** Art Blakey's Jazz Messengers were the quintessential hard bop group. Blakey was a master at nurturing young talent; this group features teenage Lee Morgan who would become a major star.

#### **Miles Davis – "Oleo" (1956)**
*Album: Relaxin' With the Miles Davis Quintet*
*First Great Quintet: Miles (trumpet), John Coltrane (tenor sax), Red Garland (piano), Paul Chambers (bass), Philly Joe Jones (drums)*

- **Philly Joe Jones' drumming:** Interactive, conversational drumming pushing soloists
- **First Great Quintet:** One of the most important groups in jazz history
- **Hard bop aesthetic:** Virtuosic but swinging; maintains groove despite speed

**Key Terms:** rhythm changes, Harmon mute, sheets of sound, block chords, interactive drumming, hard bop

**Historical Context:** Miles' First Great Quintet (1955-1957) defined 1950s hard bop. These "Prestige sessions" were recorded quickly to fulfill contract obligations before Miles moved to Columbia.

#### **Miles Davis – "So What" (1959)**
*Album: Kind of Blue*
*Sextet: Miles (trumpet), Cannonball Adderley (alto sax), John Coltrane (tenor sax), Bill Evans (piano), Paul Chambers (bass), Jimmy Cobb (drums)*

**What to Listen For:**
- **Modal structure:** Only two modes - D Dorian for 16 bars, E♭ Dorian for 8 bars, back to D Dorian for 8 bars
- **Bass ostinato:** Paul Chambers' repeated bass riff defines the tune
- **Piano/bass call and response:** Opening dialogue between Bill Evans and bass
- **Spacious, relaxed:** Slower tempo allows melodic exploration
- **No complex chord changes:** Just two modes for entire piece
- **Miles' Harmon mute:** Intimate, whispering trumpet sound
- **Coltrane's modal improvisation:** Uses modes to build melodic ideas
- **Bill Evans' impressionistic piano:** Voicings influenced by classical impressionism
- **First take spontaneity:** Recorded with minimal rehearsal
- **Cool, understated:** Contrast to hard bop's intensity

**Key Terms:** modal jazz, Dorian mode, ostinato, call and response, Harmon mute, impressionistic harmony, spontaneity

**Historical Context:** *Kind of Blue* (1959) is the best-selling jazz album of all time. This track defined modal jazz and influenced all subsequent jazz. Recorded in one take with minimal rehearsal.

**Key Terms:** post-bop, Second Great Quintet, harmonic ambiguity, polyrhythm, collective improvisation, abstract

**Historical Context:** Miles' Second Great Quintet (1964-1968) pushed modal jazz into more abstract territory. This group featured some of the most innovative musicians of the era, all still developing their own voices.

#### **John Coltrane – "My Favorite Things" (1961)**
*Album: My Favorite Things*
*Soprano saxophone*

**What to Listen For:**
- **Soprano saxophone:** Coltrane's breakthrough on this instrument; high, penetrating sound
- **Modal vamp:** Repeated two-chord pattern (E minor to E major)
- **Waltz time (3/4):** Unusual meter for jazz; from Rodgers & Hammerstein musical
- **Long solos:** Extended modal improvisation (album version is 13+ minutes)
- **Hypnotic, trance-like:** Repetitive vamp creates meditative quality
- **Indian music influence:** Drone-like quality; spiritual searching
- **McCoy Tyner's piano:** Quartal voicings (chords built on 4ths) become signature sound
- **Elvin Jones' polyrhythmic drumming:** Complex cross-rhythms over waltz feel
- **Spiritual quality:** Coltrane's growing interest in spirituality and Eastern philosophy
- **Popular success:** Became Coltrane's most commercially successful recording

**Key Terms:** soprano saxophone, modal vamp, 3/4 waltz time, hypnotic, quartal harmony, polyrhythm, spiritual jazz

**Historical Context:** This recording marked Coltrane's shift from complex harmony ("Giant Steps") to modal exploration. His "Classic Quartet" (Tyner, Garrison, Jones) became one of jazz's greatest groups.

#### **Dave Brubeck – "Take Five" (1959)**
*Album: Time Out*
*Piano; composition by Paul Desmond (alto sax)*

**What to Listen For:**
- **5/4 time signature:** Unusual meter - five beats per measure (3+2 or 2+3 grouping)
- **Paul Desmond's cool alto:** Light, airy tone; lyrical, singable melody
- **Catchy, accessible:** Despite odd meter, became hugely popular
- **Joe Morello's drum solo:** Extended drum solo in 5/4 - technically impressive
- **Vamp-based:** Repeated piano pattern establishes the 5/4 groove
- **West Coast cool jazz:** Lighter, more cerebral than East Coast hard bop
- **Classical influence:** Brubeck studied with Darius Milhaud
- **Commercial success:** Became first jazz instrumental to sell over a million copies
- **Intellectual cool:** Shows jazz's connection to classical experimentation

**Key Terms:** 5/4 time, odd meter, cool jazz, vamp, accessible, classical influence, West Coast jazz

**Historical Context:** Dave Brubeck Quartet experimented with unusual time signatures on *Time Out* album. "Take Five" became an unlikely hit, proving jazz could be both experimental and popular.

#### **Stan Getz – "The Girl from Ipanema" (1964)**
*Album: Getz/Gilberto*
*Tenor saxophone*
*Featuring: João Gilberto (guitar/vocals), Astrud Gilberto (vocals), Antonio Carlos Jobim (piano)*

**What to Listen For:**
- **Bossa nova rhythm:** Brazilian style - samba-influenced but softer, more sophisticated
- **João Gilberto's quiet guitar:** Subtle, syncopated guitar patterns
- **Astrud Gilberto's vocal:** Untrained, whispery voice (she wasn't even a professional singer!)
- **Stan Getz's warm tenor:** Smooth, lyrical tone perfect for bossa nova
- **Portuguese and English lyrics:** Bilingual version
- **Simple melody:** Highly singable, became a pop hit
- **Latin jazz fusion:** Brazilian music meeting American jazz
- **Antonio Carlos Jobim:** Brazilian composer at piano; wrote the tune
- **Soft, romantic mood:** Perfect for relaxation; became symbol of 1960s sophistication
- **Crossover success:** Topped pop charts; introduced bossa nova to American audiences

**Key Terms:** bossa nova, Latin jazz, crossover hit, Brazilian music, gentle swing, bilingual

**Historical Context:** This recording became one of the most successful jazz records ever, winning multiple Grammys. It sparked a bossa nova craze in the U.S. and showed jazz's global influences.

#### **Andrew Hill – "Refuge" (1964)**
*Album: Point of Departure*
*Piano, composer*

**What to Listen For:**
- **Avant-garde composition:** Complex, through-composed structure
- **Eric Dolphy on alto sax:** Distinctive, wide-interval leaps; vocalized tone
- **Asymmetrical phrases:** Unusual phrase lengths - not standard 4 or 8-bar sections
- **Angular melody:** Dissonant, challenging melodic lines
- **Modern classical influence:** Shows influence of 20th century concert music
- **Joe Henderson on tenor:** Contrasts with Dolphy's angular approach
- **Dense texture:** Multiple voices creating complex polyphony
- **Shifting time feels:** Not steady groove; rhythmic complexity
- **Blue Note modernism:** Represents Blue Note's more experimental side
- **Challenging but structured:** Not free jazz - carefully composed, but difficult

**Key Terms:** avant-garde, through-composed, asymmetrical phrasing, angular, polyphonic texture, Blue Note modernism

**Historical Context:** Andrew Hill (1931-2007) was one of Blue Note's most adventurous artists. *Point of Departure* is a masterpiece of "inside-outside" playing - modern but not completely free.

#### **Joe Henderson – "Serenity" (1964)**
*Album: In 'N Out*
*Tenor saxophone*

**What to Listen For:**
- **Ballad tempo:** Slow, lyrical mood
- **Henderson's warm tenor tone:** Beautiful, singing quality
- **Modal harmony:** Simple harmonic structure allows melodic development
- **McCoy Tyner on piano:** Quartal voicings similar to his work with Coltrane
- **Elvin Jones on drums:** Subtle, supportive brushwork
- **Melodic sophistication:** Henderson builds logical, song-like improvisations
- **Blue Note sound:** High-quality recording; pristine acoustics
- **Relaxed but engaged:** Not sleepy - maintains intensity while being lyrical
- **Hard bop meets modal:** Shows how hard bop musicians incorporated modal concepts

**Key Terms:** ballad, modal harmony, quartal voicings, melodic development, lyrical improvisation

**Historical Context:** Joe Henderson (1937-2001) was one of the great hard bop/modal tenor saxophonists. He worked with many Blue Note artists and had one of the most distinctive sounds on his instrument.

#### **Wayne Shorter – "Speak No Evil" (1965)**
*Album: Speak No Evil*
*Tenor saxophone, composer*

**What to Listen For:**
- **Mysterious, film noir mood:** Dark, shadowy quality
- **Complex melody:** Angular but memorable theme
- **Shorter's unique tone:** Lighter, more speech-like than typical hard bop tenor
- **Freddie Hubbard on trumpet:** Bright, confident trumpet contrasts with Shorter's mystery
- **Herbie Hancock's comping:** Sophisticated harmonic support
- **Minor key:** Gives melancholy, noir feeling
- **Post-bop harmony:** More complex than modal but not traditional bebop changes
- **Blue Note masterpiece:** One of the label's most acclaimed albums
- **Shorter's compositional voice:** Shows him as major composer, not just saxophonist
- **Enigmatic quality:** Music suggests but doesn't state; leaves room for interpretation

**Key Terms:** film noir mood, post-bop, angular melody, enigmatic, minor tonality, sophisticated harmony

**Historical Context:** Wayne Shorter was a member of Miles' Second Great Quintet and Art Blakey's Jazz Messengers. This album established him as a major composer and conceptualist, not just a sideman.

#### **John Coltrane – "Acknowledgment" (Part I of *A Love Supreme*) (1965)**
*Album: A Love Supreme*
*Tenor saxophone*
*Classic Quartet: Coltrane, McCoy Tyner (piano), Jimmy Garrison (bass), Elvin Jones (drums)*

**What to Listen For:**
- **Spiritual intent:** This is a prayer/devotional offering to God
- **Modal pedal point:** Bass repeats F pedal throughout
- **"A Love Supreme" chant:** Four-note motif repeated vocally and instrumentally
- **Building intensity:** Starts quiet, builds to ecstatic peak
- **Coltrane's sheets of sound:** Dense cascades of notes expressing spiritual yearning
- **McCoy Tyner's power:** Piano creates massive, orchestral sound with quartal voicings
- **Elvin Jones' polyrhythmic fury:** Drums create swirling, multi-layered rhythms
- **Collective intensity:** All four musicians reach transcendent state together
- **Gospel influence:** Church-like fervor and call-and-response
- **Suite structure:** This is Part I of 4-part work (*Acknowledgment, Resolution, Pursuance, Psalm*)
- **No traditional form:** Open structure allows spiritual expression

**Key Terms:** spiritual jazz, pedal point, suite, polyrhythm, sheets of sound, transcendence, gospel influence

**Historical Context:** *A Love Supreme* (1965) is Coltrane's masterpiece - a four-part suite expressing gratitude to God for helping him overcome addiction. It's one of the most important albums in jazz history and represents the peak of spiritual modal jazz.

#### **Ornette Coleman – "Lonely Woman" (1959)**
*Album: The Shape of Jazz to Come*
*Alto saxophone (plastic!), composer*
*Quartet: Coleman, Don Cherry (pocket trumpet), Charlie Haden (bass), Billy Higgins (drums)*

**What to Listen For:**
- **No chord changes:** Completely free improvisation - no predetermined harmony
- **Haunting, crying melody:** Deeply emotional, mournful theme
- **Plastic alto saxophone:** Coleman played white plastic sax; unusual, vocal-like tone
- **Don Cherry's pocket trumpet:** Small trumpet with softer, more intimate sound
- **Free but structured:** Has a composed head and clear sections
- **Rubato opening:** Bass and drums create loose, floating tempo
- **Collective improvisation:** Soloists play freely while rhythm section responds
- **Emotional directness:** No harmonic complexity hiding the feeling - pure expression
- **Controversial sound:** Many criticized Coleman's "out of tune" playing and harsh tone
- **Revolutionary:** Proved jazz could exist without chord changes

**Key Terms:** free jazz, plastic sax, pocket trumpet, collective improvisation, rubato, atonality, harmolodics

**Historical Context:** This 1959 album shocked the jazz world. Many critics said Coleman couldn't play; others saw him as a revolutionary. This track showed free jazz could be deeply emotional and structured, not just chaotic.

#### **Cecil Taylor – "Tales (8 Whisps)" (1966)**
*Album: Unit Structures*
*Piano, composer*

**What to Listen For:**
- **Percussive piano clusters:** Taylor attacks piano like a drum; dense tone clusters
- **No traditional melody or harmony:** Completely atonal, no tonal center
- **Extreme dissonance:** Sounds harsh, abrasive to ears used to traditional harmony
- **Classical avant-garde influence:** Influenced by Stravinsky, Bartók, Stockhausen
- **Relentless energy:** Non-stop intensity; physically exhausting to perform and listen
- **Group texture:** Not soloist/accompanist - everyone creates dense collective sound
- **Abstract structure:** No verse/chorus or head-solos-head
- **Extended techniques:** Uses forearms, elbows on keys; plucks strings inside piano
- **Completely free:** No predetermined forms, melodies, or harmonies
- **Cerebral and visceral:** Both intellectually complex and physically overwhelming

**Key Terms:** atonality, tone clusters, avant-garde, extended techniques, collective texture, abstract form

**Historical Context:** Cecil Taylor (1929-2018) was the most radical of free jazz pianists, combining European avant-garde classical music with African-American jazz intensity. He remained uncompromising throughout his career.

#### **Eric Dolphy – "Straight Up and Down" (1964)**
*Album: Out to Lunch*
*Bass clarinet*

**What to Listen For:**
- **Bass clarinet:** Low, woody sound - uncommon jazz instrument
- **Wide interval leaps:** Dolphy's signature - jumps octaves suddenly
- **Vocalized tone:** Makes instrument sound like human voice crying, shouting
- **Freddie Hubbard's trumpet:** More traditional sound anchors Dolphy's experimentation
- **Angular, jagged melody:** Unpredictable melodic contours
- **Tony Williams' drumming:** Young drummer (18!) playing with incredible sophistication
- **Post-bop harmony:** Not completely free but not traditional changes either
- **Bobby Hutcherson's vibraphone:** Adds color and texture
- **Out to Lunch:** Title captures the music's off-kilter, "outside" quality
- **Humor and seriousness:** Despite avant-garde nature, has playful moments

**Key Terms:** bass clarinet, wide intervals, vocalized tone, angular melody, post-bop, outside playing

**Historical Context:** Eric Dolphy (1928-1964) died suddenly at age 36, just months after this recording. *Out to Lunch* is his masterpiece - showing how to be experimental while maintaining accessibility. He also played alto sax and flute at virtuoso levels.

#### **Albert Ayler – "Ghosts" (1964)**
*Album: Spiritual Unity*
*Tenor saxophone*
*Trio: Ayler, Gary Peacock (bass), Sunny Murray (drums)*

**What to Listen For:**
- **Huge, raw tenor tone:** Ayler's sound is massive, unrefined, vocal-like
- **Folk-like melody:** Simple, march-like theme - sounds like children's song or hymn
- **Extreme high register:** Ayler screams in altissimo range (very high notes)
- **No bass walking:** Gary Peacock plays free, melodic lines, not traditional bass role
- **Free time feel:** Sunny Murray's drums create texture, not steady time
- **Honking, crying sounds:** Extended techniques - multiphonics, overtones, screams
- **Spiritual ecstasy:** Music suggests religious trance, speaking in tongues
- **Collective free improvisation:** All three musicians play freely together
- **Simple becomes complex:** Takes simple melody and explodes it into chaos
- **Gospel/spiritual roots:** Despite avant-garde sound, rooted in Black church music

**Key Terms:** extended techniques, multiphonics, altissimo register, free time, spiritual ecstasy, folk melody, collective improvisation

**Historical Context:** Albert Ayler (1936-1970) was the most radical tenor saxophonist. His sound was so extreme many couldn't listen to it. John Coltrane was deeply influenced by Ayler. Ayler died mysteriously in 1970 (body found in river).

#### **John Coltrane – "Expression" (1967)**
*Album: Expression*
*Tenor saxophone*

**What to Listen For:**
- **Late Coltrane sound:** Harsh, crying tone - very different from earlier lyrical playing
- **Free improvisation:** No chord changes or predetermined structure
- **Spiritual intensity:** Music expresses extreme emotional and spiritual states
- **Alice Coltrane on piano:** His wife plays dense, percussive clusters
- **Screaming, honking:** Coltrane uses extreme techniques; multiphonics, overblowing
- **Rashied Ali on drums:** Free, textural drumming - not traditional timekeeping
- **Dark, apocalyptic mood:** This is not uplifting - sounds like suffering, searching
- **Collective intensity:** All musicians reach extreme states together
- **Coltrane's final period:** Recorded shortly before his death from liver cancer
- **Beyond beauty:** Not trying to be "pretty" - expressing raw truth
- **Influence of Albert Ayler:** Coltrane's sound became more extreme, influenced by Ayler

**Key Terms:** late Coltrane, free jazz, spiritual intensity, multiphonics, overblowing, collective intensity, apocalyptic

**Historical Context:** This was recorded in early 1967; Coltrane died in July 1967 at age 40. His final albums are extremely challenging - moving into complete freedom and spiritual extremity. Many fans of earlier Coltrane couldn't follow him here.

### Essential Artists to Know:
- **Week 5 (Hard Bop):** Art Blakey, Horace Silver, Sonny Rollins, Charles Mingus, early Coltrane ("Giant Steps")
- **Week 6 (Modal Jazz):** Miles Davis (*Kind of Blue*), modal Coltrane ("My Favorite Things"), Bill Evans, Herbie Hancock, Wayne Shorter
- **Week 7 (Free Jazz):** Ornette Coleman, Cecil Taylor, Eric Dolphy, Albert Ayler, late Coltrane ("Expression")

### Essential Concepts BY WEEK:

**Week 5 (Hard Bop):**
- **Hard bop** - bebop + gospel/blues/R&B; funkier, more soulful
- **Soul jazz** - funky, gospel-influenced substyle (Horace Silver)
- **Sheets of sound** - Coltrane's dense cascades of notes ("Giant Steps")
- **Gospel influence** - Black church music incorporated into jazz
- **Locked-in groove** - strong, steady rhythm section

**Week 6 (Modal Jazz):**
- **Modal jazz** - improvising on modes/scales rather than chord changes
- **Mode** - type of scale (Dorian, Phrygian, Mixolydian)
- **Dorian mode** - most common modal jazz scale
- **Vamp** - repeated chord/rhythm pattern
- **Ostinato** - repeated bass line
- **Quartal harmony** - chords built on 4ths (McCoy Tyner)
- **Pedal point** - sustained bass note

**Week 7 (Free Jazz):**
- **Free jazz** - abandoning all traditional structures (harmony, form, time)
- **Atonality** - no tonal center
- **Collective improvisation** - all musicians improvising simultaneously
- **Extended techniques** - multiphonics, overblowing, screaming
- **Free time** - no steady pulse or meter
- **Harmolodics** - Ornette Coleman's theory
