## Wayne Shorter

### Biography
- **Born**: 1933, Newark, New Jersey
- **Died**: Recently (in his late 80s)
- **Significance**: One of the most important composers and saxophonists in jazz history

### Career Highlights
- Started mainstream career with **Art Blakey's Jazz Messengers** (1960)
  - Initially sounded similar to young John Coltrane
- Joined **Miles Davis's Second Great Quintet** (1964)
  - Primary composer for the group during the 1960s
  - Worked alongside Herbie Hancock, Ron Carter, and Tony Williams
- Co-founded **Weather Report** (1970s)
  - Important fusion group (jazz + rock)

### Musical Style
- Unique harmonic sensibility
- Beautiful melodic writing
- Distinct from Coltrane despite initial similarities
- Favored single-note lines and rhythmic development
- Dramatic use of space and dynamics

### Key Recording: *Speak No Evil* (1964)

**Personnel**:
- Wayne Shorter - tenor saxophone
- Freddie Hubbard - trumpet
- Herbie Hancock - piano
- Ron Carter - bass
- Elvin Jones - drums

**Recorded**: December 24, 1964

**Musical Characteristics**:
- Modal jazz approach
- Emphasis on rhythmic elements over harmonic expansion
- Less "sheets of sound" approach compared to Coltrane
- Herbie Hancock's sophisticated accompaniment

## John Coltrane - *A Love Supreme*

### Recording Details
- **Recorded**: December 9, 1964 (two weeks before *Speak No Evil*)
- **Label**: Impulse Records
- **Significance**: Most famous Coltrane work for non-jazz audiences

### Historical Context
- Recorded at the transitional point in Coltrane's career
- Last major modal jazz recording before moving into free jazz (1965-67)
- Represents spiritual awakening Coltrane experienced in 1957
- Liner notes describe it as "musical praise to the Almighty"

### Cultural Impact
- Synonymous with spiritual devotion in jazz
- Referenced frequently by scholar **Cornel West** (Harvard professor, public intellectual, theologian)
- Four-movement suite structure

### "Acknowledgment" (First Movement)

**Personnel**:
- John Coltrane - tenor saxophone
- McCoy Tyner - piano
- Jimmy Garrison - bass
- Elvin Jones - drums

**Notable Features**:
- Famous "A Love Supreme" vocal chant at the end
- Coltrane overdubbed his voice twice (only two voices, not more)
- Only take with the chant on it became the master
- Upper register "reaching" sound - emblematic of Coltrane's later style

**Alternate Versions**:
- Original sessions included **Archie Shepp** (tenor sax) and **Art Davis** (bass)
- These takes were discovered and released ~25 years later
- *A Love Supreme: Live in Seattle* - features **Pharaoh Sanders** (released recently)

### Coltrane's Stylistic Evolution
- Earlier sound (with Miles): smoother, more bebop-oriented
- *A Love Supreme* era: harder, thicker tone; more vibrato
- Later period (1965-67): free jazz; fractured lines; screaming/shouting sound in upper register

### Comparison: Shorter vs. Coltrane
**Similarities**:
- Both recorded within two weeks (Dec 1964)
- Both using modal jazz framework
- Similar tenor saxophone timbres
- Driving, energetic rhythm sections

**Differences**:
- Shorter: rhythmic emphasis, dramatic space, single-note development
- Coltrane: harmonic expansion, "sheets of sound," spiritual intensity, upper register exploration

## Dave Brubeck

### Biography
- **Born**: 1920, San Francisco
- **Died**: 2012 (age 91)
- **Style**: Cool jazz, West Coast jazz scene

### The **Dave Brubeck Quartet** (founded 1951)

**Key Personnel**:
- Dave Brubeck - piano
- **Paul Desmond** - alto saxophone
- Eugene Wright - bass
- Joe Morello - drums

### Historical Controversy: Time Magazine Cover (1954)

**The Issue**:
- *Time* magazine did first-ever cover story on American jazz
- Featured Dave Brubeck (white musician) on cover
- **Miles Davis**, **Duke Ellington**, **Louis Armstrong** (Black musicians) had never been featured
- Caused controversy in jazz community

**Brubeck's Response** (from Ken Burns's *Jazz* documentary):
- Expressed embarrassment about being chosen
- Acknowledged more deserving artists (Miles, Duke, Louis)
- Recognized the racial dynamics of the decision
- Context: 1954 was early Civil Rights era, just before Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.'s prominence

### *Time Out* (1959) and "Take Five"

**Album Context**:
- Recorded same year as *Kind of Blue*
- Year before *My Favorite Things*

**"Take Five" Details**:
- **Composed by Paul Desmond** (not Brubeck)
- In **5/4 time signature** - unusual for jazz at the time
  - Most jazz was in 4/4 or 3/4
- Modal jazz structure (ostinato over E♭ minor)
- Bridge has chord changes, but main section is modal
- Became one of the most iconic jazz pieces alongside "Sing, Sing, Sing" and "So What"

**Musical Characteristics**:
- Cool jazz sound: laid back, lighter tone, less vibrato
- Paul Desmond's alto: very different from Charlie Parker's bebop style
- Hypnotic bass ostinato (similar intensity to "My Favorite Things")
- Accessible melody - more commercializable than hard bop
- Mixture of percussion and piano with rhythmic interplay
- Energy fluctuates: intense middle section, then returns to laid-back feel

## Stan Getz and Bossa Nova

### Stan Getz Biography
- **Born**: 1927, Philadelphia
- **Died**: 1991, California (age 64)
- Moved to New York in late 1930s
- Sound rooted in **Lester Young**'s cool jazz approach
- Could also play bebop at fast tempos

### Bossa Nova Movement (1960s)

**What is Bossa Nova?**
- Fusion of American jazz + Brazilian samba
- Created by **Antonio Carlos Jobim** and **João Gilberto** (Brazil)
- Became a craze in 1960s America
- Bridge between cool jazz and Latin music

**Impact on Jazz**:
- Many musicians recorded bossa nova albums
- Hard bop musicians like **Cannonball Adderley** recorded bossa nova albums
- Demonstrated jazz's capacity for cross-cultural fusion

### "The Girl from Ipanema" (*Getz/Gilberto*, 1964)

**Personnel**:
- Stan Getz - tenor saxophone
- João Gilberto - guitar/vocals
- **Astrud Gilberto** - vocals (English lyrics)
- Antonio Carlos Jobim - piano

**Cultural Impact**:
- One of the most iconic recordings in jazz history
- Grammy Award winner
- Played at Olympics opening ceremony in Brazil (Giselle Bündchen walked to it)
- Represents Brazil internationally

**Musical Characteristics**:
- Very laid back, nonchalant feel
- Breathy saxophone tone
- Not virtuosic in the Ella Fitzgerald sense - adds to cool ambiance
- "Cool" rather than "hot" jazz
- Gentle, sustained accompaniment (not constant rhythmic drive)
- Astrud Gilberto's vocal approach: simple, understated, breathy

## Andrew Hill - *Point of Departure*

### Andrew Hill Biography
- **Born**: 1931, Chicago
- **Died**: 2007 (age 75)
- Moved to New York City in 1961
- Signed with **Blue Note Records** (1963)

### Musical Style
- Similar to **Thelonious Monk** in uniqueness - didn't sound like anyone else
- Did not achieve the same popularity as contemporaries
- Considered a "classic composer" within the jazz idiom
- Pushing boundaries of modal jazz toward free jazz

### *Point of Departure* (1964) - "Refuge"

**Title Significance**: "Point of Departure" aptly describes transition in jazz happening at this time

**Personnel**:
- Andrew Hill - piano
- **Kenny Dorham** - trumpet (played with Charlie Parker)
- **Joe Henderson** - tenor saxophone
- **Eric Dolphy** - alto saxophone (important free jazz musician)
- Richard Davis - bass
- **Tony Williams** - drums (from Miles Davis's group)

**Recorded**: 1964

**Musical Characteristics**:
- Still modal jazz but pushing boundaries
- Very dense texture
- **Angular and dissonant**
- Close voicings and complex harmonies
- Structure: piano ostinato returns after each wind solo
- Winds return with harmonic melody after rhythm section features
- **Impressionist influence** (similar to Debussy/Ravel)
- Mixture of accessibility and complexity

**Student Reactions**:
- "Made sense and didn't make sense at the same time"
- Dense, angular, dissonant
- Structured despite complexity
- Blend of jazz and impressionism

## Key Themes from This Lecture

1. **Modal Jazz Variations**: Different approaches by Shorter, Coltrane, Brubeck
2. **Cool Jazz**: Laid-back, lighter tone (Brubeck, Getz, Desmond)
3. **Cultural Fusion**: Bossa nova as bridge between jazz and Brazilian music
4. **Racial Dynamics in Jazz History**: Time magazine controversy (1954)
5. **Transition Period**: 1964 as pivotal year - modal jazz beginning to transform into free jazz
6. **Spirituality in Jazz**: Coltrane's *A Love Supreme* as musical devotion
7. **Innovation in Time Signatures**: Brubeck's use of 5/4 time
8. **Compositional Approaches**: Shorter as composer vs. Coltrane as improviser/explorer
